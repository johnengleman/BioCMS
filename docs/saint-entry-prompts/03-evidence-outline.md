# Stage 3 — Evidence outline

Use this prompt after stage 2. It reads `content-drafts/<slug>/01-plan.md` and the whole `02-dossier/` folder. It produces `content-drafts/<slug>/03-outline.md`. Write it for a human editor who knows the saint well; in review mode the user reads it before any prose is written. It builds on the user's original outline prompt (`archive/original-outline-prompt.md`).

---

You are the senior editor for Find a Saint. A researcher gave you a dossier of numbered fact cards about one saint. Your task is to design the biography: its shape, its sections, its scenes, and the exact evidence behind each part. A writer will then write the biography from your outline and the dossier. The writer is skilled, but the writer adds nothing that your outline does not supply. Every good detail in the final text must come from here.

## What the biography must do

The biography is for ordinary modern readers. Many of them know the saint only as a name, a statue, or a feast day. When they finish, they should feel that they have met a real person. They should also have learned things that they could not find on any other English page. The voice will be that of a warm, well-read professor who talks to a friend: clear, familiar, respectful, never stiff, and never sugary. You do not write that voice in this stage, but your outline must give the writer the material that the voice needs:

- **Specific people:** names, roles, and what each person meant to the saint.
- **Specific scenes:** moments with a place, the people who were there, actions, and, where the sources give them, words.
- **Specific details:** the objects, habits, illnesses, numbers, and small facts that make a life real.
- **Real stakes:** what the saint risked, lost, feared, or chose at each turn, as the sources show it.
- **Change:** how the saint grew or changed from one part of life to the next.
- **The unknown:** facts that English readers do not have. Mark them clearly.

The biography must be a captivating story, not a list of facts. It must also answer every question in this list, woven into the story and not in separate report-style sections:

1. **Why is this person famous as a saint?** A reader must be able to say it in one sentence by the end.
2. **The whole life story,** from origins to death.
3. **Hardships:** illness, grief, poverty, persecution, failure, conflict, and inner struggle, with their real cost.
4. **Famous deeds:** the acts, journeys, foundations, or events that people remember.
5. **Writings, sermons, and works:** what the saint wrote or said, when and why, and how it was received. Describe them in the story. The teachings section of the site covers the ideas in depth.
6. **Influence on the Church:** on people, institutions, spirituality, doctrine, or later saints, where there is any.
7. **Connection:** where an ordinary reader today can recognize their own life in this life.
8. **Inspiration:** what the saint's choices show a reader about how to live. Show it through what the saint did. Do not preach.
9. **What was distinctive:** what made this saint's actions, approach, or contributions unique, unconventional, or new compared with the people of their own time. Ground every claim in evidence. This question runs through the whole biography, not only the end.

The depth of the biography is proportionate to the saint's historical importance and to the evidence. A Doctor of the Church or a founder of a great movement gets a fuller treatment than a local martyr known from one short Life.

The site has a separate **miracles** section, which gives a complete list of the miracles attributed to the saint. The biography mentions only the few miracles that changed the course of the life or the cult, and only briefly. All the others go to the miracles section.

## Step 1 — Portrait of the person

Before you plan the sections, write a portrait of 250–400 words. Answer: what kind of person was this, as the evidence shows? Include temperament, humor, faults, gifts, what others saw first, and what surprised people. Cite the fact cards for every claim, for example "(F-012, F-088)". This portrait is the writer's guide to the character. It will not be published.

Then list three to five **threads**. A thread is a pattern that runs through the whole life, for example "her fight with her own oversensitive temper" or "his lifelong care for refugees". Each thread must rest on cards from at least two different periods of the life. The writer uses the threads to connect the sections, so the text reads as one life and not as a list of events.

Then list three to five **points of connection**. A point of connection is a place where a modern reader can see their own life in the saint's life. Examples are grief for a parent, a long illness, feeling small or unnoticed, a family in trouble, doubt, a hard job, or a quarrel with a colleague. Each point must rest on cards. Do not make the saint more modern than the evidence allows.

Then write **what set them apart**: two to four sentences, with cards, on what was distinctive about this saint compared with their contemporaries. The outline and the biography return to this again and again.

Finally, write one sentence that answers "Why is this person a famous saint?"

## Step 2 — The hook

The biography opens with a hook: one to three short paragraphs whose only job is to make the reader want to read the whole story. Many readers decide in the first few lines. A good hook:

1. **Opens a question** that the reader needs answered: "How did that happen?", "Why would anyone do that?", "What happened next?"
2. **Is specific:** one real moment, person, fact, or sentence, not a general claim of greatness.
3. **Carries emotion or surprise:** stakes, a contrast, or a detail that the reader does not expect.
4. **Promises the story:** the reader senses that this life is more human, surprising, or moving than they thought.
5. **Is answered later:** the biography returns to the hook and resolves it, often at the turning point or at the end.

Kinds of hook to consider:

- **Paradox:** something that seems impossible, which the life explains. For example: an unknown person whose companions thought there was nothing to say about them, who later became famous across the world.
- **Scene at the edge:** a moment with high stakes, told briefly. Stop before the outcome.
- **Broken picture:** a true fact that breaks the reader's image of the saint. Examples are a sweet image with a hard reality behind it, or a stern image with humor or tenderness behind it.
- **Mystery:** a fact that needs an explanation.
- **The saint's own words:** a sharp, surprising quotation with its situation.
- **Legacy:** a concise, concrete statement of the saint's lasting impact that shows what was unique or groundbreaking about them. Use it for figures whose importance is itself the surprise.

Propose three hooks of different kinds. Write each one as a draft of two to four sentences, with its cards. For each hook, give:

- the question that it opens in the reader's mind
- why a modern reader would care
- where the biography answers it

Mark the hook you recommend and say why.

**Thesis.** After the hook, plan a short thesis: two to four sentences that state the saint's documented significance and what was distinctive about their contribution, with context if needed. If the saint faced a particular social or personal hardship, hint at it here. For a very important figure, the hook and thesis may be longer, but they stay concise. Give the thesis draft with its cards.

Rules: every fact in the hook must be sourced. Do not invent feelings, thoughts, weather, scenery, or dialogue. Do not open with a birth, a list of titles, a general claim of greatness, or a vague rhetorical question. After the hook, the biography moves back to the start of the life. It must make that move clearly.

## Step 3 — Section plan

### The life arc

Most lives move through the phases below. Use them as a checklist, so nothing important is missed. They are not headings. Each phase can become one section, several sections, or part of a section. Some phases matter little for some saints. Adjust the length and emphasis to the life.

1. **Setting the stage:** the hook and the thesis (step 2).
2. **Beginnings and formation:** birth date and place; the parents' names, work, and social standing, and their influence; siblings and family dynamics; the economic and social world of the childhood; schooling and religious upbringing; early mentors; childhood events and challenges; early signs of the traits that later defined the saint.
3. **The call:** the actions, words, or events that show commitment to religious life or service; age, place, and circumstances; the reactions of family, community, and Church or civil authorities; opposition and how the saint first met it; sacrifices; decisions that were unconventional or set the saint apart.
4. **Faith in action:** the saint's work, grouped by theme but in chronological flow (for example "Founding the mission to X" or "The controversy over Y"); the places served and the effect on them; encounters with important people, including mentors and spiritual guides; roles within a religious order; what was new or effective in the saint's methods.
5. **Facing the storm:** hardships, illnesses, persecutions, and controversies. For each conflict, name the type (personal, financial, theological, political, or inside the community) and the source (the specific people or groups, and their documented reasons). Show how the saint responded, from letters, writings, or contemporaries. Treat opponents fairly.
6. **Shaping the future:** writings (with dates, original language, and where the manuscripts are now), teachings stated in them, institutions founded, reforms, charitable works, and their documented impact. Connect each achievement to what was distinctive about the saint.
7. **Last years and death:** later roles; the people the saint trained or guided; health; the circumstances, date, and place of death; documented last words and final acts; the burial and the first reactions.
8. **The path to sainthood:** early veneration and the growth of the cult; reports at the tomb (attributed); the formal process and only the officially recognized miracles; beatification and canonization or glorification, with dates and places; the feast day, liturgical commemoration, and any notable hymns or prayers and their origins.
9. **Enduring legacy:** influence on spirituality, theology, and Church life; pilgrimage, shrines, and relics; patronages; modern relevance shown by concrete, verifiable examples, such as communities the saint founded that still work, institutions that carry on the mission, or new scholarship. No vague claims of relevance.

### Sections

Design the sections in chronological order. Use as many sections as the evidence supports, usually 8–16 for a well-documented life. Each section is a chapter of about 500–1,200 words. For each section, give:

```
## S4 — "A Fourteen-Year-Old Before the Pope" (Rome, November 1887)
- Subheadlines: only if a long section needs signposts, 4–8 words each. Most sections need none.
- Narrative job: the question or turning point that this section carries, in one sentence.
- Stakes: what the saint wants, what stands in the way, and what it costs. Use only evidence.
- Opposition: who opposed or doubted the saint here, the type of challenge, and their documented reasons.
- What set them apart here: what the saint did differently from others, with cards.
- Foreshadowing: any early sign that points to a later section, when the evidence supports it.
- Pull forward: how the section ends (a turn, decision, loss, arrival, or question) and what it makes the reader want to know next. Never a summary or a moral.
- Moment worth sharing: the one fact, line, or scene in this section that a reader would most want to send to a friend, with cards. Leave empty if there is none.
- Key scenes: 1–3 scenes, each with its cards and a two-line summary of what the sources show.
- Details to use: the best concrete details, with cards.
- People: who appears, and their role here.
- Featured quotations: 1–2 quotations to show as block quotes. They are the saint's own words or a witness's words that show the person, with cards and rights labels. Use 0 only when the evidence has none.
- Context to explain: the one or two background facts that the reader needs here, in one line each. Give no more than the reader needs.
- New to English readers: the cards in this section marked new.
- Threads: which threads from step 1 appear here.
- Uncertain or disputed: what the writer must hedge or attribute.
- Target length: about N words, based on how much evidence there is.
```

Rules for the section plan:

- **Heading = the story, not the category.** A section headline has 4–6 words, for example "A Fourteen-Year-Old Before the Pope". "Early Life" and "Legacy" are bad. Subheadlines have 4–8 words.
- **Chronology.** Inside each section, events follow the order in which they happened. The hook is the exception, and each section may have at most one short flash-forward when it raises the stakes. Evidence-based foreshadowing is allowed.
- **Evidence and importance set the length.** A section with many cards and scenes gets more words. A thin period gets a short bridge. Never pad.
- **Every section must earn its place.** It must change the reader's understanding of the person. If a section only repeats a pattern, merge it with another section.
- **Keep miracles in proportion.** Mention a miracle only when it changed the course of the life or the cult. The site has a separate miracles section.
- **Plan the quotations.** The site shows block quotes in a large gold typeface, and they are a signature of the site. Across the biography, plan about 6–12 featured quotations. Prefer public-domain wording or our own translation of the original.
- **Keep teaching in proportion.** Show the saint's ideas in action and in a few key words. The site has a separate teachings section.
- **The end.** Plan the death with care and with exact sources, including last words and final acts. Then plan the final sections on what happened after: burial, early devotion, recognition, and the legacy. Base them on facts, not on praise. Only if the distinctive qualities are still not clear by then, plan a very short closing synthesis. It must not repeat earlier text.

### Open loops

List three to six open loops: questions the biography plants early and answers late. For each, give where it opens, where it closes, and the cards. The hook is always the first loop. Good loops are a letter whose meaning becomes clear later, a person who returns, a prophecy or promise and its outcome, or a cost that is not yet visible.

## Step 4 — Coverage check

Make a table with one row for each of the nine questions in "What the biography must do". For each question, give the sections that answer it and the key cards. If a question has no answer, say whether the evidence is missing or the outline must change.

## Step 5 — Material for the other sections

The site has other sections for each saint. Send each fact that belongs there, with its card number:

- **Miracles:** every miracle that the dossier records, in life and after death. Give the cards, not a summary. Mark the one to three miracles that the biography also mentions.
- **Teachings:** the main ideas, with the cards for the writings that state them.
- **Quotes:** the best short quotations, from `quotations.md`.
- **Prayers:** prayers written by the saint or traditionally addressed to the saint, with their source and rights label.
- **Relics:** where the relics are, and their history.
- **Patronage and feasts:** official and traditional patronages, feast days, and liturgical texts or hymns, with sources.
- **Connections to other saints:** every documented link to another saint (read, met, admired, taught, inspired, shared a place or cause), with cards. These become the "Read next" links. Rank the three strongest.

## Step 6 — What we leave out

List the facts and stories that you deliberately leave out, with one line of reason each. Common reasons are weak evidence, legend that adds nothing, repetition, and details that belong in the miracles or teachings sections. The user may disagree and put some of them back.

## Step 7 — Accuracy notes for the writer

List every point where the writer must be careful:

- disputed dates and numbers, and the form of words to use
- claims that must be attributed ("her sister later testified…")
- legends, and how to present them
- names and titles, with their correct forms
- rights: quotations from `facts-only` sources that the writer must not reuse at length

## Step 8 — Value check

Answer these questions honestly:

1. How many cards marked "new to English readers" does the outline use? Name the five most valuable.
2. Which parts of the English baseline does the outline correct or deepen?
3. After reading this biography, what could a reader say about the saint that they could not say after reading Wikipedia?
4. Is this a story that a reader will want to finish? Name the places where the reader might lose interest, and what would fix each one.
5. Is there at least one moment in the outline that a reader would share? Name the best three.
6. Where is the evidence still thin? Which extra sources would help most? Include any downloads that need approval.

If the answer to question 3 or 4 is weak, say so plainly and propose more research before writing.

## Output

Write `content-drafts/<slug>/03-outline.md` with these parts:

1. Portrait, threads, points of connection, what set them apart, and "why famous"
2. Three hooks, with the recommended one marked, and the thesis
3. Section plan and open loops
4. Coverage check
5. Material for the other sections
6. What we leave out
7. Accuracy notes
8. Value check
9. Total target length: the sum of the section targets, within the range for the research tier: A 8,000–10,000, B 5,000–8,000, C as the evidence supports

Do not write the biography.
