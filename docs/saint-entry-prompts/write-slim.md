# Write the biography (slim prompt)

The standard writing prompt since 2026-10-06. It is short on purpose: long rule sets made writers obey rules instead of writing well. It keeps what the owner has asked for and trusts the writer with the rest.

## Who reads this

Ordinary people who love the saints or are curious about them: a parent, a student, someone in grief, someone who reads English as a second language. Write the way a good writer of popular history writes for them: clear, warm, natural English that a reader enjoys and never has to read twice.

## The heart of the story

An independent editor read a careful, faithful draft and said what the owner felt: "What is missing is a heartbeat… We watch her from outside, through a record of her actions." The owner: "If you made it a movie, it'd be a very boring movie." A life is not a record of acts. Write it so a reader feels it.

- **One desire, and why it was beautiful to the saint.** Every saint's life turns on one great love or desire. Name it early, at the first decision, in the saint's own words where the sources have them, so the reader feels why it mattered, not only what was done. Then keep the reader wondering how it will end: do not give away the outcome in the summary or the opening, and tell each great moment once, in full, in its place. Let the climax arrive cleanly, with nothing in front of it, in story words rather than document words. The bigger the moment, the slower the telling: give the payoff the opening promised the fullest scene in the life, and build toward it in the chapters before. If the great turn comes early (a conversion, a call), the later life needs its own question: what the saint now loves, and what it costs. Tell those years through that question, not as a chronicle.
- **Play the turning points like scenes in a film.** Choose the four or five moments where the life changes, and slow them down: the place, the sound, the people, what the saint feared or hoped, the words at the turn, as far as the sources allow. Let the reader stand inside the saint, through the saint's own words, prayers, and letters. Compress the rest: a list of virtues or penances becomes one good scene and a sentence. The middle years are where lives turn into a list of places, posts, and books: in each period of the life, tell one moment where the saint's desire meets resistance (a person, an illness, a temptation, an honor, the saint's own fear or limits), and let the reader feel it before the narrator says what God meant by it. Compress everything else in that period to a sentence or two.
- **Show what it cost.** A saint's cost lives in partings, illness, and loneliness, not only in battles won. Put each loss on the page at its date, with one beat of feeling and, for a long trial such as years of illness, one concrete day. Follow the people the saint loved (a teacher, a friend, a sister) to the end, and close each relationship you open on the page: a parting, a death, a reunion, so later words and choices land. Never cancel a cost with a line such as "she did not mind"; if the source says she bore it gladly, show the gladness and the burden together.
- **A warm narrator, and an ending that means something.** The narrator is a person who loves this saint and helps the reader feel the years pass. The narrator may say in plain words what a moment meant, when the sources support it: that is interpretation, not invention. But trust the scene, and cut the sentence that explains it: a scene ends on its last image, not on a line that tells the reader what it meant. End the story when the story ends: after the death, one or two short scenes or a short closing paragraph, then the strongest image, or one quiet sentence after it. The history of the order, the relics, the medals, the councils, and the titles belong in other sections of the site, not in the life. Do not add a paragraph that explains what the reader should learn.

## The voice

- **Natural English first.** Write sentences a good editor would be happy with. Vary their length. Join ideas the way a fluent writer does: a short "who" clause, a short phrase in apposition ("Her mother, Hortulana, had been a pilgrim"), or a second sentence. One short interruption is fine; two or three in one sentence are not. Do not glue two unrelated facts together with ", and" ("Saint Clare was the abbess there, and he loved her like a father"). Give each fact the sentence shape it needs.
- **Easy to follow, not childish.** Plain words, no jargon. Most sentences of 10–22 words. Never stack several interruptions in one sentence: the owner rejected "Later, in a history of his home city's bishops, he described the preacher without flattery," and "The man's habit, the rough robe he wore, was dirty."
- **The real actor is the subject.** A person, or God, does what only a person can do. Not "A muddy street had sent the icon through the gate."
- **Every "he" and "she" is clear.** When two people of the same sex are in the paragraph, use a name or a role.
- **Reverent and warm.** The narrator stands inside the faith of the Church that venerates the saint and loves the saint. God, grace, and prayer are real in this story. No praise words ("holy", "humble" about the saint): show the act. No skeptical or clinical tone. Tell the Church's Lives as the Church remembers them; scholars' doubts go only in "A Note on the Sources".
- **Choose the details. Link the chapters.** The owner likes a short closing line that links a chapter to the next one ("Soon a Pope would ask her to let them go."). Use it where the story truly turns; where it does not, end the chapter on its own image, so the links never sound like a habit. Small, true details are what make these lives better to read than others, but each one needs its time and place: use it where it shows who the person is, lets the reader see the moment, or moves the story. Never insert a detail just because the notes have it. A paragraph that lists five small facts in a row (the food, the pilgrimage, the money, the pebbles…) reads as a list, not a story.
- **Keep the private moments:** visions, voices, temptations, tears that the saint or a companion reported.

## Sources inside the story

The numbered notes carry the sources, so the story does not have to.
- **Do not prove the story inside the story.** The note shows where each story comes from, so never stop to say who else saw or heard it, who swore to it later, or who passed it on ("Two of her sisters were there and heard him." "Saint Clare told this story to Francisca herself."). Name a witness only when that person acts or speaks in the scene, or when two tellers truly disagree. Otherwise just tell what happened. Keep doubts out of the story: no "probably", "or perhaps", or "the Testament that has come down under her name" in the narrative. When a source is unsure, say less (leave out the day the witness only guessed) and put the doubt in the note or in "A Note on the Sources". Never stop the story to talk about a book ("The early Life was written soon after… It describes those visits"); a book never sees, says, or adds anything in the story.
- **Few names.** Name only the people the reader will follow. Call a one-time witness "a neighbor", "one of the sisters", "a man who served in her father's house". At most two new names in a paragraph.
- **Church words:** use the plain word when there is one ("robe", "night prayer"). When the story needs the Church word, let the meaning come with it or just before it ("the Pope's pardon, which the Church calls absolution"). Never tack a dictionary sentence on afterward ("Absolution is the Church's pardon for sin.").

## Fixed rules

- **"Saint" before every saint's name, every time** ("Saint Clare", "Saint Francis"), in childhood scenes too: give the birth name once, as a fact ("Saint Sergius was born Bartholomew"), and call the saint by the saint's name after that, but use "she" and "he" freely when the person is clear, so the names do not crowd the page. "Blessed" for the blessed. Never change a quotation for this.
- **Headings** say plainly what happens, in 3–8 words, like a table of contents: "Saint Clare Leaves Home at Night".
- **Quotations** in plain modern English with the exact meaning; nothing added. Lead into a block quote with the moment itself ("She lay face down and prayed:"), and do not retell the same events just before or after it. Quote whole sentences or one striking phrase; do not splice fragments of a quotation into your own sentence.
- **Honesty** (`citations.md`, sections 1, 1b, 2, and 3): every fact from the notes, the cards, or `facts.md`; nothing from memory; when a detail is missing, write less. A numbered note after every quotation and at the end of every story paragraph, with the exact place. Borrowed words in quotation marks.

## Before you finish

Read your text aloud in your head, as the owner will. Ask: would this be a good film? Do I feel what the saint wanted and what it cost? Fix every passage that reads like a record, and every sentence that sounds stiff, glued together, or like a list of facts. Then run the check script (`lint_biography.py biography.html --summary summary.txt`) and fix every MUST FIX line.
