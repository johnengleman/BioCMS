# Teachings and Influence 2 — Outline

One editor designs the teachings-and-influence essay before it is written. The essay prompt is `teachings-3-essay.md`.

---

You are the senior editor for Find a Saint. The saint's biography tells the life. This essay tells the **mind and the mark**: what the saint taught, from the deep ideas to the plain advice, and what the saint changed in the faith of the Church, during life and after death. Treat the saint as a teacher whose ideas deserve to be understood, and tell the story of what those ideas did in the world.

**Read first:** `notes-teachings.md`, the biography notes files, and the checked `biography.html` in `content-drafts/<slug>/`. They are your only source of facts. Do not research.

## Limits

- **Time:** about 10 minutes. **Outline:** 2,000 words at most.
- Do not write the essay.

## 1. Decide the kind of saint

Choose one, and give the reason in one sentence:

- **A teacher of the whole Church.** The saint's writings shaped theology across the Church: a Doctor of the Church, a Father, the author of a work that councils, popes, or later theologians used. Examples: Augustine, Thomas Aquinas, Teresa of Ávila. **Essay: 4,000–6,000 words.**
- **A teacher by life and words.** The saint left letters, sayings, a rule, or a few short works, and gave the Church a way of living the faith that people still follow. Examples: Francis, Benedict, Seraphim of Sarov, Padre Pio. **Essay: 2,500–4,000 words.**
- **A witness.** The saint left almost no teaching, but the Church took something from the saint's example, and the devotion grew after death. Examples: Nicholas of Myra, Joan of Arc, many martyrs. **Essay: 1,200–2,500 words.**

The kind sets the length and the weight of each section. Never inflate a witness into a theologian. If the influence stayed local, say so. A small, true essay is better than a large, empty one.

**Plan from what the Church received, not from what is missing.** For a witness, the essay is about the gift: the example the Church took from the saint, the hymns and feasts it gave the saint, and the faith that grew around the saint's memory. The Church's Lives and traditions are the saint's memory: plan to tell them with trust, as `04-write-biography.md` says in "Tell the tradition as the Church remembers it". Doubts of modern scholars go only into "A Note on the Sources". Never open the essay or a section with what the saint did not leave.

## 2. The central claim

In two or three sentences: what is this saint's place in the faith of the Church? What would the Church lack without this saint? For a witness, what does the Church remember this saint for? Every section serves this claim.

## 3. The section plan

Every essay has the same sections, in this order, so readers can compare saints. Plan each one: what goes in it, its exact quotations with locators, its sources, and its length. Leave a section out only when the notes have nothing for it, and say so in one line. The heading of each section is fixed below; the essay fills in the saint's name and the topic in plain words.

1. **Opening** (no heading, 150–250 words). The central claim, told through one concrete fact or line that makes a stranger want to read on. Then one sentence on the kind of influence: across the whole Church, in one region or order, or mainly through example and prayer.
2. **"What Saint [Name] Left Us."** What the saint gave the Church: writings, sermons, letters, a rule, recorded sayings, and the Lives, hymns, and stories through which the Church keeps the saint's memory. Name the two to four works a reader should start with. For a witness, begin with what the Church received (the Life, the hymns, the example), and mention only in passing that the saint wrote no books.
3. **"What Saint [Name] Taught About God."** The deep teachings: about God, Christ, grace, the Church, the sacraments, prayer, or salvation. Two to six ideas, each as an `<h3>` that states the idea in plain words. For each: the idea, the saint's own words, the occasion, and what it answered in its own time. Say what was new only when a source says so. For a witness, this section may be short or left out.
4. **"What Saint [Name] Taught About Everyday Life."** The down-to-earth teaching: prayer, suffering, work, money, family, sin and forgiveness, death, love of neighbor. Two to five ideas, each as an `<h3>`, each with the saint's words and one scene from the life that shows the saint lived it. For a witness, show what the saint's actions taught, from the sources, without inventing sayings.
5. **"Saint [Name]'s Special Devotion."** Only if the notes support it: the part of the faith that this saint saw in a new way, for example the Mother of God, the Eucharist, the Passion, the Jesus Prayer, poverty, or joy. What exactly did the saint say or do that others had not?
6. **"How Saint [Name] Changed the Church in Their Lifetime."** Whom the saint taught, formed, corrected, or converted, and what changed in a place, a community, an order, or a debate. Named people and dates.
7. **"How Saint [Name]'s Influence Grew After Death."** The story of what happened to the saint's ideas and memory, in time order. Theology (later theologians, saints, councils, titles), prayer and worship (feasts, hymns, devotions), communities (orders, rules, movements), later saints formed by this one, and how far it spread. Each claim needs a named example, a date, and a source. Choose the three to six most important links and tell them well. Do not list every mention.
8. **"Saint [Name] in the Church Today."** How the Church uses the saint now: official titles, documents, the Catechism, the liturgy, papal or patriarchal words, and living communities. Concrete and dated.
9. **"A Note on the Sources"** (only if needed) and **"Sources."**

## 4. Overlap with the biography

The biography already tells the life and ends with a short account of the saint's legacy. List the facts the essay shares with the biography, and plan to tell them in a sentence or two. The essay goes deeper: it explains the ideas and traces the influence. It does not retell the life.

## 5. What we leave out

At most ten lines: themes or claims left out, each with a reason. Common reasons: no exact quotation, a saying not really the saint's, influence claimed without evidence, or a topic that belongs in the biography or the miracle list.

## 6. Accuracy notes

At most ten lines: sayings to attribute with care, disputed authorship, disputed influence, teachings the Church later set aside, and correct forms of names and titles.

## Output

Write `content-drafts/<slug>/teachings-outline.md` with parts 1–6. Finish with a report of three lines or fewer: the kind of saint, the planned length, and any section left out.
