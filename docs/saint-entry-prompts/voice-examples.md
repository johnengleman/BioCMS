# Voice examples

The writer and the checker read this file first. These examples outrank every description of the voice in the prompts. The site owner will add paragraphs they love and paragraphs they dislike.

## The owner's verdict on the first drafts (2026-09-30)

"It seems like we're just telling a bunch of facts, and we're not really telling a story."

## Example 1 — Christmas night, 1886

The facts are the same in both versions. They come from Thérèse's Manuscript A, as checked.

### Not like this (a list of facts)

> On 25 December 1886 the family had left shoes by the chimney, with presents inside. Thérèse was thirteen. Her father, tired from midnight Mass, saw the shoes in the chimney and said something she was not meant to hear. In her own record his words were, in English, "Well, fortunately this is the last year!" Thérèse was going up the stairs to take off her hat. Céline saw tears in her eyes and told her not to come down. Thérèse had heard her father's remark. She felt the old tears rise. Then something happened that she could only describe as a change in her whole self.

Why it fails: every sentence starts with its subject and states one fact. Nothing builds. The reader is told what happened but never stands in the room. The one line of real drama, the father's words, is buried and labeled "in her own record".

### Like this (a story, with the same facts)

> In the Martin house, Christmas still ended the way it had when Thérèse was small. After midnight Mass the family came home, and the youngest daughter went to the chimney, where her shoes waited with presents inside, and the whole family enjoyed watching her open them. In 1886 that youngest daughter was nearly fourteen.
>
> Her father was tired. As she climbed the stairs to take off her hat, she heard him below, looking at the shoes: "Enfin, heureusement que c'est la dernière année!" Well, luckily this is the last year.
>
> It was a small, weary remark, the kind any father makes. For Thérèse it should have been a disaster. For years any cross word had reduced her to tears, and the tears to more tears, until someone had to comfort her out of them. Céline, following her up, saw her eyes fill and knew exactly what would happen next. "Oh, Thérèse, don't go down," she said. "It would hurt you too much to look in your shoes right away."
>
> Thérèse went down anyway. She went to the chimney and joyfully pulled out every present, "looking as happy as a queen," she wrote years later. Her father laughed with her. Nobody in the room knew what had just happened. She did. The work she had not managed in ten years, she wrote, Jesus did in one instant.

Why it works:

- **It opens in a place and a ritual,** so the reader knows what is at stake before the blow falls.
- **The line of dialogue lands at the turn of the scene,** in her father's own words, with no label in front of it.
- **The stakes come from her own record:** years of tears. Her inner life comes from what she wrote, attributed once and lightly ("she wrote years later"), not invented.
- **Nothing is invented.** Every action and detail is in her account; only the arrangement and the rhythm are the writer's.
- **The sentences vary.** Long ones carry the scene, and short ones land the turn.
- **The paragraph ends on the meaning of the moment,** in her words, not in the narrator's praise.

Every quoted word here follows Manuscript A ("Oh ! Thérèse, ne descends pas, cela te ferait trop de peine de regarder tout de suite dans tes souliers"; "ayant l'air heureuse comme une reine"; "l'ouvrage que je n'avais pu faire en 10 ans, Jésus le fit en un instant"). Confirmed on 2026-09-30 against card F-OW-059 (Ms A 44v–45v, copied from the Carmel transcription).

## The owner's second verdict (2026-09-30)

On the story version: "What's the purpose? … Maybe it needs to be a little bit more respectful. … I'm bored, and I don't feel like reading it all. … It's dry and boring still."

The causes: no central question that drives the whole life; a cold, detached narrator ("The Church makes no miracle claim about it", "Her parents, as it happens, are saints"); the story stops to footnote itself ("The sources disagree about the day… the story is the same whichever you choose"); too many small facts (dates of confirmation, lists of patronages); and the saint's love of God, the center of her life, appears only as a label.

## Example 2 — The opening: a purpose, and reverence

Every fact and quotation here is on a checked card: E-P6-085, E-P6-234 (the kitchen remark and the meat juice; Taylor's and Laveille's public-domain English), MQ-001 (Ms A 10r–10v), and F-OW-235 (Ms C 2v–3r).

> In the summer of 1897, Sister Thérèse of the Child Jesus lay in her cell at the Carmel of Lisieux, resting after one of the painful cautery treatments the doctor had ordered for her lungs. It was the hour of recreation, and from the kitchen next door she could hear the sisters talking. One of them was talking about her. Sister Thérèse would soon die, the voice said, and what would the prioress find to write about her afterward? "This little Sister, amiable as she is, has certainly never done anything worth speaking about."
>
> By the world's measure, it was true. She was twenty-four. She had entered the convent at fifteen and lived inside its walls ever since. She had founded nothing and preached to no one, and her highest charge was helping with the novices. When the prioress wrote the customary letter about her after her death, there would seem to be very little to put in it.
>
> A few weeks later, at the worst of her illness, when all food had become unbearable to her, a lay sister brought her a cup of meat juice. Thérèse gently refused it, and the sister went back to the kitchen hurt. Sister Thérèse, she said, was not only no saint; she was not even a good nun. When the words reached the infirmary, Thérèse was delighted. "To hear on my deathbed that I am not a good religious," she said, "what joy!" The only part she objected to was the charge that she had never known suffering. "And I, who have suffered so much since my earliest childhood!"
>
> Twenty-eight years later, in St. Peter's Basilica, Pope Pius XI declared her a saint. In 1997 the Church named her a Doctor of the Church, one of the small company of saints whose teaching it holds up to the whole world. The little sister who had done nothing worth speaking about became one of the most loved saints of modern times, and the story she wrote in cheap school notebooks has been read in some fifty languages.
>
> How did that happen? The answer is the whole of her life, and it begins with a contradiction. When she was about three, her sister offered the little ones a basket of doll clothes to choose from, and Thérèse took the whole basket. "I choose everything!" Years later she saw her soul in that moment. "My God, I choose everything," she wrote. "I don't want to be a saint by halves." She wanted to be a great saint. Yet the longer she lived, the more clearly she saw how small she was: between the saints and herself, she wrote, lay the same distance as between a mountain whose summit is lost in the sky and an obscure grain of sand trodden underfoot.
>
> Most people would have given up the dream. Thérèse gave up the climbing. God would not inspire desires that could never be fulfilled, she reasoned, and since she could not make herself bigger, she would look for another way to Him: a little way, very straight and very short. In the houses of the rich, she noticed, a lift now did the work of the stairs. "The lift that must raise me up to Heaven," she wrote, "is your arms, O Jesus!"
>
> That discovery, made in obscurity and lived out in a laundry, a refectory, and a sickroom, is why the sister in the kitchen was wrong, and why so many people have loved Thérèse ever since. It began in a watchmaker's family in Normandy, with a father who had once wanted to be a monk.

Why it works:

- **It has a purpose.** One question drives the whole biography: how did the nun "who never did anything worth speaking about" become one of the greatest saints? Her inner drama (she wanted to be a great saint, found she was a grain of sand, and discovered a way to God for small souls) is the answer, and every chapter should move the reader closer to it.
- **It is reverent without gushing.** The narrator stands inside the faith: God, grace, and her love for Jesus are real in the story, as the Church and the saint understood them. There is wonder ("How did that happen?") but no praise words.
- **It trusts the reader.** No "in Mother Agnes's record", no "the sources disagree", no edition notes. Attribution appears only where a reader could be misled, and source doubts go into a short note at the end.
- **It chooses.** A handful of vivid, telling moments, not every date. Confirmation dates and patronage lists belong elsewhere on the page.
- **Her humor and humanity show:** she is delighted to be called a bad nun, and she objects only to being told she never suffered.
