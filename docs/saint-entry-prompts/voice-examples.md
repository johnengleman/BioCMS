# Voice examples

The writer and the checker read this file first. These examples outrank every description of the voice in the prompts. The site owner will add paragraphs they love and paragraphs they dislike.

## The owner's verdict on the first drafts (2026-09-30)

"It seems like we're just telling a bunch of facts, and we're not really telling a story."

## Example 1 — Christmas night, 1886

The facts are the same in both versions. They come from Thérèse's Manuscript A, as checked.

### Not like this (a list of facts)

> On 25 December 1886 the family had left shoes by the chimney, with presents inside. Thérèse was thirteen. Her father, tired from midnight Mass, saw the shoes in the chimney and said something she was not meant to hear. In her own record his words were, in English, "Well, fortunately this is the last year!" Thérèse was going up the stairs to take off her hat. Céline saw tears in her eyes and told her not to come down. Thérèse had heard her father's remark. She felt the old tears rise. Then something happened that she could only describe as a change in her whole self.

Why it fails: every sentence starts with its subject and states one fact. Nothing builds. The reader is told what happened but never stands in the room. The one line of real drama, the father's words, is buried and labeled "in her own record".

### Like this (a story, with the same facts)

> In the Martin house, Christmas still ended the way it had when Thérèse was small. After midnight Mass the family came home, and the youngest daughter went to the chimney, where her shoes waited with presents inside, and the whole family enjoyed watching her open them. In 1886 that youngest daughter was nearly fourteen.
>
> Her father was tired. As she climbed the stairs to take off her hat, she heard him below, looking at the shoes: "Enfin, heureusement que c'est la dernière année!" Well, luckily this is the last year.
>
> It was a small, weary remark, the kind any father makes. For Thérèse it should have been a disaster. For years any cross word had reduced her to tears, and the tears to more tears, until someone had to comfort her out of them. Céline, following her up, saw her eyes fill and knew exactly what would happen next. "Oh, Thérèse, don't go down," she said. "It would hurt you too much to look in your shoes right away."
>
> Thérèse went down anyway. She went to the chimney and joyfully pulled out every present, "looking as happy as a queen," she wrote years later. Her father laughed with her. Nobody in the room knew what had just happened. She did. The work she had not managed in ten years, she wrote, Jesus did in one instant.

Why it works:

- **It opens in a place and a ritual,** so the reader knows what is at stake before the blow falls.
- **The line of dialogue lands at the turn of the scene,** in her father's own words, with no label in front of it.
- **The stakes come from her own record:** years of tears. Her inner life comes from what she wrote, attributed once and lightly ("she wrote years later"), not invented.
- **Nothing is invented.** Every action and detail is in her account; only the arrangement and the rhythm are the writer's.
- **The sentences vary.** Long ones carry the scene, and short ones land the turn.
- **The paragraph ends on the meaning of the moment,** in her words, not in the narrator's praise.

Every quoted word here follows Manuscript A ("Oh ! Thérèse, ne descends pas, cela te ferait trop de peine de regarder tout de suite dans tes souliers"; "ayant l'air heureuse comme une reine"; "l'ouvrage que je n'avais pu faire en 10 ans, Jésus le fit en un instant"). Confirmed on 2026-09-30 against card F-OW-059 (Ms A 44v–45v, copied from the Carmel transcription).

## Example 2 — The opening: a purpose, and reverence

Every fact and quotation here is on a checked card: E-P6-085, E-P6-234 (the kitchen remark and the meat juice; Taylor's and Laveille's public-domain English), MQ-001 (Ms A 10r–10v), and F-OW-235 (Ms C 2v–3r).

> In the summer of 1897, Sister Thérèse of the Child Jesus lay in her cell at the Carmel of Lisieux, resting after one of the painful cautery treatments the doctor had ordered for her lungs. It was the hour of recreation, and from the kitchen next door she could hear the sisters talking. One of them was talking about her. Sister Thérèse would soon die, the voice said, and what would the prioress find to write about her afterward? "This little Sister, amiable as she is, has certainly never done anything worth speaking about."
>
> By the world's measure, it was true. She was twenty-four. She had entered the convent at fifteen and lived inside its walls ever since. She had founded nothing and preached to no one, and her highest charge was helping with the novices. When the prioress wrote the customary letter about her after her death, there would seem to be very little to put in it.
>
> A few weeks later, at the worst of her illness, when all food had become unbearable to her, a lay sister brought her a cup of meat juice. Thérèse gently refused it, and the sister went back to the kitchen hurt. Sister Thérèse, she said, was not only no saint; she was not even a good nun. When the words reached the infirmary, Thérèse was delighted. "To hear on my deathbed that I am not a good religious," she said, "what joy!" The only part she objected to was the charge that she had never known suffering. "And I, who have suffered so much since my earliest childhood!"
>
> Twenty-eight years later, in St. Peter's Basilica, Pope Pius XI declared her a saint. In 1997 the Church named her a Doctor of the Church, one of the small company of saints whose teaching it holds up to the whole world. The little sister who had done nothing worth speaking about became one of the most loved saints of modern times, and the story she wrote in cheap school notebooks has been read in some fifty languages.
>
> How did that happen? The answer is the whole of her life, and it begins with a contradiction. When she was about three, her sister offered the little ones a basket of doll clothes to choose from, and Thérèse took the whole basket. "I choose everything!" Years later she saw her soul in that moment. "My God, I choose everything," she wrote. "I don't want to be a saint by halves." She wanted to be a great saint. Yet the longer she lived, the more clearly she saw how small she was: between the saints and herself, she wrote, lay the same distance as between a mountain whose summit is lost in the sky and an obscure grain of sand trodden underfoot.
>
> Most people would have given up the dream. Thérèse gave up the climbing. God would not inspire desires that could never be fulfilled, she reasoned, and since she could not make herself bigger, she would look for another way to Him: a little way, very straight and very short. In the houses of the rich, she noticed, a lift now did the work of the stairs. "The lift that must raise me up to Heaven," she wrote, "is your arms, O Jesus!"
>
> That discovery, made in obscurity and lived out in a laundry, a refectory, and a sickroom, is why the sister in the kitchen was wrong, and why so many people have loved Thérèse ever since. It began in a watchmaker's family in Normandy, with a father who had once wanted to be a monk.

Why it works:

- **It has a purpose.** One question drives the whole biography: how did the nun "who never did anything worth speaking about" become one of the greatest saints? Her inner drama (she wanted to be a great saint, found she was a grain of sand, and discovered a way to God for small souls) is the answer, and every chapter should move the reader closer to it.
- **It is reverent without gushing.** The narrator stands inside the faith: God, grace, and her love for Jesus are real in the story, as the Church and the saint understood them. There is wonder ("How did that happen?") but no praise words.
- **It trusts the reader.** No "in Mother Agnes's record", no "the sources disagree", no edition notes. Attribution appears only where a reader could be misled, and source doubts go into a short note at the end.
- **It chooses.** A handful of vivid, telling moments, not every date. Confirmation dates and patronage lists belong elsewhere on the page.
- **Her humor and humanity show:** she is delighted to be called a bad nun, and she objects only to being told she never suffered.

**Copy the method, never the words.** Writers took sentences from this example into other saints' lives: "How did that happen?", "How did a … who … become …?", "The answer is the whole of her life", "By the world's measure". Those words belong to this example only. Another life needs its own way to raise its question, and often the opening scene raises it without any question sentence at all.

## The owner's models: two lives on OrthoChristian (2026-10-04)

The owner named two lives as the kind of writing he likes: the life of Eldress Nikodima of Diveyevo, told by her spiritual daughter (https://orthochristian.com/52291.html), and the life of Blessed Matrona of Moscow (https://orthochristian.com/79033.html). Both translations are under copyright, so this file does not copy them. It describes what they do, and Examples 3 and 4 show the same methods with our own checked text.

What these two lives do:

- **They begin plainly.** The first sentence gives the birth, the village, and the family. There is no hook and no question. Within three paragraphs comes one small story from childhood that shows the whole temperament. In Nikodima's life it is a stubborn girl who hides in a nettle patch after a quarrel and will not come out when her mother calls. The reader knows her from that moment.
- **A person tells every story.** The narrator knew the saint, or names the one who did: a spiritual daughter, a relative at the baptism, a student who lived in the same room. The source is given in three or four words inside the sentence, never as a note about books.
- **The witness then speaks at length.** A named person tells the moment in the first person, for a whole paragraph, with their own worries in it: an exam, a father in prison, a mother to feed. The narrator does not interrupt.
- **Each scene is small.** One visitor, one need, the saint's few words, and what happened. There are no crowds and no summaries of "many people".
- **The saint talks like a person.** The lines are short, homely, and direct, about tea, a cart, a walking stick. There are no speeches.
- **One object carries a chapter.** A handful of sweets given at a parting tells a nun what her life's work will be. A lost gold ring carries a whole interrogation. The chapter is named for the object.
- **Faults and hard facts are told calmly.** The saint was a troublemaker as a child. A poor mother planned to give her baby away. The narrator says so in a plain sentence and moves on.
- **God's action is stated as fact,** in one sentence, sometimes with one verse of Scripture, and without argument.
- **The meaning comes after the story,** in one plain sentence, and never before it.
- **The last scene is the grave and the people who come to it.** The saint's own words about what will happen after death come last, and then what did happen.

What not to take from them: the closing paragraph of general praise, the lists of addresses and offices, and the footnotes.

**These lives are models for voice and scene only. They are not models for the structure or the coverage of a Find a Saint biography.** They leave out much that our biography must have: an opening that makes a stranger want to read, the thesis, what set the saint apart from the people of their time, the writings and works, the saint's influence on the Church, how the fame grew, the path to sainthood, and context for a reader who does not know the Church. Those come from the outline and from "The ending" in `04-write-biography.md`. Do not imitate the layout, the numbered short chapters, or the sentence patterns of these lives. Each tradition also has its own tone: a Catholic saint's life is told as the Catholic Church remembers it, and does not take on Russian turns of phrase.

## Example 3 — A plain opening

A biography does not need a hook with a question. When the first true story already shows the person, begin there. Every fact below is in the checked Seraphim draft (scene cards SC-01 and SC-02; Sergius 1841, Elagin 1863, and Motovilov's Conversation).

> Prokhor Moshnin was born in Kursk in 1754, the youngest child of a merchant who was building a church. His father died when the boy was about three, with the church still unfinished, and his mother, Agafia, took over the work and saw it through. When Prokhor was about seven she took him up with her onto the half-built walls. He fell from the height of the building to the ground. Agafia ran down expecting to find him dead, and found him on his feet, without a scratch.
>
> His elder brother Alexei kept a shop, and Prokhor was put to work behind its counter among the straps and cords, the tar, the harness and the bow-yokes, the bast shoes and the iron. His heart was not in it. On feast days he read books about the faith, sometimes to friends who gathered to listen, and he made friends with one of the town's fools for Christ.
>
> More than fifty years later, as an old monk in the forest of Sarov, he explained the life of the soul to a young friend in the language of that shop. "I come from Kursk merchants," he said, "and when I was not in the monastery we traded in goods that give more profit." The merchant's habit of mind stayed with him. He only changed the goods.

Why it works:

- **It starts with the birth and it is not dull,** because the third sentence is already a story, and the family is drawn in one fact: a widow who finishes building a church.
- **The reader meets the man in his own words** by the third paragraph, and the words are homely: shop goods, profit.
- **The question of the life is raised without a question mark.** A merchant's son who "changed the goods" makes the reader ask what he traded for.
- **The meaning comes last,** in two short sentences.

## Example 4 — Let the witness tell it

The facts and both quotations are in the checked Seraphim draft (scene card SC-03; Chichagov 1903, public domain, our translation).

> At first he told no one what he had seen. Later he told a few. After Communion, he said, the Mother of God had come to him in a light no words could describe, with the apostles John and Peter. She turned to John, pointed to the sick novice, and said: "This one is of Our kin." One of the people he told was a woman named Ksenia who looked after a church, and she remembered his way of telling it:
>
> > "Her right hand, my joy," Father Seraphim said to the church-woman Ksenia, "she laid on my head, and in her left hand she held a staff. With this staff, my joy, she touched poor Seraphim. In that place, on the right thigh, a hollow formed, mother. All the water ran out into it, and the Queen of Heaven saved poor Seraphim. The wound was very big, and the pit is still there, mother. Look, give me your hand!" "And Father would take my hand himself and put it into the hollow," Mother Ksenia added, "and it was so big that my whole fist went in."

Why it works:

- **The narrator sets the moment in four sentences and then steps back.** The witness has the whole paragraph.
- **The witness is a named person with a small place in the story,** a woman who looked after a church, and the source is given inside the sentence.
- **The vision is told as fact,** as he and the Church understood it. The narrator neither argues for it nor holds it at a distance.
- **The saint talks like a person:** "my joy", "mother", "poor Seraphim", "give me your hand".
- **One physical detail makes it real:** her whole fist went into the hollow.
- **Nothing follows to explain it.** The next paragraph goes on with the life.

**These two examples are also a method, never a template.** Do not begin every life with a birth and a fall, and do not give every witness a wound to touch. Ask what the first true story of this life is, and who was in the room.

## Example 5 — Easy to read

### Not like this

> Cardinal Hugolino had become Pope Gregory IX. In the summer of 1228 a quarrel with the Romans drove him from Rome, and he went north by way of Rieti and Spoleto and visited the Poor Ladies.
>
> > "For, worn out as he was by that grievous sickness which was the end of all his sufferings, he bade that he should be laid naked on the bare ground, that in that last hour wherein the enemy could yet rage against him, he might wrestle naked with his naked foe."

Why it fails: two names and three place names arrive in two sentences, and the reader is not told who Hugolino was or who the Poor Ladies are. The quotation is in the English of 1908 ("bade", "wherein", "foe"), and it is one sentence of fifty words.

### Like this

> Francis had one powerful friend in Rome: Cardinal Hugolino, who had protected the brothers for years. A few months after Francis died, that friend was elected Pope and took the name Gregory IX. In the summer of 1228 he came north to Assisi and went to pray at the tomb.
>
> > "He was worn out by the illness that ended all his sufferings. He asked to be laid naked on the bare ground. In his last hour the enemy could still attack him, and he wanted to wrestle naked with a naked enemy."

Why it works:

- **Each name comes with who the person is,** and only the names the story needs are there.
- **The quotation says what the old translation says,** in words a reader of today uses, in three sentences. The checker compares it with the Latin.
- **No sentence has more than thirty words,** and the sentences still vary.
- **Nothing is made childish.** The facts and the feeling are the same.

## Example 6 — Straight sentences

The facts are the same in both versions. They come from the checked Francis draft (Thomas of Spalato's account).

### Not like this

> On 15 August 1222, nearly the whole city of Bologna crowded into the square in front of the public palace to hear a man preach. A student named Thomas, from the town of Spalato, stood in the crowd. Later, in a history of his home city's bishops, he described the preacher without flattery. The man's habit, the rough robe he wore, was dirty. He himself looked like someone to despise, Thomas wrote, and his face was plain. After that he did not preach the way preachers preach. He talked like a man speaking to a public assembly, and everything he said ran toward one end: to put out old hatreds and to make peace.

Why it fails: the owner pointed at the third, fourth, and fifth sentences. Two phrases stand in front of "he described". An explanation sits between "habit" and "was dirty". "Thomas wrote" cuts a sentence in half. The reader has to wait, three times in a row, to find out what the sentence is about.

### Like this

> On 15 August 1222, almost the whole city of Bologna crowded into the main square to hear a man preach. A student named Thomas stood in the crowd. Years later Thomas wrote down what he saw, and he did not flatter the preacher. The man's robe was dirty. He looked like someone to despise, and his face was plain. He began with three words: "angels, men, demons." Then he stopped sounding like a preacher. He talked the way a man talks at a town meeting, and everything he said had one aim. He wanted the people of Bologna to end their old hatreds and make peace.

Why it works:

- **Every sentence starts with who acts** or with one short phrase of time.
- **Nothing interrupts.** No sentence has more than one comma, apart from the date and the three quoted words.
- **The source is named once,** at the start ("Thomas wrote down what he saw"), and it covers the lines after it.
- **"Robe" replaces "habit",** so no explanation is needed.
- **It is not chopped.** Short sentences sit beside longer ones joined with "and", and the paragraph still builds to its last line.
- **Nothing is lost.** Every fact of the first version is here, except the town of Spalato, which the reader does not need.

## The owner's sixth verdict (2026-10-05)

On the Nicholas teachings essay: "A lot of what you wrote initially is pretty critical. Basically, you start off by saying he left us nothing, almost, and that a lot of what people say he left us is not true. That was made up."

The essay opened its main section with "Nothing in his own hand survives" and a scholar's view that almost nothing about him is certain, and it spent a paragraph on which speeches his biographers "put in his mouth". That is a skeptical historian's voice, the same fault as the second verdict, in a new form. The fix is "Tell the tradition as the Church remembers it" in `04-write-biography.md`: the Lives, hymns, and stories are the saint's memory in the Church, told with trust and with the teller named once; scholars' doubts go only in a short note at the end.

### Not like this

> Nothing in his own hand survives. The Catholic Encyclopedia of 1911 says that almost nothing about him is certain except that he was bishop of Myra in the fourth century. What the Church has instead is a shelf of writing by other people.

### Like this

> Saint Nicholas left the Church no books. He left it something it has kept for seventeen centuries: the memory of a bishop who gave in secret, stood between the innocent and the sword, and called himself a sinner. The Church keeps that memory in his Life, written in the ninth century by the monk Michael, and in the hymns it sings to him every 6 December and every Thursday.

## The owner's seventh verdict (2026-10-05)

On a sentence in the Saint Seraphim biography: "That before sentence is terrible English writing. 'A muddy street had sent.' That's all backwards… I don't even understand how that was ever written in the first place."

### Not like this

> A muddy street had sent the wonderworking icon of Kursk, the icon of the Sign, through the Moshnins' gate.

Why it fails: the subject is a street, and a street cannot send anything. The writer reached for a clever figure, and the reader no longer knows who did what. The clergy carried the icon; the mud only made them change their road.

### Like this

> The clergy were carrying the wonderworking icon of Kursk along the street. Heavy rain had turned the street to mud, so they carried the icon through the Moshnins' yard instead. Saint Seraphim's mother saw her chance and came out with her sick boy in her arms.

Why it works: every sentence starts with the person who acted, and the rain and the mud appear only as what they were, the reason for the new road.
