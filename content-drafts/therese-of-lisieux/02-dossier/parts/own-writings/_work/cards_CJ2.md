### How the "last words" were recorded, and how reliable they are
- Claim: The Carmel site publishes an essay by the historian Claude Langlois. His points (reported here in our words): no sister knew shorthand; Mère Agnès first copied each evening what she had heard, and from 8 July 1897 noted sayings through the day, "turning her long presence into a continuous interview", which Thérèse knew (see CJ 18.8.3). Four versions exist, all after her death: two made for the process (1909: 306 sayings; 1910: 275), the Novissima Verba (1927: 362), and the "Carnet jaune" (714 sayings). The Carnet jaune was written out in 1921–1923 from a "Gros carnet noir" of 1904–1905, which was compiled from loose sheets; the black notebook was destroyed and only one loose sheet survives. So there is no way to check the Carnet jaune against its sources. Langlois (Les dernières paroles de Thérèse de Lisieux, 2000) holds that Mère Agnès, "by her own admission", reworked some sayings into "quasi-slogans", but that most sayings came through unrewritten; the "strategic" sayings (her authority to publish the writings; Thérèse's mission after death) are the ones to treat with most care. Céline (Sr Geneviève) began her own notes on 21 July 1897 at Léonie's request, and wrote down only sayings addressed to her.
- When / where: 1897; copies 1904–1905, 1909, 1910, 1921–1923, 1927; critical edition 1971
- People: Mère Agnès de Jésus; Sr Geneviève; Claude Langlois; Jean-François Six (critic, 1973)
- Source: C. Langlois, "Les dernières paroles de Thérèse sont-elles de Thérèse ?", https://archives.carmeldelisieux.fr/oeuvres-de-therese/dernieres-paroles-de-therese/les-dernieres-paroles-de-therese-sont-elles-de-therese/
- Source type: scholarship
- Rights: unknown (modern essay; facts only)
- Original words: « Le carnet jaune a été rédigé en 1921-1923. »
- Translation: "The Yellow Notebook was written in 1921–1923."
- Other sources: Mère Agnès's own "Remarque importante" at the end of September in the Carnet jaune claims Thérèse told her on 16 Jul 1897 "you alone know all the folds of my little soul", so that "later, because of the authority that would be given me, people could trust entirely what I would say and write about her" (cj-septembre-1897, appendix; signed Sr Agnès de Jésus).

### Origin of "a shower of roses" and "spend my heaven doing good"
- Claim: According to Langlois (Carmel site), the two process versions of the sayings date the "shower of roses" to 9 Jun 1897: Sr Marie du Sacré-Cœur said, "We will be very sad when you die", and Thérèse answered, "Oh no, you'll see, it will be like a shower of roses." In her deposition Marie explained she had read in the refectory a story from the Life of St Aloysius Gonzaga in which a sick man saw roses fall on his bed as a sign of healing; Thérèse then said at recreation: "Me too, after my death I will make roses rain." The other saying, "I want to spend my heaven doing good on earth", was said separately on 17 Jul 1897. The first Histoire d'une âme (1898, ch. XII) printed "After my death, she had told us gracefully, I will make a shower of roses fall"; the phrase vanished from the 1899–1900 editions and returned in 1901, paired with "I want to spend my heaven doing good on earth", which had been written on the wooden cross of her grave. At the process the witnesses cite "spend my heaven" about twenty times and mostly ignore the roses. Céline drew "Thérèse covering her crucifix with roses" in 1912.
- When / where: 9 Jun and 17 Jul 1897; editions 1898–1901; drawing 1912
- People: Sr Marie du Sacré-Cœur; Mère Agnès; Sr Geneviève; Mgr de Teil (vice-postulator)
- Source: Langlois essay (URL above), section on the "pluie de roses"
- Source type: scholarship (reporting the process texts and her sisters' depositions)
- Rights: unknown (facts only)
- Original words: « Moi aussi – après ma mort, je ferai pleuvoir des roses »
- Translation: "Me too: after my death I'll make it rain roses."
- Other sources: The Carnet jaune entries for 9 Jun and 17 Jul are in the April–July part of this dossier (F-OW cards from out_cj1).
- New to English readers: yes (the origin in the Aloysius reading and the textual history)
