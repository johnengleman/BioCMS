## Part 1 — Ancestors and parents (to 1858)

### F-LF-001 — The Martins came from Athis-de-l'Orne
- Claim: Laveille says her paternal ancestors were farmers named Martin at Athis-de-l'Orne (Domfront district) from the 16th century; the first sure record is the baptism of a Jean (John) Martin at Athis on 2 Apr 1692; her grandfather Pierre-François Martin was baptized there on 16 Apr 1777. The family lived near the church, then at a farm called "la Quentinière".
- When / where: 1692–1777; Athis-de-l'Orne (Orne)
- People: Jean Martin (b. 1692); Pierre-François Martin (grandfather)
- Source: LAV p.21–22; his footnote cites Abbé Madeline, *Athis-de-l'Orne et la Bienheureuse Thérèse* (Flers, Imprimerie Catholique)
- Source type: early-life
- Rights: public-domain
- Other sources: LAV p.461–462 (marriage record 1818 names Pierre-François as son of "the late John (Jean) Martin and Marie-Anne Bohard of Athis").
- New to English readers: yes
- Confidence: medium — Laveille relies on a local booklet not seen.
- Notes: Madeline's booklet not found (gap).

### F-LF-002 — Grandfather Pierre-François Martin, career soldier
- Claim: Pierre-François Martin enlisted in the 65th Infantry on 26 Aug 1799; corporal 1800, sergeant 1804, sub-lieutenant and lieutenant 1813, captain 1815 (confirmed 1816); served in the Army of the Rhine, at Belle-Île, Brest, in Prussia and Poland (1807), in the 1814 campaign in France, in the Morbihan (1815) and in Spain (1823–24); moved to the Legion of the Loire-Inférieure (1816), the 19th Light Infantry (1821), the Strasbourg staff (1828); retired 12 Dec 1830. Knight of the Order of St Louis, 20 Aug 1824 (conferred by Charles X).
- When / where: 1799–1830; France, Prussia, Poland, Spain
- People: Pierre-François Martin (1777–1865)
- Source: LAV p.465 (Appendix VI, "Military service of Captain Martin"); LAV p.23 fn. 2; LAV p.24 fn.
- Source type: official (service record as printed by Laveille)
- Rights: public-domain
- Other sources: LAV p.23 says he got the Cross of St Louis "at the age of forty-seven during the Spanish campaign".
- New to English readers: yes
- Confidence: high for dates as printed; one campaign date is printed "1779–1801", surely an error for 1799–1801.
- Notes: Carmel page "Le couple Martin" gives his postings as Bordeaux, Avignon (1826), Strasbourg (1828), retirement Dec 1830.

### F-LF-003 — Paternal grandparents married at Lyon, 1818
- Claim: Captain Pierre-François Martin, of the 42nd line regiment, married Marie-Anne-Fanie Boureau, daughter of Nicolas Boureau and Marie Nay, of rue Vaubecourt, Lyon, in the church of Saint-Martin d'Ainay, Lyon, on 7 Apr 1818 (civil marriage 4 Apr 1818). A colonel, the Count de Labesse, was a witness.
- When / where: 7 Apr 1818; Saint-Martin d'Ainay, Lyon
- People: Pierre-François Martin; Marie-Anne-Fanie Boureau (grandmother)
- Source: LAV p.461–462 (Appendix II, parish register copy certified 23 Apr 1926)
- Source type: official
- Rights: public-domain
- New to English readers: yes
- Confidence: high
- Notes: The register spells the bride "Marie-Anne-Françoise"; Laveille corrects to "Fanie". Her father Jean-Nicolas Boureau (great-grandfather) was a captain; a son of his, Jean-Prosper, aged 12½, died a prisoner of the Prussians in Silesia on 21 Sep 1813 (LAV p.466).

### F-LF-004 — Louis Martin born at Bordeaux, 22 (or 23) Aug 1823
- Claim: Louis-Joseph-Aloys-Stanislas Martin was born at 3 rue Servandoni, Bordeaux, the third of five children, while his father was on the Spanish expedition. He was baptized privately at birth and solemnly on 28 Oct 1823 at Sainte-Eulalie by Abbé Martegoute, prison chaplain; godparents Léonce de Lamothe and Ernestine Beyssac.
- When / where: Aug 1823; Bordeaux
- People: Louis Martin; Abbé Jean-Antoine Martegoute; Abp d'Aviau du Bois de Sanzay
- Source: LAV p.22–23 (text and footnote copy of the register)
- Source type: early-life / official
- Rights: public-domain
- Other sources: Carmel page "personnage/louis-martin" and "le-couple-martin": born 22 Aug 1823; ondoyé at birth; baptized at Sainte-Eulalie in October.
- New to English readers: partial (birth date and place are known; the circumstances are not in the baseline)
- Confidence: high for 22 Aug; see Notes.
- Notes: CONFLICT — Laveille's text says "August 22", but his printed copy of the register says "born the twenty-third of the month of August last" (checked on the page image, LAV p.23). All other sources give 22 Aug. See F-LF-005 on the archbishop.

### F-LF-005 — The archbishop's reported words over the baby
- Claim: Laveille says the Archbishop of Bordeaux, Mgr d'Aviau du Bois de Sanzay, attested the baptism certificate in writing on 14 Jan 1824 and "probably on this occasion" told the parents: "Rejoice, for this child is one of the predestined!"
- When / where: 14 Jan 1824 (date of the attestation); Bordeaux
- People: Mgr Charles-François d'Aviau du Bois de Sanzay
- Source: LAV p.22–23
- Source type: early-life (family tradition as told in 1925)
- Rights: public-domain
- New to English readers: yes
- Confidence: low — Laveille himself says "probably"; no source is given.
- Notes: Mark as family tradition. FND p.13 says the same archbishop had cured a Poitiers Carmelite (later foundress of Lisieux) by his blessing and "was to baptise the father of the Little Flower" — that is inexact (he only attested the baptism).

### F-LF-006 — Louis's brothers and sisters all died young
- Claim: Louis's only brother Pierre, meant for the navy, was lost in a shipwreck while young; his elder sister Marie married and died in 1846 aged 26; his younger sister Fanny died in 1853 aged 27 after marrying; his youngest sister Sophie, his godchild and favourite, died aged nine.
- When / where: before 1853
- People: Pierre, Marie, Fanny (Anne-Fannie, b. Avignon 1826), Sophie Martin
- Source: LAV p.22 fn. 2
- Source type: early-life
- Rights: public-domain
- Other sources: Carmel "Le couple Martin": Pierre was 4 and Marie 3 at Louis's birth; Anne-Fannie born at Avignon 1826.
- New to English readers: yes
- Confidence: medium
- Notes: By 1853 Louis was the only surviving child of five. No source here gives the date of Pierre's shipwreck.

### F-LF-007 — Louis learns watchmaking at Rennes, Strasbourg and Paris
- Claim: Louis began watchmaking in 1842 at Rennes with his father's cousin Louis Bohard; in Sept 1843 he went on to Strasbourg, where he lived and worked with the Mathey family, watchmakers and friends of his father, and studied the mechanism of the cathedral clock; he finished his training in Paris, lodging with his maternal grandmother. He learned German at Strasbourg, drew well, and copied favourite passages of literature into notebooks.
- When / where: 1842–c.1850; Rennes, Strasbourg, Paris
- People: Louis Bohard; the Mathey family (Aimé Mathey); cousin Henri-Charles de Lacauve
- Source: Carmel page "Le couple Martin" (https://archives.carmeldelisieux.fr/le-couple-martin/); LAV p.24–25, p.28
- Source type: scholarship (Carmel essay) + early-life
- Rights: unknown (Carmel) / public-domain (LAV)
- Other sources: Louis's own letter to F.-J. Nogrix, 1883, recalls a prank trip "some twenty-five years ago" from Paris to Strasbourg to surprise "l'ami Aimé Mathey", pretending to look at a watch in his shop window, and giving his baby daughter a silver rattle; he regrets that Mathey is not a practising Catholic.
- New to English readers: yes
- Confidence: high
- Notes: LAV p.24 prints a letter of Louis's mother, 25 Aug 1842, to Louis at Rennes ("you are the dream of my nights"; "Remain always humble, dear son").

### F-LF-008 — Louis tries to enter the Great St Bernard hospice
- Claim: Louis, about twenty, went up to the Augustinian canons' hospice of the Great St Bernard, travelling partly on foot and partly by coach. The prior asked if he had finished his Latin; he had not, and was told to go home, finish his humanities and come back. He studied Latin (and, the Carmel says, Greek) at Alençon under the curé of Saint-Léonard for over a year, then fell ill and gave it up.
- When / where: Laveille: Sept 1843; Carmel: a first visit in Sept 1843 and a second, with intent to enter, about two years later; Great St Bernard pass (Switzerland)
- People: Louis Martin; the prior of the hospice (unnamed)
- Source: LAV p.26–28; Carmel "Le couple Martin"
- Source type: early-life / scholarship
- Rights: public-domain / unknown
- Original words: « Have you finished your Latin studies, my son? » (LAV p.27, Laveille's English)
- New to English readers: partial — the baseline lists it only as a target ("not in the baseline").
- Confidence: medium — the dialogue is Laveille's reconstruction; the fact of refusal for lack of Latin is in both sources.
- Notes: CONFLICT on dating: Laveille (Sept 1843, one visit) vs Carmel (Sept 1843 visit on the way to Strasbourg, then a second visit two years later). LAV p.27 cites "Histoire d'une Ame: Introduction".

### F-LF-009 — Louis sets up as master watchmaker at Alençon, 1850

- When / where: 1850; Alençon, rue du Pont-Neuf
- People: Louis Martin; his parents
- Source: Carmel "Le couple Martin"; Carmel "personnage/louis-martin" ("En 1850, il achète un fond de commerce d'horlogerie à Alençon")
- Source type: scholarship
- Rights: unknown
- Other sources: LAV p.28, p.30–31 (rue du Pont-Neuf, parish Saint-Pierre de Montsort; lived there till his 35th year).
- New to English readers: partial
- Confidence: high
- Notes: See F-LF-021 for the Sunday closing story.

### F-LF-010 — The Pavillon, Louis's retreat
- Claim: In 1857 Louis bought "le Pavillon", a small hexagonal tower with a little garden in the Sénatorerie quarter of Alençon, where he read, prayed and kept his fishing tackle; he put a statue of the Virgin in its garden. Later he took Céline and Thérèse there; the garden had strawberries, currants, geraniums and roses.
- When / where: 1857 onward; Alençon
- People: Louis; later Céline and Thérèse
- Source: Carmel "Le couple Martin"; LAV p.78
- Source type: scholarship / early-life
- Rights: unknown / public-domain
- Other sources: CF 169 (22 Oct 1876) and CF 214 (15 Jul 1877): outings to the Pavillon to pick strawberries and redcurrants.
- New to English readers: yes
- Confidence: high
- Notes: This statue is probably the one later called the "Virgin of the Smile" (LAV p.68 fn.: given to Louis "before his marriage by a pious lady in Alençon, who was called 'the saint'"). The two sources do not say explicitly that the Pavillon statue and the house statue are the same object; treat as unconfirmed.

### F-LF-011 — Maternal grandfather Isidore Guérin, soldier and gendarme
- Claim: Isidore Guérin, Thérèse's maternal grandfather, was born at Saint-Martin-l'Aiguillon (Orne) on 6 Jul 1789; joined the army 6 Jun 1809; fought at Wagram, then in Spain (Oudinot division) from Vittoria to Toulouse; joined the foot gendarmerie 1 Oct 1816, the mounted gendarmerie 1 Jun 1823; served at Saint-Denis-sur-Sarthon from 23 Feb 1827; left the service 10 Sep 1844 and retired to Alençon, where he died 3 Sep 1868. As a child his family had hidden his uncle, a priest, from Revolutionary pursuers.
- When / where: 1789–1868; Orne
- People: Isidore Guérin (grandfather); his wife Louise-Jeanne Macé
- Source: LAV p.28 and fn. 1 (from "the Archives of the Guérin family and the Archives of the Ministry of War")
- Source type: official / early-life
- Rights: public-domain
- Other sources: LAV p.462–463 (Appendix III): marriage of Isidore Guérin, gendarme, and Louise-Jeanne Macé (spelled Massé), at Pré-en-Pail (Mayenne), 16 Sep 1828.
- New to English readers: yes
- Confidence: high
- Notes: Carmel "Le couple Martin": to add to his gendarme's pension he worked as a carpenter (menuisier) and his wife briefly ran a café in rue Saint-Blaise.

She signed official papers "Azélie" but all her letters "Zélie".

### F-LF-013 — Zélie's unhappy childhood
- Claim: Zélie wrote to her brother that her mother, strict with the girls and indulgent to the boy, made her childhood sad; she suffered from severe headaches and breathing troubles in childhood; she later wrote she had been ill from age seven to eleven or twelve, then had a serious illness after which she was cured of everything.
- When / where: 1830s–1840s
- People: Zélie; her mother Louise-Jeanne Macé; brother Isidore
- Source: CF 15 (7 Nov 1865, to Isidore); CF 213 (15 Jul 1877, to Mme Guérin); Carmel "Le couple Martin"
- Source type: own-writing (Zélie)
- Rights: public-domain (letter text); transcription unknown — our translation
- Original words: « Mon enfance, ma jeunesse ont été tristes comme un linceul, car, si ma mère te gâtait, pour moi, tu le sais, elle était trop sévère » (CF 15)
- Translation: "My childhood and my youth were as sad as a shroud, for if my mother spoiled you, with me, as you know, she was too strict."
- New to English readers: yes
- Confidence: high
- Notes: CF 213: « J'ai été ainsi depuis l'âge de sept ans jusqu'à onze ou douze ans. Après, j'ai fait une grande maladie et j'ai été guérie de tout. »

- People: Zélie; the Superior of the Hôtel-Dieu (unnamed)

### F-LF-016 — "Have Alençon lace made": 8 Dec 1851
- Claim: On 8 Dec 1851 (feast of the Immaculate Conception), after a novena, Zélie heard an interior voice she attributed to the Virgin: « Fais faire du point d'Alençon ». The Carmel stresses the words "have it made", not "make it": she was to run a business, not only to work with her hands.
- When / where: 8 Dec 1851; Alençon
- People: Zélie
- Source: LAV p.29–30; Carmel "Le couple Martin"
- Source type: early-life (family tradition from Zélie's own account)
- Rights: public-domain / unknown
- Original words: « Fais faire du point d'Alençon »
- Translation: "Have Alençon lace made."
- Other sources: Zélie's letter to Isidore, 1 Jan 1863 (CF 1), alludes to "favours known only to myself" from the Virgin (LAV p.37 fn.).
- New to English readers: yes
- Confidence: medium — the words come from later family tradition, not a dated note.
- Notes: See M-LF-020 (list of extraordinary events in the family story).

### F-LF-017 — Point d'Alençon: what the lace was
- Claim: Point d'Alençon is needle lace made of very fine hand-spun linen thread, in small pieces (20–30 cm) joined by invisible stitches; the industry was started at Alençon about 1664 under Colbert with 30 workers from Venice. Zélie specialised as an "assembleuse" (the worker who joins the pieces). Her workers worked at home; she took orders, supplied designs, checked the work and did the final joining; 500 francs a metre was a normal price for her lace.
- When / where: 1850s–1877; Alençon
- People: Zélie and her outworkers
- Source: LAV p.29–30 and fn.
- Source type: early-life
- Rights: public-domain
- Other sources: Carmel "Le couple Martin": she kept her "bureau" open on Thursdays to deliver, receive and pay work; she usually repaired the tulle and did the joining herself. FR Wikipedia (via helper notes): up to about twenty workers.
- New to English readers: yes
- Confidence: high (process); medium (price and workforce)
- Notes: The number of workers is not given by Laveille or the Carmel; "about twenty" is Wikipedia only.

### F-LF-018 — The lace business: dates, the Pigache house, a medal
- Claim: Zélie opened her own lace "bureau" in 1853 in her parents' house, 42 rue Saint-Blaise. Her sister and father made the first contacts with Paris shops; Marie-Dosithée later remembered they began "small and in tears". From 1856 Zélie worked for the Paris house of Pigache; at the Alençon exhibition of 20 Jun 1858 Pigache's lace won a silver medal, and the jury credited "the intelligent direction of Mademoiselle Zélie Guérin". She went back to her own account in 1863, when Louis took over dealings with clients.
- When / where: 1853–1863; Alençon
- People: Zélie; Marie-Louise Guérin; M. Pigache
- Source: Carmel "Le couple Martin" (quotes Marie-Dosithée's letter of 10 May 1874 and the 1858 jury report)
- Source type: scholarship (Carmel essay quoting documents)
- Rights: unknown
- Original words: « notre commencement pour le point d'Alençon s'est fait petitement et dans les larmes » (Marie-Dosithée, 10 May 1874)
- Translation: "our start in Alençon lace was made in a small way and in tears."
- Other sources: Carmel "personnage/zelie-martin" (own account from 1863). LAV p.29: her father bought 42 rue Saint-Blaise in 1843.
- New to English readers: yes
- Confidence: high
- Notes: CONFLICT on "1863": the helper read the Carmel short page as a possible typo for 1853; the fuller Carmel essay explains both dates (1853 own bureau; 1856–1863 for Pigache; 1863 own account again). No real conflict.

In a letter of 1 Jan 1863 Zélie warned him, saying her husband knew Paris and "has told me of some of his own experiences"; she asked, as a New Year's gift, that he visit Notre-Dame-des-Victoires daily to say a Hail Mary.
- When / where: 1 Jan 1863; Alençon → Paris
- People: Isidore Guérin; Zélie; Louis
- Source: CF 1 (1 Jan 1863); LAV p.36–37 (English)
- Source type: own-writing (Zélie)
- Rights: public-domain (letter); transcription unknown
- Original words (LAV English): "If you but knew through what trials he has passed!"
- New to English readers: yes
- Confidence: high
- Notes: Laveille: Isidore became a pharmacist at Lisieux and "one of the most practical and devoted Catholics of his time" (LAV p.37).

- When / where: spring 1858; Pont Saint-Léonard, Alençon
- People: Zélie Guérin; Louis Martin
- Source: LAV p.31; Carmel "Le couple Martin"

## Part 2 — Marriage and the Alençon household (1858–1872)

### F-LF-022 — The wedding at midnight, 13 Jul 1858
- Claim: Louis Martin, watchmaker, and Zélie Guérin, "manufacturer of Point d'Alençon", married at Notre-Dame d'Alençon on Tuesday 13 Jul 1858; the civil marriage was on the evening of 12 Jul; the church marriage was at midnight "without any parade", with only the Alençon family and witnesses. The dean of Saint-Léonard, Abbé F. Hurel, received the consent.
- When / where: 12–13 Jul 1858; Notre-Dame, Alençon
- People: Louis; Zélie; Abbé F. Hurel; Abbé Jamot (curé of Notre-Dame)
- Source: LAV p.464–465 (Appendix V, register copy certified 17 Jul 1922, and footnote)
- Source type: official
- Rights: public-domain
- Other sources: Carmel "Le couple Martin" ("à minuit").
- New to English readers: yes (the midnight hour)
- Confidence: high
- Notes: Signatures include "F. Martin" (Louis's mother Fanie) and "Louise Guérin" (Zélie's mother).

### F-LF-023 — The first months of continence
- Claim: According to family records, Louis proposed on the wedding night that they live as brother and sister; Zélie agreed; after about a year (Laveille says after he learned this went against what she felt was God's plan for her; the Carmel says after "a better understanding of Christian marriage") they lived as an ordinary married couple.
- When / where: 1858–1859; Alençon
- People: Louis; Zélie
- Source: LAV p.31; Carmel "Le couple Martin"
- Source type: early-life / scholarship
- Rights: public-domain / unknown
- New to English readers: yes

### F-LF-024 — Their daily religious practice
- Claim: Louis and Zélie went to the 5.30 a.m. Mass together each morning (the "workers' Mass"), received communion more often than Sunday although frequent communion was not usual, kept the Church's fasts, prayed together, read Lives of the saints, and Louis joined the monthly night adoration of the Blessed Sacrament.
- When / where: from 1858; Alençon
- People: Louis; Zélie
- Source: LAV p.32–33; Carmel "Le couple Martin"
- Source type: early-life

He sent most of his fish (eels, trout from the Sarthe) to the Poor Clares of Alençon.
- When / where: 1850s–1870s; Alençon

## Part 4 — Zélie's cancer and death (1865–1877)

### F-LF-090 — The lump in the breast
- Claim: As a young woman Zélie struck her breast against the corner of a table; a lump remained. ### F-LF-170 — Canon Delatroëtte's refusals
- Claim: The Prioress, Mère Marie de Gonzague, was now willing but had to consult the community's ecclesiastical superior, Canon Delatroëtte, parish priest of Saint-Jacques, "of lively faith… but with rigid, definitely moulded convictions". - Claim: Bishop Hugonin of Bayeux was "gentle and benevolent", studious and old, and left much administration to his vicars general. She said, "from the age of three I have desired to give myself to the good God." Louis said they were going to Rome and she would speak to the Pope. She "did more than show my diamonds… I gave them", shedding tears. The Bishop caressed her, a favour "no other child had ever received from him", and said, "All is not lost, little one… You shall certainly receive my reply in Italy." She had achieved nothing.

### F-LF-173 — Paris to Milan: nobles, cards, and "pharisees"
- Claim: On 4 Nov 1887 Louis left Lisieux with Céline and Thérèse for Paris (Carmel: 3 a.m.). The carriages carried religious emblems and pilgrims displayed noble titles. Thérèse wrote that these titles seemed "smoke and vanity", citing the *Imitation*. She also wrote that in a month among priests she saw they were "still subject to human weakness", so prayer for priests came first (HA ch. vi p.95). Each compartment had a saint as patron; Mgr Legoux, seeing the Lisieux three, named theirs St Martin. In the plains of Champagne cards took more hours than prayer, even in Louis's compartment. Asked to play, Louis declined; a vexed passenger cried, "Fortunately, pharisees are rare." Louis said nothing and soon shook the man's hand. In Paris on 6–7 Nov the pilgrims were consecrated to the Sacred Heart at Montmartre. Thérèse knelt before Our Lady of Victories (see F-LF-143). The party went by Basel (a few hours at the station) to Lucerne, where the scenery drew a cry of admiration. Thérèse wrote she would recall this "little corner of the heavens" in Carmel (HA p.97). In Milan they heard Mass at St Charles Borromeo's tomb; the sisters climbed the dome; Thérèse called the Campo Santo "masterpieces" and rebuked an old grumbler: "he had better have stayed home".

### F-LF-179 — Audience with Leo XIII, Sunday 20 November 1887
- Claim: Early on 20 Nov, agency carriages fetched groups in a steady downpour. At 7.30 a.m. pilgrims assembled under Mgr Germain and Abbé Révérony, with the bishops of Nantes, Séez and Vannes, in a hall hung with red damask. At 8 a.m. Leo XIII entered in a red mantle over a white woollen cassock, deathly pale, with trembling hands, and said Mass slowly with an Italian accent. In the audience chamber he sat on an ordinary armchair on a low step, took each pilgrim's hand, said a few words and gave a medal. Laveille footnotes that Mgr Germain presented him as author of a manual of Thomistic philosophy and the Pope praised him. Bayeux pilgrims came next, presented by Révérony; over a hundred people were present. As the two sisters approached "with calm and resolute faces beneath their black mantillas", Révérony "announced in a loud voice that he absolutely forbade anyone to speak to the Holy Father". Thérèse looked at Céline, who said, "Speak!" Thérèse kissed the Pope's foot, took his hand, in tears: "Most Holy Father, I have a great favour to ask… in honour of your Jubilee, permit me to enter Carmel at fifteen." The Pope bent his head to her mantilla. Révérony said, "this is a child who desires the life of Carmel, but the superiors are, at the moment, considering the question." Leo XIII: "Well, my child, do whatever the superiors shall decide." Thérèse, hands on his knees: "O Holy Father, if you only said yes everyone else would agree." He looked at her fixedly and said gravely: "Well, my child, you shall enter if it be God's will." She tried to ask again; two noble guards told her to rise and, when she stayed, took her by the arms, and Révérony had to help before she left the Pope's knees. Louis had received his blessing earlier and met her at the door "bathed in tears, which she made no effort to dry".

### F-LF-182 — Home by Assisi and Florence
- Claim: The party left Rome on 24 Nov at 6 a.m. At an early stop Mgr Legoux came to their carriage and asked, "Well, how is our little Carmelite?" She saw that all the pilgrims knew her secret. Laveille says that since Rome he had heard "this young girl of Lisieux" discussed in his own compartment with curiosity and admiration, and he never met her, though "he had lived for a whole month in her immediate vicinity". At Assisi she mislaid some articles of dress at the convent and missed the carriage to the station. Only Révérony's carriage was left, full. He had a companion sit with the driver and put her opposite him in the best seat. She felt "like a squirrel caught in a trap". He spoke of Carmel and promised to do all he could to help her enter at fifteen. She wrote that she then lost confidence in creatures and relied on God alone. At Florence, while others visited the Uffizi, she went to the convent of St Mary Magdalen de' Pazzi and asked at her tomb to enter Carmel. Her hand alone was small enough to pass through the grating around the body, so she touched pilgrims' rosaries to the relics. The pilgrims then passed Pisa and Genoa, along the sea, and prayed at Notre-Dame de la Garde (Marseille) and Fourvière (Lyon). They reached Paris on 2 Dec 1887.

## Part 8 — The Carmel, and Louis's illness (1888–1894)

### F-LF-184 — The Carmel of Lisieux: foundation in 1838 and its poverty
- Claim: The Carmel of Lisieux began through two sisters named Gosselin, pupils at the Carmel of Pont-Audemer, whose health barred them from entering; they decided to found a house at their own cost. Bishop Dancel approved it on 16 Dec 1835. Fr Pierre Sauvage, curate of Saint-Jacques, found nuns at Poitiers. The Gosselins took the habit at Poitiers on 14 Apr 1837. Two professed nuns of Poitiers, Sr Elizabeth of St Louis (Prioress) and Sr Geneviève of St Teresa (Sub-Prioress), came with them and arrived on 16 Mar 1838 in the rain. They lived first in a thatched house lent by Mme Le Boucher in the Chaussée de Beuvillers: two garrets for a dormitory, a room 18 feet square split in three, a box as sideboard, the floor as serving table, so few plates that they were washed during dinner. Townspeople were cold. Fr Nicolas Sauvage said Mass in a whisper "like the priests… in the Catacombs". In Sept 1838 they moved to a house in the rue de Livarot. Dinner was sometimes fried leeks. One postulant's clothing was put off for lack of cloth. The large wing was built in 1858 and the chapel blessed 6 Sep 1852. Marie de Gonzague built the second wing in 1876, with the infirmary and the cell Thérèse later used.

### F-LF-185 — Mother Geneviève of St Teresa, the "Foundress"
- Claim: Clare Bertrand was born at Poitiers on 19 Jul 1805, entered the Poitiers Carmel on 26 Mar 1830 and made profession 22 Jul 1831. She was Prioress of Lisieux several times (elected 1842, 1845, 1851, 1860 as the book prints them; the dates are inconsistent inside the book). From 1849 she had an internal tumour, and from about 1884 she was a confirmed invalid, kept in an armchair or bed. Her Superior Fr Sauvage lost confidence in her business sense after a letter from Poitiers, opened too late, said not to name her Prioress. She received the devotion to the Holy Face from the Carmel of Tours in 1847. FND says she was "in things spiritual the real Foundress". Thérèse called her a saint whose holiness "was imitable" (HA ch. viii, per FND foreword). FND says Geneviève did not guess Thérèse's destiny and was alarmed at "the boldness" of her aspirations, but "loved and esteemed her greatly". She died on 5 Dec 1891 (see F-LF-207).

### F-LF-190 — The rule she lived by
- Claim: Laveille sums up the Carmelite rule: Breviary office; the word of God "six and often seven hours a day" (office, reading, meditation); continual fast from 14 Sep to Easter plus Church fasts and every Friday of the year; perpetual abstinence from meat except in sickness; profound silence except two hours' recreation daily; work in cell or office; coarse serge; a bed of "a hard palliasse supported on three planks"; never to excuse oneself even if unjustly accused; complete obedience. Only the community room was heated. Thérèse admitted in last illness that she had suffered from cold "even to death" (a sister: she walked about fifty yards through open cloisters and an icy corridor to her cell, slept in two thin blankets, and shivered all night, never asking for relief).

### F-LF-191 — Marie de Gonzague: portrait of the prioress
- Claim: In April 1888 Mère Marie de Gonzague was in the second year of a fourth three-year term, succeeding Mère Geneviève. Laveille says she was born at Caen in 1834 of a noble Calvados family, with initiative, administrative talent, "a great heart and a charming personality", but "very impressionable, and of somewhat distrustful character, predisposed to melancholy", without always "even-balanced judgement". He says she had desired Thérèse's entry but judged a vocation so early needed testing, and "with a certain natural impulsiveness not always held in check" made the child's early life painful. She was the same nun who "built the second wing" in 1876 (FND p.12). She was elected again in 1896.

### F-LF-221 — The Act of Oblation to Merciful Love, 9 June 1895
- Claim: Thérèse pronounced her Act on the feast of the Holy Trinity, 9 Jun 1895, "as victim of holocaust to the Merciful Love of the Good God". The thought came during Mass: "shall Thy Justice alone receive victims of holocaust? Has not Thy merciful Love itself need of them?" She wrote the formula and wanted a theologian to check it. Fr Lemonnier of the Missionaries of La Délivrande, preaching a retreat, found it sound and changed one word: she had written "Infinite are the desires…", and on his advice she put "Immense". She told only two novices and renewed it until her last hour. A few days later, at the Way of the Cross, she felt "wounded with a dart of fire so ardent that I thought I should die"; she told Mère Agnès at once, who paid no heed, and only on 7 Jul 1897 did Mère Agnès question her again (HA ch. xii p.228).

### F-LF-223 — Treatments: massage, blistering, cupping, cauterizing
- Claim: On Good Friday 1896 the prioress (Marie de Gonzague, recently re-elected) let her keep all austerities; Thérèse even cleaned the convent windows between the long Offices. The prioress removed her from the sacristy. Dr de Cornière, the community doctor, and Dr La Néele (husband of cousin Jeanne) had noticed nothing alarming, and prescribed "massage, blistering, cupping, cauterizing". She endured them smiling. Céline testified that one day she counted five hundred applications of the cauterizing needle, and then folded a large blanket in four for a mattress, as she had no permission to give one (Summarium 1919 p.394 §1001). After Lent 1897 the doctor applied the needle more often. Dr de Cornière said: "I shall not be able to cure her… besides, this soul is not made for earth." A community doctor said that of a hundred persons attacked as she was, "perhaps two at most might have a chance of recovery".

### F-LF-237 — Death, 30 September 1897
- Claim: On 29 Sep, from early morning, she had a "distressing rattle in the throat"; at midday she asked, "Mother, is this the death-agony?" The Office of St Michael and prayers for the dying were read. After the doctor left she asked, "Is it today, Mother?" and Marie de Gonzague said, "Yes." On the morning of 30 Sep, looking at the statue, she said, "it is pure agony without any measure of consolation." About 3 p.m. she held out her arms like a cross and the prioress placed an image of Our Lady of Mount Carmel on her knees. She said: "Oh, my Mother, present me to the Blessed Virgin without delay." About 5 p.m. the bell called the community. For two hours the death-rattle tore her chest and the bedcovers were soaked. Céline cooled her lips with a small piece of ice. About 7 p.m. she asked, "Am I not going to die?" The prioress said yes. She said: "Well then, let it be so. Oh, I would not wish to suffer less." Gazing at the crucifix: "Oh… I LOVE Him! My God, I… LOVE… THEE!" Laveille calls these her last words. She then sank back, raised herself as if called, opened her eyes with "heavenly peace and indescribable joy", and gazed above the statue "for a space sufficient to recite a Credo"; then her head fell back. She died about 7.20 p.m. on 30 Sep 1897, aged 24 years and 9 months.

## Part 10 — After death: the book, the family, the cult (1897–1925 and later)

### F-LF-241 — How *Histoire d'une âme* was made, 1897–1898
- Claim: The Carmel archive says a dead Carmelite normally produced a short circular (about three pages) for other Carmels. Mère Agnès saw the illness was far advanced when Thérèse told her on 30 May 1897 of the first haemoptysis of April 1896. Marie de Gonzague wrote a preface near the end of 1897. Fr Godefroid Madelaine of Mondaye revised the text from early 1898 to March 1898 and proposed many cuts, mostly of intimate details (he later apologised for his "blue pencil"); Fr Norbert Paysan corrected the poems. Madelaine saw the dying Bishop Hugonin on 7 Mar 1898 and got an oral imprimatur (Laveille: imprimatur dated 8 Mar 1898); Hugonin died in early May 1898. The editors made the three manuscripts appear addressed to one person, the prioress; divided the whole into 12 chapters; added letters, about fifty poems, four prayers, passages of the plays, novices' memories and a selection of last words. Working title "Un cantique d'amour ou le passage d'un ange"; title fixed by April 1898. Isidore Guérin paid for printing; quotes he received ranged from about 1,400 to 2,100 francs per 1,000 copies; on the advice of Fr Marie des Augustins (Assumptionist) he chose the Œuvre de Saint-Paul, Bar-le-Duc, for 2,000 copies in two runs of 1,000. It appeared on 21 Oct 1898 (a note by Céline; FR Wikipedia: 20 or 21 Oct), later than hoped (30 Sep). It sold at 4 francs. 121 copies went to French Carmels, 18 to French-speaking Carmels abroad and three, via Br Siméon, to Leo XIII. FR Wikipedia, citing the 1956 introduction, says Marie de Gonzague imposed the single addressee, and when nuns asked to see the autographs she is said to have wanted to destroy them; Mère Agnès saved them by retouching the passages that showed the real addressees; Marie restored them in 1910. Mère Agnès testified that Thérèse told her to print the manuscript with prudence, "whatever you do, I will have done it".

### F-LF-246 — The "rain of roses": how devotion began
- Claim: Laveille says letters of favours arrived by hundreds and the Carmel printed them in volumes, *Pluie de roses* ("Shower of Roses"): in fifteen years seven volumes, no less than 3,000 pages; the first recorded facts go back to 1902; vol. vii covers the years between beatification and canonization. From 1898 the *Annals of the Propagation of the Faith* recorded unhoped-for mission results. A White Father in Africa wrote (Dec 1910) that he put her picture in Christian homes and catechism schools, that natives asked who this little "bikira" (virgin) was, and pagans came "in compact crowds". Père Roulland testified that she had "very remarkable influence in converting souls" in Japan, China and the Indies, and that many Trappistine nuns in Japan attributed their vocation to her. Postulants came to Lisieux from all over France, Ireland, Portugal, Italy, Constantinople and Argentina. Pius X and later popes praised the book. Laveille also quotes Maurice de Waleffe, a sceptic, in *Le Journal*: "Her short and uneventful life was that of a lily which opens at morn and silently fades at eve… She has written a book… Here we have the real miracle."

### F-LF-256 — The 1880 decrees against the unauthorised congregations

### F-LF-257 — The Ferry school laws, 1881–1882
