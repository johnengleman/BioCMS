# Notes: Laveille, St Thérèse de l'Enfant Jésus (English trans. 1928), PDF pp. 120-191
# Book page + 20 = PDF page. Quotes copied from OCR text. "[Laveille comment]" = author's own commentary/embellishment.

## Benedictine Abbey school (1881-)
p120 Léonie had attended the Benedictine convent school; Céline was admitted with her; M. Martin, satisfied with its training, decided Thérèse should replace Léonie there; Céline and Thérèse became day-boarders together.
p120 Laveille: entry to the school "marks a new phase of the long trial she had suffered since 1877" [Laveille comment].
p120 School = Benedictine Abbey of Notre-Dame-du-Pré, of Saint Désir, in a suburb of Lisieux, "at a considerable distance from Les Buissonnets". Founded 1011 (checked page image). Originally contemplative; in 17th century began teaching local girls and set up a boarding-school; interrupted by the Revolution; prosperous in 19th century; later diminished by competition of new schools.
p120 October 1881: Thérèse first went there; about sixty pupils from Lisieux town and "the best families of the neighbourhood". Instruction "solid and rather above the ordinary"; education "simple and thoroughly religious". Mix of farmers' daughters and more refined town girls caused "a certain amount of friction to the annoyance of the latter." Source footnote: "From a manuscript notice on the Abbey of Notre-Dame-du-Pré."
p121 Description of the school: "High grey walls", "faultlessly neat but somewhat austere-looking classrooms", "a chapel built in Louis-Philippe's time", "a spacious garden, but hemmed in from any outlook". Thérèse aged eight and a half. Consolation: she returned every evening to Les Buissonnets.
p121 Cousins Marie and Jeanne Guérin also attended. Routine: each morning Céline and Thérèse joined them at the pharmacy (M. Guérin's); all four walked together to the Abbey accompanied by a servant of M. Guérin.
p121 That servant later became a Benedictine; gave evidence at the Beatification Process as Sr Joséphine de la Croix (footnote). She recalled "after thirty years" her relations with Thérèse.
p121-122 Quote (Sr Joséphine de la Croix, the Guérin servant): "When the little one found herself alone with me on the way to the convent or in the house, she became affectionate and confiding, and freely told me her little secrets. These intimate conversations centred, quite naturally, on spiritual things. For her age she was exceptionally intelligent and reflective." Scene: before her First Communion, Thérèse heard workmen blaspheming and excused them: "we must not judge of the hidden things of souls, that these people had received much less grace than we, and that they were more unfortunate than blameworthy." Source: "Process of the Ordinary, p. 136."
p122 Placed at once in a class of much older girls, some fourteen years old. Some classmates equalled or surpassed her in arithmetic and orthography (spelling); she soon placed herself "amongst the first". Special attraction for Sacred History and Church History.
p122 Catechism: grasped doctrine easily but literal recital of the text "was at first a difficult effort".
p122 M. l'Abbé Domin, chaplain of the convent, taught religious instruction; he named her "his little Doctor" for precise answers to difficult questions.
p122 When she occasionally got less than her usual marks in religious doctrine she was "inconsolable" because her father would have less joy "that evening when he examined her notes" (M. Martin checked her marks each evening).
p122-123 Turbulent pupils misbehaved in corridors/staircases when unsupervised; Thérèse looked on "dumbfounded"; her silent disapproval resented.
p123 Bullying, Thérèse's own words: "There was one pupil in particular, aged fourteen, ... possessed of little intelligence, able, nevertheless, to pose before the others as better than she really was. Seeing me, so young, almost always first at composition and beloved by all the nuns, she became jealous and made me pay in many ways for my little successes. With my timid and delicate nature I knew not how to defend myself, and simply wept in silence." Source: "Histoire d'une Ame, ch. iii, p. 37."
p123 Laveille: temptation of children "to torment collectively the rival" [Laveille comment, but implies group teasing].
p123 She disliked noisy recreation; her joy was unpetalling roses and strewing petals before the Blessed Virgin's statue, decorating a little altar. "Unskilful in handling a racquet or a croquet mallet"; weaker pupils took revenge by mocking her awkwardness at games.
p123-124 Often ill, she stayed home on some class-days but still completed composition exercises set; some companions suspected she stayed home on purpose to study for competitions. Source: "Cf. manuscript notice on the Abbey of Notre-Dame-du-Pré."
p124 Result: she often spent recreations apart, alone with her catechism memorizing, or with a few "model children".
p124 Sœur Marie du Saint Rosaire (older pupil, later a Benedictine nun; "president of a pious association") quote: "Thérèse came during recreation, according to the custom of the school, to ask advice. She was then about ten, and I was greatly surprised at her question. She asked me to explain to her the method of meditation. She also described to me then, as far as I can remember, how she herself made mental prayer on holidays. She hid behind her bed-curtain the better to recollect herself, 'and there,' she said, 'I think.'" (no separate footnote given for this quote).
p124-125 Teachers noted she hardly ever followed the prayer-book method at Mass; they signalled her by word or look; she thanked them with a smile and went back to her own prayer. She prayed "without noise of words."
p125 Nuns did not fully see her worth; her timidity was seen as a fault [Laveille comment on "trial from Providence"].
p125 A nun's portrait: "A winning and delicate smile was her habitual expression of countenance, as soon as her tears, too ready and too frequent it must indeed be said, were dried. Sweet and gracious manners, tender piety, obedience in even the smallest things, a shrinking from turbulent and noisy associates or games, such were the characteristics of her school life. All this was, however, veiled by the excessive timidity and sensitiveness already noted." Source: "Manuscript notice on the Abbey of Notre-Dame-du-Pré."
p125 Evening reunion of the "little Queen" and her "dear King" (M. Martin); holidays at Les Buissonnets with Céline; hours with the Guérin family; played "solitaries" (hermits) with cousin Marie Guérin.
p126 Scene: returning from the Abbey one evening, Thérèse said to Marie Guérin, "Lead me; I am going to close my eyes." Marie: "I want to close mine too". Walking blind on a footpath crowded with wares, they tumbled over cases of early vegetables set out by a grocer. He was "furious, and fumed and threatened" as he gathered them; the two girls opened their eyes and ran away.

## Pauline enters Carmel; vocation first felt
p126 Laveille: the devil, jealous, tried "to cut off this life" [Laveille comment].
p126 Thérèse overheard Pauline and Marie talking of Pauline's intention of soon entering Carmel. Pauline then described cloister life to her.
p126-127 One evening, by "sudden illumination", Thérèse saw Carmel was the "distant desert" she had dreamed of; "that from that day onward there was never the least doubt in her mind." Source: "Histoire d'une Ame, ch. iii, p. 42."
p127 Pauline encouraged her to tell the Mother Prioress of the Lisieux Carmel; the Prioress heard the child's "great confidences", did not deny the vocation, but said she did not take postulants of nine years; must wait until her sixteenth year. (Prioress unnamed here.)
p127 Pauline entered Carmel October 1882; became Sœur Agnès de Jésus. At first parlour visits Pauline could give "scarcely more than two or three minutes" to Thérèse. Months after: "bitter sadness and physical suffering which tended to increase day by day."
p127 Laveille frames the coming illness as demonic: "The demon was going to profit by her weak condition" [Laveille comment].

## The 1883 illness ("strange malady")
p127 March 1883: M. Martin in Paris with Marie and Léonie for Holy Week ceremonies in the great churches; suddenly called back to Lisieux.
p128 Thérèse and Céline had been left in Mme Guérin's charge. After a conversation with her uncle (M. Guérin) who spoke about her dead mother, she wept silently. Later that evening seized "without any apparent cause, by fits of violent trembling." Taken back to Les Buissonnets; condition "decidedly critical".
p128 Symptoms: "Terrifying visions drew from her cries of distress"; "Strange words which she seemed to articulate in spite of herself, then long hours of prostrate suffering". One day she tried to climb over the bed-rail to throw herself to the ground; sisters restrained her by force.
p128 Léonie quote: "One Sunday ... I had remained alone to take care of her during High Mass. Seeing her very calm, I ventured to leave her for a few moments. On returning I found her stretched on the floor between the bed and the wall. She might have been killed or badly hurt, but, thanks to God, she had not even received a scratch." Source: "Deposition at the Process of the Ordinary, p. 130."
p128 Hallucinations: nails in the walls of her room looked like "huge fingers burnt black"; she cried "I am frightened! I am frightened!" Face wore "an indescribable expression of terror".
p128 Scene: one evening M. Martin sat by her bed holding his hat; she stared, then with horror at the hat cried in a choking voice, "Oh, the big black beast!" "The poor father went away in tears."
p128-129 Sometimes she did not recognize her relatives; sometimes she hit her head violently against the sides of the bed.
p129 Dr Notta ("a conscientious practitioner") said: "Science is powerless in face of these phenomena; nothing can be done." Source: "Cf. Deposition of Sœur Marie du Sacré-Cœur (Marie) at the Process of the Ordinary."
p129 Thérèse later wrote that even in the worst crisis she never lost the use of reason; heard and understood everything around her. Laveille takes this as proof of a "hidden power" attacking her [Laveille comment].
p129 Pauline's clothing (reception of the habit) approached; not mentioned before Thérèse lest regret worsen her; but Thérèse declared she would go. On the day she embraced Pauline, sat on her lap, hid under her veil, received caresses. Then returned by carriage to Les Buissonnets; next morning "a fresh attack of even greater violence". (Date of clothing not given here.)
p129 Continual watch again; Marie nursed her "with unwearied kindness"; the invalid would hardly let Marie leave the room except for church or Carmel.
p129 M. Martin requested a novena of Masses for her cure at Notre-Dame-des-Victoires, Paris.

## The cure: smile of the Virgin (Pentecost Sunday 1883 per other sources; here "On Sunday, during the novena")
p130 Thérèse's account: "On Sunday, during the novena, Marie went out into the garden, leaving me with Léonie who was reading near the window. After a few minutes I began to call in almost a whisper 'Marie, Marie.' Léonie, accustomed to hear me continually calling in that way, paid no attention. I then cried out loudly, and Marie came back to me. I saw her perfectly as she came in, but for the first time failed to recognize her. I looked searchingly all round, gazed anxiously into the garden, and began again to call 'Marie, Marie.' It was unutterable suffering, this inexplicable and constrained strife..."
p130 Marie whispered to Léonie and went out "pale and trembling". Léonie carried Thérèse to the window; Marie in the garden held out her arms, smiling, calling "Thérèse, my little Thérèse"; still not recognized. Marie, weeping, knelt at the foot of the bed and turned to the Blessed Virgin (statue); Léonie and Céline did the same.
p130 Thérèse: "Finding no help on earth and almost dead from grief, I also turned towards my heavenly Mother beseeching her with all my heart to have pity on me."
p130-131 Thérèse: "All at once the statue became animated. The Virgin Mary became so beautiful that I shall never find words to express that heavenly loveliness. Her countenance breathed sweetness, goodness, and ineffable tenderness; but what penetrated to the depths of my soul was her ravishing smile. Then all my pain vanished; two big tears gushed from my eyes and fell silently." ... "The holy Virgin advanced towards me. She smiled on me ... how happy I am, thought I. But I will tell no one, for then my happiness would vanish." Then she recognized Marie "without effort". Source: "Histoire d'une Ame, ch. iii, p. 48."
p131 Witnesses present: Marie, Léonie, Céline. Marie, seeing her face transfigured, guessed a miracle; questioned Thérèse, who confirmed the apparition; all trace of illness gone.
p131 Marie told the Carmel "immediately though discreetly"; Thérèse later meant to tell only Pauline, but nuns who had heard rumours questioned her in the parlour; her reserved account was talked over by nuns "at the risk of being modified or amplified". Thérèse feared her clumsy account caused inaccuracies; "her mind became tortured with anxieties, which were quieted only years afterwards before another statue" (Our Lady of Victories, Paris, 1887 - see later).
p131 Footnote 2: her fears were "sometimes so great as to obscure in her memory the reality of the heavenly vision"; Laveille calls it divine permission to keep her humble [Laveille comment]; her account "confirmed in every detail" by Marie's deposition at the Process of the Ordinary (quoted next page).
p132 Marie's deposition (footnote, full): "The most terrible crisis in Thérèse's illness was that which she has described in her Life. I believed that she was about to succumb. It was then that, with my sisters, I threw myself on my knees at the feet of the holy Virgin. Three times I repeated the same prayer. At the third repetition I saw Thérèse gaze fixedly at the statue of the Blessed Virgin. Her countenance became radiant like one in ecstasy. She confided to me that she had seen the Blessed Virgin herself. This vision lasted four or five minutes, then she looked tenderly at me. From this time onward no trace of her malady was apparent. On the following day she resumed her ordinary mode of life." Source: "Summarium of 1919, p. 416."
p132 The statue: long owned by the Martin family (mentioned earlier in book); a copy of Bouchardon's model, which was executed in silver for the Church of Saint-Sulpice, Paris, and disappeared in the Revolution. Now stands over the Saint's tomb in a side chapel of the Lisieux Carmel called "the Chapel of the Shrine".
p132 "the demon was conquered. Once more the Immaculate had crushed him with her heel." [Laveille comment]

## Holiday at Alençon (1883)
p132 Before First Communion, to restore her health, M. Martin accepted invitations of old acquaintances; took her to Alençon; friends received them in their châteaux of Saint-Denis, Grogny, and "very probably Lanchal". Fine season; she was fêted and petted.
p133 Thérèse admits she yielded "for the moment" to the charm of attentions. Later reflection (Thérèse): "Alas! how well the world plans to combine the joys of earth with the service of God. How seldom it thinks of death." Phrase "the bewitchment of trifles" quoted. (No footnote.)

## First Communion delay; Mgr Hugonin
p133 When told that in the early Church fragments of the Host were given to little children, she exclaimed: "Why is it not so now?"
p133 Diocesan rule: child must complete her eleventh year within the year of admission. Born January 2, 1873, she was "two days short" when Spring 1883 First Communion came; she lamented "these two unlucky days".
p133 Scene: in Lisieux with Marie, she saw the Bishop of Bayeux, Mgr Hugonin, walking toward the station with one of his Vicars-General. Thérèse: "Oh, Marie, shall I run and ask his permission to make my first Communion this year?" Marie had "considerable difficulty" preventing her.

## Preparation for First Communion (1884)
p133-134 Restored to health, she returned to the Benedictine Abbey. Marie (since Pauline's departure supplementing the nuns' teaching) undertook her preparation; each evening in Thérèse's room or the garden of Les Buissonnets.
p134 Pauline, from Carmel, prepared "a beautiful little notebook" for Thérèse to record daily sacrifices and aspirations of love (recalling the earlier chaplet of "practices"). After three months the booklet recorded 818 sacrifices and 2,774 acts of love.
p134-135 Thérèse asked Marie for half an hour of mental prayer daily; refused. Marie quote: "she asked my permission for at least a quarter of an hour's mental prayer every day. I did not grant this either. Seeing her so fervent, and that she comprehended in so exalted a manner the things of heaven, I thought it better to act with very great reserve on this point. The dear little one submitted with her usual docility; but innocently, and without suspecting that she was thus giving herself up to real contemplation, she used to hide herself in a corner of her room which could be easily closed in by the bed-curtains, and there she would spend a long time on the half-holidays 'thinking about the good God, about the shortness of this life, and about eternity.'" (no footnote given).
p135 Final retreat week: Thérèse stayed day and night at the Abbey convent. Each evening the first mistress came with "her little lantern", drew back the bed-curtains and kissed her forehead. Thérèse to her: "O Madame, I love you so well that I am going to confide to you a great secret." She showed her Pauline's booklet, which she kept hidden under her pillow. Classes in the study hall recapitulated Abbé Domin's instruction.

## First Communion day (date implied: 8 days before Ascension 15 May = 8 May 1884; Laveille does not state it explicitly here)
p135 Thérèse: "At last ... the happy day of all days dawned for me. What ineffable memories the least details of those celestial hours left in my soul. The joyous awakening at break of day, the tender and respectful kisses of my mistresses and bigger companions, the dressing-room filled with snowy vesture in which each child was clothed in turn; above all, our entrance to the chapel to the strains of the matin-hymn: 'O sacred altar girt with angel-guard!'"
p136 Thérèse: "Ah, how sweet it was, this first kiss of Jesus to my soul! Yes, it was a kiss of love. I felt that I was loved, and I said in return: 'I love Thee; I give myself to Thee for ever.' ... On this day, our meeting could not be called by the simple name of regard but of Fusion. We were no longer two; Thérèse had disappeared like the drop of water which is lost in the ocean's depths; Jesus alone remained". Source: "Histoire d'une Ame, ch. iv, p. 58." Laveille footnote explains "fusion" does not deny duality [Laveille comment].
p136 She wept for joy; companions said: "Why did she cry? Had she some scruple of conscience? No, it was because she had not her mother near her, or her Carmelite sister whom she loves so much." Source: "Histoire d'une Ame, ch. iv, p. 59."
p137 That evening she wrote three resolutions in her notebook: (1) "I will never give way to discouragement." (2) "I will say the Memorare every day." (3) "I will endeavour to humble my pride." Source (checked page image): "Deposition of Sœur Françoise-Thérèse (Léonie) religious of the Visitation, at the Process of the Ordinary, p. 132."
p137 That afternoon Thérèse recited the Act of Consecration to the Blessed Virgin in the name of all the communicants.
p137 Same day Pauline made her profession at Carmel. After the Abbey ceremony M. Martin took Thérèse to the Carmel to see Pauline "wearing a white veil like her own, and crowned with roses." Thérèse felt envy of Pauline's lot, not pain.
p137 Family meal at Les Buissonnets; M. Martin, "in accordance with custom", gave his "little Queen" a pretty watch.
p137-138 She had to wait eight days for second Communion: May 15, feast of Ascension (1884), with her father and eldest sister (Marie). Communion then allowed only on greater feasts (before Pius X's reform); she hungered for it.
p138 Scene: before a Communion, Marie spoke of the role of suffering, adding perhaps God would not lead her that way. After Communion Thérèse felt desire for the cross and conviction it would be granted: "Then ... my soul was flooded with consolation such as I have never again known in my whole life." Source: "Histoire d'une Ame, ch. iv, p. 61."

## Confirmation 14 June 1884
p138-139 Céline quote: "She received the sacrament of Confirmation on June 14, 1884. The days immediately preceding are in particular deeply graven in my memory. Thérèse, usually so calm, was no longer the same; a sort of enthusiasm and holy rapture were perceptible in her exterior. One day, during her preparatory retreat, I expressed my astonishment at seeing her thus. She explained to me what she understood regarding the power of this sacrament, of the Holy Spirit taking possession of her whole being. There was in her words such conviction, in her countenance such ardour, that, penetrated by a sense of the supernatural, I came away profoundly moved. This incident so struck me that I can see even now her actions, her attitude, the place where she stood, and the memory will never be effaced from my mind." Source: "Deposition of Sœur Geneviève de la Sainte-Face [OCR 'Sainte-Foie'] at the Process of the Ordinary, p. 123."

## School 1884-85; friendships
p139 After vacation, studies resumed with more success. Recreations: gave "honourable burial" under the large chestnut-trees of the Abbey to little birds fallen from nests. Told stories so well the older pupils formed a circle round her until the Sister in charge, wanting play not talk, broke up the group.
p139 Two special friends; one attachment fleeting. The other friend, after some months' absence, returned and gave her "merely a look of indifference". Thérèse: "But ... the good God has given me so faithful a heart, that when it has once loved it loves always; so I continue to pray for that companion, and I love her still." Source: "Histoire d'une Ame, ch. iv, p. 63."
p140 She kept away from pupils' attachments to particular mistresses. Thérèse: "How I thank God for having allowed me to find nothing but bitterness in earthly friendship. With a heart like mine I should have let myself be captured and my wings cut. How then could I fly away and be at rest!" Source: "Histoire d'une Ame, ch. iv, p. 64."

## Scruples begin; Deauville/Trouville 1885
p140 May 1885: renewed (second, "solemn") Communion with companions; no joyous sense of Presence. "She had been tormented with scruples for several days, and this heavy trial was to last for many months."
p140 Day after ceremony, aunt Mme Guérin took her to Deauville with her own children (Marie and Jeanne). Family took a villa on the quay de la Touques, then called "Chalet Colombe". Church of Notre-Dame-des-Victoires (Deauville) "a long distance away"; they went every evening to May devotions; Thérèse insisted when others hesitated; never let fatigue stop her. Also early Mass there despite storm and rain.
p141 September 1885: Mme Guérin's second visit to Trouville-Deauville, in a house in the rue Charlemagne; invited her nieces, M. Martin being away on a long journey.
p141 Scene (headache): one evening in the little sitting-room, Marie Guérin (about fourteen) complained of a headache; her mother fondled her "addressing her in the most affectionate terms without obtaining anything but tears." Thérèse (who had frequent headaches but never complained) a few days later sank on a chair in a corner of the sitting-room and cried; asked why, said "I have a headache." No one sympathized; her aunt gravely reproved her as one playing the spoiled child. Source footnote: "Cf. Deposition of Sœur Joséphine de la Croix, a Benedictine nun, at the Process of the Ordinary, p. 141."
p141-142 Cousin Jeanne Guérin, "slightly sarcastic": "Thérèse is wanting in confidence and simplicity. She is, in all probability, tormented by some big scruple and does not dare admit it." Source: "Cf. 'La Mission providentielle de la Bienheureuse Thérèse de l'Enfant Jésus,' an article published in La Croix of November, 1913, by M. l'Abbé Lepetit, professor of the Seminary of Caen."
p142 Thérèse: "I was paid back in my own coin, and I firmly resolved never again to imitate others, for I understood now the fable of 'The Ass and the Dog.' I was the ass who, on seeing the lap-dog petted, placed his unwieldy hoof on the table to be kissed in turn. If I was not driven away with blows like the poor animal, I received, nevertheless, the price of my folly, and this price cured me for ever of the desire to attract attention." Source: "Histoire d'une Ame, ch. iv, p. 67."
p142 Scene (ribbons): Mme Guérin gave her "sky-blue" ribbons for her golden hair; pleased at first; then felt obliged to confess, even at Trouville, "this childish pleasure which, to her, seemed a sin." Source: "Ibid., p. 66" (Histoire d'une Ame ch. iv) (checked page image).

## M. Martin's journey, Aug-Oct 1885
p142 Rest of vacation at Les Buissonnets; prolonged absence of her father, a privation she felt keenly.
p142 At the "earnest desire" of Abbé Marie, parish priest of Saint-Jacques, Lisieux, M. Martin toured Germany, Austria, Constantinople, and Italy; six weeks; series of letters to his daughters.
p142-143 Itinerary: Munich (museums); Vienna (bridges "unequalled in the traveller's experience even in Paris"; the Belvedere); Constantinople (Santa Sophia, panorama from the tower of Galata); then Italy: Naples ("that enchanting city"), Pompeii, Rome (St Peter's "the most magnificent in the world"), Milan on the eve of re-entering France.
p143 Pet names (footnote: "Affectionate titles which M. Martin loved to give to his daughters"): Marie "his Diamond"; "good Léonie"; "the Intrepid" Céline; Thérèse "Queen of his heart"; Pauline "the delicate Pearl" of Carmel.
p143 Letter from Vienna, reply to feast-day wishes for August 25, 1885: "I seemed to see you all around me in the Belvedere, and to hear Thérèse's sweet and sympathetic voice murmur a little compliment to me. It so moved me that I wished to be back at Lisieux, to be there in good earnest to embrace you all." Source: "Unpublished letter of August 30, 1885."
p143 From Rome to Marie: "It is certainly here that I experience the greatest joy. Tell 'my Pearl' (Pauline) that my happiness is too great to last." Source: "Unpublished letter of September 27, 1885." Laveille asks if a presentiment [Laveille comment].
p143-144 From Milan (eve of re-entering France): "Everything I see is splendid, but with earthly beauty; our heart is never satisfied as long as it sees not the Infinite Beauty. ... Welcome the intimate joy of family life! It is this which brings us nearest to the beauty above." Source: "Unpublished letter of October 6, 1885."

## Leaving school; private lessons; Children of Mary (1885-87)
p144 Abbey classes re-opened October 1885; Thérèse already attending some days when M. Martin returned. She went alone: Céline had finished her studies at end of the previous school-year.
p144 Scruples "far from being calmed, had, alas, redoubled." She worked diligently but "suffered so much from headaches" that after a few months her father withdrew her from school: beginning of 1886.
p144 New arrangement: several times a week she was brought to "a lady of good position" for lessons (lady unnamed here); also meant as first contact with the world. Scene: she sat at a desk "in an antiquely furnished room, surrounded by lesson- and copy-books where numbers of well-meaning ladies came daily in search of distraction." One lady went "into ecstasy" over her beautiful hair; another whispered asking who the pretty little girl was. Thérèse heard all; feared for her humility.
p144-145 To join the Association (Sodality) of the Children of Mary at the Abbey she accepted a new probation among older pupils; condition: attend two or three times a week a lesson in manual work at the Abbey.
p145 Mother Saint-Placide, directress of the boarding-school, told Sodality members on receiving her request: "We shall never have to regret her name on our list." (no footnote).
p145 She came "without enthusiasm ... and even with a certain amount of weariness, 'for she had not, like the others, a favourite amongst the mistresses.'" Thérèse: "and then, no one noticing me, ... I went up to the tribune of the chapel until my father came for me." Source: "Histoire d'une Ame, ch. iv, p. 68."
p145 Senior girl, later Benedictine Sœur Saint-Jean l'Evangéliste, admired her recollection and tried to catch her after work, but Thérèse went before the Tabernacle, "usually remained there for more than an hour." Source: "Cf. Deposition of Sœur Saint-Jean l'Evangéliste at the Process of the Ordinary, p. 154."
p145 Footnote anecdote: this same older girl once teased Thérèse by pretending to doubt her sincerity; the "grieved and astonished" Thérèse replied: "Marie, I never tell a lie." (implies the companion's name was Marie).
p145 Became a Child of Mary officially on May 31, 1887; "last notable favour" from the Benedictines; she stayed grateful all her life.
p146 Footnote: letter she wrote soon after entering Carmel to Mother Sainte-Placide, her old Directress, given in Appendix.

## Marie leaves for Carmel (Oct 1886); end of scruples
p146 October 1886: Thérèse nearly fourteen; sister Marie, her "principal confidante", carried out a plan formed months before to join Pauline at Carmel. Thérèse first shed tears; asked who would now guide her in troubles of conscience.
p146 She prayed to her four little brothers and sisters who died in infancy, asking peace of mind, to prove "up there they still know how to love." Result: "A delicious peace filled her soul hitherto so tormented"; scruples ceased. (No footnote.)
p146 Still left: "excessive sensitiveness which, even yet, often betrayed itself by floods of tears."

## Christmas 1886 "conversion"
p146-147 Thérèse: "On that holy night ... Jesus, the sweet Infant of an hour, changed the night of my soul into floods of light. In making Himself weak and little for my sake, He made me strong and courageous. He clothed me with His armour, and since then I go from victory to victory, advancing, as it were, with giant strides. The fountain of my tears was dried up, not to be reopened, save rarely and with difficulty."
p147 Scene (Thérèse's words): returning to Les Buissonnets after midnight Mass, she expected her shoes on the hearthstone filled with gifts "as in my infant days—which proves that I had been, till now, treated like a little baby." Papa loved to watch her delight. This time, "contrary to his usual habit of indulging me in everything", he felt annoyed. Going up the stairs to her room she heard him say: "For a big girl like Thérèse, this is too childish; I hope this will be the last time."
p147 Céline, low voice: "Do not go down immediately; wait a little while; you would surely cry when looking at the presents before papa."
p147 Thérèse: "Driving back my tears, I went down quickly to the dining-room, then, endeavouring to quell my beating heart, I took the shoes and drew out joyously all the gifts, looking as happy as a queen. Papa laughed; no trace of displeasure appeared on his countenance; Céline thought she was in a dream. ... little Thérèse had regained for ever her strength of soul lost at the age of four and a half years."
p148 Thérèse: "In that luminous night began the third period of my life, the most beautiful of all and the most full of graces from heaven. In an instant Jesus, pleased with my good will, accomplished the work that I had failed to do in many years." Source: "Histoire d'une Ame, ch. v, p. 75."
p148 Poem, in imitation of a Chateaubriand poem, first stanza: "Oh, well-beloved memory, / The joyous days of infancy!" Later stanzas mention the plain, wheatfield gleaning with sisters, the little white daisy, the Sunday walk, "The evening meal, the perfume of the rose, / The summer-day at Buissonnets / So gay." Source: "Cf. Histoire d'une Ame, Poem: 'What I have Loved.'" Written in Carmel.
p148-149 Laveille: trial "at an end"; she would become "one of the most valiant souls" [Laveille comment].

## Chapter VI: Vocation to Carmel - obstacles - journey to Italy
p150 Thérèse's summary of the Christmas grace: "Charity entered my heart with the need of entire forgetfulness of self; from that time forth I was happy." Source: "Histoire d'une Ame, ch. v, p. 75." Aged fourteen. References to St Francis and St Augustine [Laveille comment].
p151 Scene (Thérèse): "One Sunday, in closing my book at the end of Mass, a picture representing Our Saviour on the cross slipped out a little from the pages, showing me one of the Divine Hands pierced and bleeding. ... My heart was rent with grief at the sight of this precious Blood falling to the earth with no one eager to gather it as it fell, and I resolved that in spirit I would stand continually at the foot of the cross to receive the Divine dew of salvation and to pour it out on souls. From that day the cry of the dying Jesus, 'I thirst,' resounded in my soul at every instant ... I felt myself devoured with the thirst for souls, and I desired at all costs to snatch sinners from eternal flames." Source: "Histoire d'une Ame, ch. v, p. 75."
p151 Prioress had said she was too young to think of entering.
p151-152 Léonie: "At this period, Thérèse, being constantly at home, was in truth the joy of the family. The very servants had a great love for her; everything about her breathed of peace, goodness, and consideration for all. She always forgot self in order to bring pleasure to others. Her evenness of temper was so simple and seemed so natural that no one would believe her perpetual renunciation cost her anything." Source: "Deposition of Sœur Françoise-Thérèse of the Visitation, at the Apostolic Process." (no page)
p152 Thérèse, later to one of her sisters: "I had great compassion for those in service. In noticing the difference between masters and servants, I said to myself: 'How well this proves that there must be a heaven where each will be placed according to his interior merit. How well then will the poor and lowly be recompensed for the humiliations which they have endured in this life.'" (no footnote)
p152 Poor at Les Buissonnets: each Monday beggars rang at the garden gate; Thérèse gave them a piece of white bread and protected them from "barking Tom, the faithful watch-dog who made himself an object of terror to beggars."
p152 In town she now accompanied her father more often; beggars under sculptured gables appealed to M. Martin's purse at almost every street corner.
p152 Mme Guérin's maid (later a Benedictine = Sr Joséphine de la Croix) quote: "When she was scarcely fourteen years of age she visited poor little girls and taught them their catechism. I went with her several times to these families. I was then witness of her joy and of the gratitude shown her by the children." Source: "Deposition of Sœur Joséphine-de-la-Croix, O.S.B., at the Apostolic Process, p. 140."
p153 Thérèse: "During the illness of the mother of a family, I looked after her two children, the elder of whom was only six years old. ... instead of promising them toys or sweets, I would speak to them of the eternal recompense which the Little Jesus would give to good children." The elder promised to give way to her sister and "would never forget the lessons of the 'grand lady,' as she called me." Source: "Histoire d'une Ame, ch. v, p. 87."
p153 Mme Guérin's servant confided she did not feel love for God: "As I did not at all feel this love, I mentioned the fact to her, saying that I had not this love for God by any means. She explained to me that love does not consist in sentiment, but in the practice of virtue." (no footnote)
p154 Deathbed words (Thérèse): "I beseech the good God that all the prayers which are offered for me serve not to allay my sufferings, but may be entirely for sinners." Source: "Deposition of Sœur Geneviève de la Sainte-Face at the Apostolic Process, p. 369."

## Pranzini (1887)
p154 M. Martin did not let his daughters read newspapers, "not even La Croix, to which he was a subscriber", but topics of the day were discussed in the family.
p154 "In the latter part of June, 1887" (Laveille's dating): everyone spoke of the murder of two women and a girl in the rue Montaigu, Paris, by Pranzini. He showed no repentance and refused religious help. Thérèse "at fifty leagues distance" resolved to save his soul.
p154-155 Thérèse: "In order to succeed in preventing the irremediable misfortune, I employed every spiritual means imaginable, and, knowing that of myself I could do nothing, I offered for the ransom of this unfortunate man the Infinite merits of Our Saviour and the fruits of the Church's treasury." Prayer: "My God, I feel assured that You will pardon the unfortunate Pranzini; I would believe this even if he did not go to confession or give any mark of contrition, so great confidence have I in Your mercy. But this is my first sinner; for that reason I ask but a sign of repentance for my own consolation." Source: "Histoire d'une Ame, ch. v, p. 76."
p155 She now thought it allowable to glance at the newspaper daily (against her father's rule) to follow his fate.
p155 September 1 (1887): she read in La Croix the execution report: handed to Deibler (executioner) and assistants; hair cut, neck bared, hands bound; "at two minutes to five, while the birds sang in the trees around"; order "Sabre au clair"; he appeared "with livid face"; chaplain stood in front to hide the machine; he repelled priest and executioners; Deibler pushed him onto the guillotine; an assistant held his head by the hair. "But before the final stroke ... he asked for the chaplain's crucifix, and three times he kissed it." An assistant lifted the head "by one ear".
p156 La Croix report ends: "we said to ourselves that if human justice is satisfied, perhaps too this last kiss will have satisfied Divine Justice which demands, above all, repentance." Source: "La Croix, 1 September, 1887."
p156 On reading this Thérèse's tears betrayed her emotion "and she was obliged to run away" (to hide; she was not supposed to read papers).
p156 Thérèse: "I had then obtained the desired sign, and this sign was very consoling to me. ... the lips of 'mon premier enfant' were pressed to the Divine Wounds. Ah, what an ineffable response! My desire to save souls increased each day since this wonderful grace. I seemed to hear Jesus whisper to me as He did to the Samaritan woman: 'Give Me to drink.' ... the more I gave to Him drink, the more did the thirst of my poor soul increase". Source: "Histoire d'une Ame, ch. v, p. 76-77."
p156 She weighed other vocations: foreign missionary nun, nursing Sister, priests' helper in parish works; an interior voice pointed to the contemplative life [partly Laveille comment].
p157 Céline (Sœur Geneviève de la Sainte-Face): "The Servant of God ... has herself confided to me the reason of this preference. It was in order to suffer more, and by this means to gain more souls to Jesus. She reasoned that it is more difficult to nature to work without seeing the fruit of its labour, without encouragement, without distraction of any kind; that the most painful of all tasks is that of conquering self." Thérèse was "longing ... 'to become a prisoner as soon as possible, in order to give the beauty and freedom of heaven to souls.' Finally, in entering Carmel, her special object was to pray for priests and to immolate herself in the interests of holy Church." Source: "Deposition at the Process of the Ordinary, p. 204."
p157 Footnote anecdote: during the Rome journey a pilgrim lent her annals of missionary nuns; she accepted with enthusiasm, then gave them to her sister (Céline) saying: "I will not read them, for I have too ardent a desire to consecrate myself to works of zeal, and I wish to be hidden in a cloister so as to give myself more completely to the good God." Source: "The Spirit of Saint Thérèse de l'Enfant Jésus."
p157 Maxim of St John of the Cross adopted "without even the aid of a director": "The smallest movement of pure love is more useful to the Church than all works combined."
p158 Pauline encouraged her; Marie, "with her authority as eldest sister", considered her too young and opposed immediate entrance. Speaking through the grille was rare and difficult.
p158 Céline, who also aspired to religious life, "suppressing her own desire", consented to let Thérèse go first.
p158 Evenings: Céline and Thérèse sat in the "Belvedere" (at Les Buissonnets) hand in hand watching trees and stars, talking of Carmel; quoted Canticle (Cant. viii 1): "Having found Him without, He has given us His kiss so that no one can despise us."
p158 M. Martin had both girls learn housekeeping and "the different duties that fall within a woman's sphere".
p158-159 Daily routine: Mass each morning. Thérèse usually received Communion in the 15th-century chapel forming the apse of the cathedral (St-Pierre, Lisieux), by a tradition "sometimes denied" built by Bishop Cauchon in expiation of his part in Joan of Arc's trial.
p159 Her confessor (unnamed), "contrary to the custom of that time", allowed her Communion several times a week.
p159 She continued lessons with the lady teacher chosen by her father; teacher proud of her; Thérèse's desire for knowledge went beyond the teacher's programme.
p159-160 Imitation of Christ: her constant companion; learned it by heart in a few months; could repeat entire chapters.
p160 Abbé Arminjon, Conferences on The End of the World and the Mysteries of the Future Life. Thérèse: "The reading of that book ... brought to my soul a happiness not of earth. I foresaw already what God has in store for those who love Him, and seeing how great is the eternal recompense compared with the trifling sacrifices of this life, I longed to love Jesus, to love Him ardently, and give Him a thousand marks of tenderness while it was yet in my power." Source: "Histoire d'une Ame, ch. v, p. 78."
p160 Thérèse: "I found but one soul ... to encourage me in my vocation: that of my dear Pauline. My heart found in hers a faithful echo, and without her, I would certainly never have arrived at the sacred shore which she had reached five years before." Source: "Ibid., p. 81." (checked page image)

## Telling her father (Pentecost 1887)
p161 M. Martin then sixty-four. Looked healthy but weakened by "an attack of paralysis" from which he quickly recovered; friends feared recurrence. Also once stung while fishing by "a poisonous fly", leaving "a small excrescence" on his neck that resisted all remedies and sometimes caused great pain.
p161 Thérèse, fourteen and a half, decided to enter Carmel at the coming Christmas (anniversary of her "grace of conversion"). Chose the Day of Pentecost for her "great disclosure".
p161 Scene: after the long ceremonies at the cathedral and Vespers, M. Martin, "a little fatigued", sat at the entrance to the garden at the back of the house, hands joined, in May sunshine at sunset. [Descriptive detail of sunset/birds is Laveille's embellishment.]
p162 Thérèse, eyes full of tears, sat beside him without a word. He drew her to his heart: "What is it, my little Queen? Tell me...." He rose to hide emotion and walked slowly with his arm round her. Amid tears she revealed her call to Carmel and wish to enter soon.
p162 He wept. Then said she was very young for so grave a decision; listened to her reasons, which she gave calmly. He went to a wall where grew tiny white lily-like flowers, plucked one and gave it to her as a symbol of virginal purity. (Footnote: a white marble group of this scene now stands on the spot in the garden of Les Buissonnets.) Comparison to Christ's agony [Laveille comment].

## Uncle Guérin's opposition (1887)
p162 Since her mother's death, M. Guérin was her guardian; his consent needed. He was surprised, troubled, at first against it.
p162-163 M. Guérin: "This would be ... a unique case in the whole of France. It would, in fact, be almost a scandal for a child of fifteen years to enter Carmel." He would oppose it with all his power; "a miracle would be necessary" to change his mind.
p163 Thérèse in consternation; "for three days she felt abandoned by God and man"; dark clouds over the sky. On the fourth day he had changed: "A miracle is no longer necessary ... I have prayed to God to give me an unbiassed inclination of heart, and my prayer has been granted. Go in peace, my dear child. You are a little privileged flower that the Saviour wills to cull for Himself; I will not stand in opposition." He embraced her. (no footnote)

## Canon Delatroëtte's refusal
p163 The Mother Prioress (named p164: Mother Marie de Gonzague), having seen Thérèse often in the parlour, was now willing; had to consult the ecclesiastical superior of the community representing the Bishop: M. le Chanoine Delatroëtte (checked page image; OCR "Delatréette"), curé of Saint-Jacques parish, where the convent stands. "A man of lively faith, of a disposition in the main benevolent, but with rigid, definitely moulded convictions".
p164 He held no one should enter the Carmel before completing her twenty-first year. Footnote: the Carmelite Constitutions fixed no age limit for postulants; they only required profession not before the completed seventeenth year.
p164 Scene: when the Canon visited the infirmary to see the venerable Mother Geneviève de Sainte-Thérèse, foundress of the Lisieux Carmel, she (at the Prioress's suggestion) renewed the petition. Delatroëtte's "outburst": "Again this young girl ... To hear you talk, one would think that the salvation of the community depended on the entrance of this child. There is no danger in delay. Let her remain with her father until she has reached her majority. Besides, do you believe that I would persist in refusing without having consulted God? Let no one speak further to me on this affair." Source: "Deposition of Rev. Mère Agnès de Jésus at the Apostolic Process, p. 161." (checked image)
p164 M. Martin now fostered the vocation; took her to the Canon's house (presbytery). A "sharp and decisive 'No'" cut short her prepared speech. Canon at parting: "However, I am only the delegate of Monseigneur. If he allows you to enter, I shall have no more to say."
p165 They left the presbytery "in torrents of rain". M. Martin at once offered to take her to Bayeux.

## Bayeux: Mgr Hugonin and Abbé Révérony, 31 Oct 1887
p165 Mgr Hugonin, Bishop of Bayeux: "gentle and benevolent", studious, "already advanced in years"; left part of the administration to his Vicars-General.
p165 Abbé Révérony (OCR "Reverony"): successively parish priest of Vaucelles de Caen, military chaplain, curé of Saint-Pierre, Caen, Vicar-General of Bayeux; reputation as apostle and director; refused the episcopate; Mgr Hugonin rarely took an important step without consulting him. Source: "See the Vie de l'Abbé J. M. Reverony, Vicaire-Générale de Bayeux. (Anonymous.) La Chapelle Montligeon, 1900."
p165 Révérony fixed October 31, 1887 as date of M. Martin's interview with the Bishop. First time Thérèse visited anyone without her sisters.
p166 To not look a child, she put up her hair, which until then fell on her shoulders "in natural curls".
p166 Thérèse's account: "The Abbé Reverony was very friendly, although he looked slightly surprised. Noticing the tears in my eyes, he said to me: 'Ah, I see diamonds. You must not show these to Monseigneur.'" They "passed through large state-rooms, which made me feel as insignificant as a tiny ant". Monseigneur was walking in a corridor with two priests; the Vicar-General brought him to their room: "three enormous armchairs were ranged in front of a brightly burning fire."
p166 Papa knelt with her for the blessing. Révérony offered her the central armchair; she declined; he insisted, "telling me to show that I was capable of obedience"; she sat "buried in a monumental seat where four girls like me might sit at ease"; he took an ordinary chair.
p166-167 Papa told her to explain the purpose. Bishop asked if she had long desired Carmel. "Oh yes, Monseigneur ... a very long time." Révérony, smiling: "certainly not as long as fifteen years." Thérèse: "That is true ... but it is not much less, for from the age of three I have desired to give myself to the good God."
p167 Bishop, thinking to express the father's wishes, said she should stay home longer; M. Martin took her side, adding they were going to Rome on the diocesan pilgrimage and she would speak to the Holy Father if not permitted before then.
p167 Bishop said he must discuss with the Superior (Delatroëtte) first. Thérèse, "disregarding M. Reverony's warning, I did more than show my diamonds to Monseigneur, I gave them—shedding tears." He caressed her ("a favour that no other child had ever, it appears, received from him"). Bishop: "All is not lost, little one ... but I am pleased that you are going to Rome with your father; you will thus confirm your vocation. Instead of weeping you should rejoice. Besides, I am going to Lisieux next week; I shall speak to the Superior about you. You shall certainly receive my reply in Italy." He took them into the garden. Source: "Histoire d'une Ame, ch. v. pp. 89-91."
p167 "The audience was finished, and Thérèse had, alas, accomplished nothing."
p168 Bishop and Vicar-General thought the step "inopportune and unusual"; apparently decided to withhold approval until further examination [Laveille's inference]. Last resource: petition in person to the Pope.

## The 1887 pilgrimage: organisation
p168 Context: recent spoliation of Italian convents; Pope a virtual prisoner; bishops led pilgrimages in protest and consolation.
p168 Mgr Germain, Bishop of Coutances, strong protester against the Italian Government, "apparently" initiated the pilgrimage; avowed object: honouring the sacerdotal jubilee of Leo XIII.
p168 Abbé (later Mgr) Legoux, Vicar-General of Coutances, organizer of pilgrimages, "active disposition and ultramontane tendencies", title of Pope's chamberlain; recruited clergy and wealthy families of the region.
p169 Journey made both an act of loyalty and "a magnificent pleasure trip". Programme published in Bayeux diocese attracted many families; Mgr Hugonin sent Vicar-General M. Révérony at their head to represent the diocese.
p169 Money/logistics: organized by the "Agence Lubin", which "for an agreed sum payable in advance" provided the railway journey, hotel accommodation and meals, and sight-seeing tours by vehicle in principal towns. (Sum not stated.)
p169 M. Martin loved travel; this let him see Europe's sights a second time.
p169 November 4 (1887): M. Martin left Lisieux with Céline and Thérèse for Paris, where all pilgrims were to meet.
p169-170 Carriages "decorated with religious emblems"; "the flower of the Normandy nobility"; pilgrims displayed titles. Thérèse: "Far from dazzling us, ... all these titles of nobility appeared to our eyes as smoke and vanity. I recalled these words of the Imitation: 'Pursue not the shadow of a great name.' I understood that true greatness is not to be found in a name but in the soul." Source: "Imit., b. iii, ch. xxiv, 2." (for the Imitation quote).
p170 Thérèse on priests: "For the past month I have met many holy priests, and I have seen that if their sublime dignity has raised them above the angels, they are, as men, still subject to human weakness and frailty. If, then, holy priests whom Jesus in the Gospel calls the salt of the earth, show that they have need of prayer, what of those who are tepid?" Source: "Histoire d'une Ame, ch. vi, p. 95." Led her to rank prayer for priests first among Carmel's duties.

## Paris, 4-7 Nov 1887
p170 November 7 fixed for the pilgrims' gathering in the Basilica of the Sacred Heart, Montmartre. Before that M. Martin showed his daughters the main monuments of Paris.
p170-171 Our Lady of Victories church, Paris: Thérèse at the foot of the "white statue"; Our Lady made her understand "that it was in very truth she who had smiled on her and cured her"; this ended the doubts caused by Carmelites' questioning (see p131).
p171 She feared Italian pagan (or pagan-inspired) art would endanger her modesty; "Having no knowledge of evil, she dreaded to discover it"; asked the Virgin and St Joseph ("the father and protector of virgins") to guard her; felt heard.
p171 Consecration of pilgrims to the Sacred Heart at Montmartre; then departure for Switzerland.

## Train; St Martin; the "pharisees" insult
p171-172 Each train compartment placed under a saint's patronage (patron of presiding priest or his parish). Mgr Legoux, having noticed the fervour of the three Lisieux pilgrims, loudly announced their patron as St Martin; M. Martin smiled and thanked him.
p172 Crossing the plains of Champagne M. Martin hoped for hymns, but card-playing took more hours than prayer, even in his compartment. Asked to play, he declined, saying it would be better to give more time to devotions. One vexed passenger cried: "Fortunately, pharisees are rare." (checked image) M. Martin said nothing, pretended not to hear, and soon found a chance to shake hands with the man "so utterly wanting in respect for his grey hairs."

## Switzerland
p172 First halt: Bâle (Basel), arrived at nightfall; could not visit; after a few hours at the station left in the middle of the night for Lucerne.
p172 Lake of the Four Cantons; Pilatus and Righi with snow-capped summits; universal cry of admiration.
p173 Thérèse: "What good it did my soul to see those beauties of nature spread out thus in profusion! How they raised it to Him who is pleased to scatter His wondrous works over this land of exile which lasts but for a day." Source: "Histoire d'une Ame, ch. vi, p. 97."
p173 From Lucerne the railway followed the Reuss. Thérèse: "At times we were carried up nearly to the summit of a mountain. Below the awful precipices seemed ready to engulf us in their unfathomable depths. Or again, we would pass through a charming hamlet with its chalets and its graceful belfry ... Then, an immense lake with calm and crystal waters mingling their azure tint with the gold of the setting sun. ... I had there a foretaste of the wonders of heaven." Source: "Ibid." (HdA ch. vi, p. 97).
p173 Thérèse: "Later on, in the hour of trial when, a prisoner in the Carmel, I shall be able to see only a little corner of the heavens, I will recall this scene, and the remembrance will give me courage. ... I will love Him alone, and shall not be so unfortunate as to become attached to trifles". Source: "Ibid."
p173-174 Through the Saint-Gothard tunnel into Lombardy (summer greenery compared to leafless Normandy). Passed Lugano and Como (lake in moonlight).

## Milan
p174 Milan reached at nightfall. Next day: the Duomo (holds body of St Charles Borromeo); M. Martin and daughters attended early Mass celebrated by Mgr Germain before the shrine.
p174 Thérèse and Céline climbed dome and turret to the top, until people below looked "as small as ants."
p174-175 Campo Santo (monumental cemetery) of Milan. Thérèse: "What masterpieces! Here is a little child strewing flowers on its father's tomb; the lifeless marble is forgotten; the delicate petals seem to slip from the fingers. Here the light veil of the widow, and ribbons woven through young maidens' hair, seem to float and shimmer in the breeze."
p175 An old gentleman pilgrim grumbled at everything and could not endure enthusiasm; Thérèse "frankly declared" he had better have stayed home, but did not complain. Thérèse: "What an interesting study ... is the world to one who is on the eve of leaving it." Source: "Histoire d'une Ame, ch. vi, p. 98."

## Venice, Padua, Bologna
p175 Next day by train to Venice; arrived at nightfall about ten o'clock; taken by gondolier in darkness through canals to the pre-booked hotel; "lugubrious" arrival [description largely Laveille's own experience]. Thérèse: "This city ... has many charms, but it is melancholy."
p176 Venice sights: palace of the Doges, St Mark's, museums, Grand Canal; underground prisons/dungeons inspired her "with horror and pity".
p176 Footnote (IMPORTANT): at Venice Thérèse Martin stayed at the Hotel della Luna, "where the author of this book, a member of the same pilgrimage, also stayed. Without knowing her, he probably passed her often in the corridors or on the stairs." (Laveille was himself a pilgrim.)
p176 Padua: cathedral (sic - Basilica of St Anthony); venerated relic of St Anthony, then admired Donatello's sculptures on the tomb.
p176 Bologna: skipped details of San Petronio and Francia's works; hurried to church of St Catherine (of Bologna), "little Collettine of the fifteenth century" [i.e. Poor Clare of Colettine reform]; body with imprint of the Infant's kiss on its face; limbs "pliant still", blood liquid after 400 years; she had been a lady of honour at the Ferrara court.
p176 Footnote: "She, however, confided later to one of her sisters that the embalmed body of the saint was for her a not very attractive sight." (checked page image)

## Loreto
p176-177 Loreto "a real joy to her". Route along the Adriatic ("little triangular-sailed fishing barques"), plains with vines on elms and cherry-trees. Loreto a simple village without a fine house or comfortable hotel; Thérèse said this was its charm.
p177 Santa Casa (Holy House) inside a basilica; she touched with her rosary "the bowl of the Infant Jesus" (little wooden vessel). At the altar inside the Santa Casa, Thérèse and Céline "by a holy daring" succeeded in receiving Holy Communion.
p177 Some hours later train left for Rome through Umbria; fine autumn weather.

## Rome: arrival, hotel, Révérony watching
p178 Arrived at night; Thérèse asleep in a corner of the carriage when porters cried "Roma, Roma!"
p178 Martins lodged at the Hotel du Sud, Via Capo le Case (still exists; memorial tablet with relief of the saint set up there May 17, 1925, date of canonization - footnote). Mgr Germain, Mgr Legoux and most Coutances pilgrims at the Hotel Minerva.
p178 Abbé Révérony observed Thérèse's actions and demeanour ("An inquisitorial eye") since Normandy and at every halt; she was a little embarrassed, foresaw closer watch at the Vatican.
p178 Pilgrims divided into five groups for visits; Martins' group began with the Campagna sites.

## Coliseum escapade
p179 Thérèse and Céline wanted to kiss the arena ground and gather dust stained with martyrs' blood. Original soil buried at least 25 feet; one excavated part, a gaping hole protected by "a strong barrier". Despite the barricade Thérèse leaned over: "Come, follow me ... we shall be able to get down."
p179 Thérèse: "We set ourselves immediately to the attempt, climbing over ruins which crumbled beneath our feet, while papa, astonished at our daring, called out to us from the distance. But we no longer heard anything." Céline, who had listened to the guide, found the stone marked with a cross indicating where martyrs fought; they knelt; "My heart throbbed with emotion as I touched with my lips the dust empurpled with the blood of the early Christians."
p179-180 Thérèse at the Coliseum: "I asked the grace that I, too, might be a martyr for Jesus, and I felt deep down in my heart that my prayer was heard. All this occupied but a few moments. Having collected some small stones, we returned towards the walls to resume the perilous part of our enterprise. Papa, seeing us so delighted, could not chide us; I even saw that he was proud of our courage." Source: "Histoire d'une Ame, ch. vi, p. 102."

## Catacombs; St Cecilia; St Agnes
p180 Catacombs of St Calixtus, guided by a Trappist of Norman origin, Père Marie-Bernard; galleries lit by a torch. Thérèse and Céline lay in turn in the stone loculus where St Cecilia's body was found (footnote: the reproduction of Maderna's statue was not yet placed there then).
p180 Thérèse: St Cecilia "became ... my saint of predilection, my intimate confidante. What above all gained my love was her perfect abandonment to God, her unlimited confidence, which enabled her to convert to virginity souls who had never a thought before but to seek their joys in the things of earth." Source: "Histoire d'une Ame, ch. vi, p. 103."
p180 At the tomb of St Agnes she picked up and took a small fragment of ancient mosaic as a souvenir for Pauline (Sœur Agnès de Jésus).

## Rome sightseeing
p181 Wager/plan to see Rome's most famous wonders in six days; hurried by guides with little time for prayer; constant rain, "leaden sky". Basilicas incl. St Peter's, Scala Santa, Mamertine prison, museums, galleries: she does not mention them in her autobiography (footnote: except Holy Cross of Jerusalem).
p181 Thérèse at Santa Croce in Gerusalemme: "we venerated several fragments of the true Cross, two of the thorns, and one of the sacred nails. In order to examine them more at leisure, I managed to remain last, and as the religious who had charge of the precious treasures was preparing to replace them on the altar, I asked him if I might touch them. He replied in the affirmative, though appearing doubtful that I should succeed. I then put my little finger through an opening in the reliquary, and was thus able to touch one of the precious nails ... I acted towards Him, as can be seen, like a child who believes that everything is allowed her, and who regards as her own the treasures of her Father." Source: "Histoire d'une Ame, ch. vi, p. 110."

## Papal audience, 20 Nov 1887 (Sunday)
p182 Audience fixed for November 20. Early morning, Agency carriages collected groups at hotels in continuous downpour. 7.30 a.m.: all assembled under Mgr Germain and Abbé Révérony, with the Bishops of Nantes, Séez and Vannes (OCR "Vanne"), in a vast hall hung with red damask, with a very simple altar at one end.
p182 Exactly 8 a.m. Leo XIII appeared: flowing red mantle over white woollen cassock; silent blessing; knelt to prepare; "deathly pale countenance", eyelids lowered over dark eyes; trembling hands leaning on the arms of his chaplains to ascend the altar; Latin in slow measured voice, Italian accent. Then a Mass of thanksgiving by a chaplain. [Descriptions probably Laveille's own eyewitness memory; he was present.]
p183 Audience in the Audience Chamber: Pope seated on an ordinary armchair raised on one or two steps, wearing soutane and camail; took each pilgrim's hand, a few words, blessing, gave a commemoration medal. Mgr Germain presented each of his (Coutances) flock by name and rank.
p183 Footnote: "The writer of these lines was introduced by Mgr Germain as the author of a Manual on Thomistic Philosophy, and the Pope of the Encyclical Aeterni Patris deigned to address to him the most encouraging congratulations." (Laveille present at the same audience.)
p183 Bayeux pilgrims came next; Mgr Germain did not present them; M. Révérony watched formalities. Over a hundred people present. When he saw the two sisters approach "with calm and resolute faces beneath their black mantillas", he "announced in a loud voice that he absolutely forbade anyone to speak to the Holy Father."
p183-184 Thérèse looked to Céline; journey of 500 leagues at stake. Céline: "Speak!"
p184 Thérèse kissed the Pope's foot, grasped his hand, eyes in tears: "Most Holy Father, I have a great favour to ask." The Pope bent his head until it touched her mantilla; "dark piercing eyes". Thérèse: "Most Holy Father, in honour of your Jubilee, permit me to enter Carmel at fifteen."
p184 Révérony, standing by the Pope: "Most Holy Father, this is a child who desires the life of Carmel, but the superiors are, at the moment, considering the question." Pope: "Well, my child, do whatever the superiors shall decide." Thérèse, hands joined resting on the Pope's knees: "O Holy Father, if you only said yes everyone else would agree." Leo XIII, looking fixedly and kindly, "in an earnest voice": "Well, my child, you shall enter if it be God's will."
p184 She began to renew her petition; two of the noble guard told her to rise; she stayed kneeling; "they took her by the arms, and M. Reverony had to come to their aid before she would relinquish her post at the knees of the Sovereign Pontiff." (No footnote citation for the audience dialogue.)
p185 M. Martin had received the blessing before her; met her coming out "bathed in tears, which she made no effort to dry". Torrents of rain.
p185 She had earlier offered herself to the Infant Jesus as His plaything, "His little ball"; accepted being tossed or pierced at His pleasure; interior peace despite disappointment.

## Brother Simeon
p185-186 Some days later M. Martin visited Brother Simeon, founder and Superior of St Joseph's college (Rome); met Abbé Révérony there and "gently reproached" him for not helping Thérèse; told Brother Simeon of her attempt. Brother Simeon: "That never happens in Italy." Révérony did not defend himself; believed he interpreted his Bishop's wishes [Laveille's interpretation].

## Naples, Pompeii
p186 Day after the audience (21 Nov): early start; passed Aquino (birthplace of St Thomas) and Monte Cassino; Pompeii in a rain shower, merciless guide; she wished to wander alone among ruins.
p186-187 Naples: Bay, Vesuvius; San Martino monastery (converted to museum). Thérèse: "I felt truly that joy is not to be found in the things that surround us; it resides in our inmost soul. We can possess joy in the depths of an obscure prison as well as in a royal palace." Source: "Histoire d'une Ame, ch. vi, p. 109."
p187 Coming down the hill from San Martino the horses bolted ("took the bit between their teeth and ran away"); she attributed safe arrival at the hotel (where all passed the night) to her guardian angel. Mentions "rich hotels" of Naples.
p187 Next day back at Rome; Martins eager to go home.

## Homeward journey
p187 Departure from Rome November 24 (1887), train at six o'clock in the morning for northern Italy. At an early stop Mgr Legoux came to the carriage door where only Céline and Thérèse remained: "Well, how is our little Carmelite?" She realized all pilgrims knew her secret; suffered from it; the revelation won her some "sympathetic" glances.
p187-188 Laveille (eyewitness): since leaving Rome he heard the story of "this young girl of Lisieux" told in his compartment "with curiosity, but also with unfeigned admiration"; wished to see her but never did; "he had lived for a whole month in her immediate vicinity."
p188 Past the lake of Perugia (Trasimene; "lac de Pérouse"); arrived one clear sunlit evening at the foot of Assisi.
p188 Assisi scene: in her eagerness at the convent of Assisi she mislaid some small articles of dress; searching for them she missed the carriage to the station. Only M. Révérony's carriage remained, already full. She had to ask him. He had a companion sit with the driver and placed her opposite him in the best seat. She felt "like a squirrel caught in a trap." He spoke continually of Carmel, promising to do all in his power to realize her desire to enter at fifteen.
p189 Thérèse: after so many disillusions "she had lost confidence in creatures, and could no longer rely on anyone but God alone."
p189 Florence: rain; while others visited the Uffizi and Pitti Palace, Thérèse went to the convent of St Magdalen of Pazzi; knelt at her tomb asking entry to Carmel. Only Thérèse's hand was small enough to pass through the grating round the body; she touched rosaries of pilgrims to the relics; this "noble office" made her "feel very proud".
p189 Short stops at Pisa and Genoa. Thérèse: "We returned to France ... by a splendid route. We now skirted along by the sea, and one day during a storm the railway ran so close to the water's edge that the waves seemed almost to reach us. Further on, we travelled through plains covered with orange-trees, olives, and graceful palms."
p190 Thérèse: "At evening-time the numerous seaports gleamed with brilliant lights ... Yet, without regret, I watched this fairy-like tableau vanish away; my heart aspired to greater things." Source: "Histoire d'une Ame, ch. vi, p. 111."
p190 Venerated Notre-Dame de la Garde at Marseilles and Notre-Dame de Fourvière at Lyons; arrived Paris December 2 (1887); pilgrimage ended.
p190 M. Martin offered, "probably during the journey from Paris to Lisieux", to take her on a pilgrimage to Jerusalem; she declined; wanted a cell. [Laveille's motive guess: to console her or shorten the wait.]

## Waiting for Carmel (Dec 1887 - Lent 1888)
p190 Still hoping to enter on December 25. At Lisieux she went at once to the Carmel; Sœur Agnès de Jésus (Pauline) reminded her the Bishop had promised a written reply; advised her to write to the Bishop asking to enter at Christmas. She wrote; Mgr Hugonin remained silent.
p190-191 January 1, 1888: letter from the Prioress, Mère Marie de Gonzague: the Bishop had written to the monastery on December 28 (1887) sanctioning her immediate entrance, but the Prioress thought it better to defer entry until after Lent.
p191 Thérèse later learned the delay was to soothe M. Delatroëtte, who still opposed, and to spare the postulant a Carmelite Lent at the start. Decision "at first ... extremely painful to Thérèse". She used the months to practise self-forgetting and austere novitiate practices in advance. (Actual entry date falls after PDF p.191, outside this range.)
