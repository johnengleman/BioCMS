# Notes: "The Foundation of the Carmel of Lisieux and its Foundress, Reverend Mother Geneviève of St Teresa" (English, 1913)
# Page tag = PDF page. Quotes copied exactly. [comment] = author's commentary.

p1 Blank.
p2 FOREWORD: Thérèse, in Chapter VIII of her Life, speaks of "the happiness of having lived for several years with a Saint whose holiness was imitable, because it lay in the practice of ordinary and hidden virtues." That "Saint" = Reverend Mother Geneviève of Saint Teresa. Book based on documents kept in the monastery and verbal testimony of people who knew the Foundress; written in response to admirers of Sister Teresa.
p3 Title: The Carmel of Lisieux.
p4 BACKGROUND: Carmel of Pont-Audemer (Eure), suppressed at the Revolution, re-established April 1803; to get Government authorisation it had to open a boarding school (against the Constitutions). Among its pupils: two sisters named Gosselin (OCR "Gosselm"), strongly drawn to Carmel; delicate health barred them as postulants; they decided to spend their fortune founding a convent where they could live as benefactresses under special vows.
p4 Bishop Dancel of Bayeux and Lisieux formally approved on December 16, 1835, and chose Lisieux. He died soon after; successor Bishop Robin, a friend of the Gosselin family, appointed as Superior Father Peter (Pierre) Sauvage, first curate of the parish of Saint James (Saint-Jacques) in Lisieux. He knocked in vain at many Carmels.
p4 On advice of Bishop de Beauregard of Orleans, Sauvage applied to the Carmel of Poitiers, after a pilgrimage to Our Lady of Grace. February 21, 1837: letter from the Prioress of Poitiers (Reverend Mother Pauline) promising nuns, asking that the Misses Gosselin and companions first make their noviceship at Poitiers.
p4-5 April 14, 1837: she received them and gave the habit with names of the four first Carmelites of St Teresa's Reform: elder Gosselin = Teresa of Saint Joseph; younger = Mary of the Cross; others = Antoinette of the Holy Ghost and Ursula of the Saints.
p5 1838, before vows, they returned to Lisieux with two professed nuns of Poitiers: Sister Elizabeth of Saint Louis, appointed Prioress; Sister Geneviève of Saint Teresa, Sub-Prioress and Mistress of Novices. Travelled by diligence; time of political unrest; refused secular dress; the two professed crossed Paris in long black veils; no one insulted them.
p5 SCENE: arrival at nightfall in pouring rain, after a long journey "in a waggon covered with an awning, and driven postilion-wise by a friendly man who had a great respect for Carmelite poverty!" Benefactress Madame Le Boucher, warned by a scrupulous friend not to break the great silence kept after 8 p.m., received them into her house and "left them the whole evening without speaking a single word to them!" [comment: recalls scenes in St Teresa's Foundations.]
p6 Arrival date: March 16, 1838. Mme Le Boucher lent part of her house free: a thatched house in the Chaussée de Beuvilliers. Mother Geneviève wrote later they found "the poverty of Bethlehem, such as they had pictured it in their meditations."
p6 ROOMS: no cells for novices; two garrets joined by removing the connecting door = dormitory. First floor: one room fitted as chapel, adjoining room as choir. Another room eighteen feet square at the end of a narrow passage partitioned into three: kitchen, refectory-and-recreation room, cell of the Sub-Prioress (Geneviève), who had to go in and out carefully "to avoid upsetting the stove or breaking the plates and dishes." Refectory so tiny they could barely sit. A box = sideboard; a large firescreen in a corner = larder; paved floor = serving table. So little crockery "the plates had to be washed in the middle of dinner before the meal could be finished." A small room = Prioress's cell. No parlour: a grated door in the middle of the passage served for visitors and as turn (but the passage was the only way to the attic stairs).
p7 Courtyard and garden in full view of neighbours; nuns rarely went out. Townspeople unenthusiastic; ill-natured reports reached Father Nicholas Sauvage, Vicar of Saint James's and uncle of the Superior; so when saying Mass in the little chapel he spoke in a whisper "like the priests of the first ages of the Church in the depths of the Catacombs." The nuns sang the Office on feast days without lowering their voices. Blessed Sacrament reserved from the first evening. Inconveniences gave "much merriment at recreation".
p8 After five months a large old house in the rue de Livarot was bought (on site of the present turn-Sisters' dwelling). Prioress, Sub-Prioress and Sister Teresa of Saint Joseph (one of the most valuable subjects; later Prioress for a term) planned the conversion. Mother Elizabeth's talent for organisation had influenced her choice as foundress.
p8 Father Sauvage set up an altar of repose in a corridor of the new house for the Blessed Sacrament procession (Corpus Christi). Kneeling before the picture of Saint Teresa over it, a young girl (later Sister Adelaide of Providence) "saw the canvas become animate, and heard the words, 'It is here you will become a nun.'" The Carmel still receives the Corpus Christi procession yearly.
p8 August 24 (1838): Bishop Robin blessed the house before completion; date by chance = anniversary of St Teresa's founding of the first convent of the Reform (St Joseph's, Avila). Many came, from curiosity; civic authorities regarded the foundation as useless.
p9 Preachers at clothings and professions tried to dispel prejudice. Bishop Robin said the first Mass in the temporary chapel (used till 1852). Carmel dedicated to Mary Immaculate; later also to the Sacred Heart of Jesus.
p9 September 5, 1838: nuns moved into rue de Livarot; enclosure regularly established. They left the widow (Mme Le Boucher) with regret; she had been kind after the first blunder.
p9 New house not a real monastery: part of the loft divided to make the required twelve cells; no sleeping room for turn-Sisters (seculars at first), so one postulant had to leave the enclosure every evening to shut the outside gate; no contact with outside at night in illness or alarm. First floor: several cells, chapter room, infirmary. Kitchen and refectory on the second floor; the fatigue "laid the seeds of a malady" from which the poor lay Sister eventually died.
p9 September 10, 1838: the two Foundresses (Gosselin sisters) and Sister Antoinette of the Holy Ghost, renamed Sister John of the Cross, made their vows.
p10 Right after arrival received: white-veil (lay-sister) postulant Sister Radegunda of the Heart of Jesus; choir postulant Sister Saint Joseph. Then three more: a widow who became Sister Louise of Jesus; Sister Adelaide of Providence; Sister Aimée of Jesus (later Prioress of Lisieux for three years, then Prioress-foundress of the Carmel of Coutances). By 1840 eight religious professed under Rev. Mother Elizabeth of Saint Louis.
p11 POVERTY/MONEY: convent grew by buying neighbouring houses when for sale; the foundresses' fortune not large; first subjects brought little or no dowry. Father Sauvage made collections in person and by circular letters; helped by Father Gauthier, Chaplain of the College of Lisieux, "among the first benefactors of the Carmel". Collections bore so little fruit that Father Gauthier gave them up: one year, after two months of vacation begging town to town, he came back "with the paltry sum of £8."
p11 Sauvage refused a loan to buy a neighbouring property (which would have allowed the plan of the Ceremonial = book of Carmelite customs with building regulations); [comment: the Pope later forbade religious communities to borrow].
p11 Father Sauvage, in his book on the foundation: "It seemed ... as if Our Lord had taken on Himself the charge of providing for the temporal needs of His spouses, but He disposed things in such a way as to lead them to an absolute dependence on Himself. ... They did not, like the Poor Clares, ring their bell as a signal of distress, but He Who cares for the birds of the air would send, just at the right moment, an unexpected alms which enabled them to go to market and procure their daily food."
p12 More than once dinner was only fried leeks (compare the vine leaves of St Teresa's first companions). One postulant's clothing was indefinitely postponed because there was no money to buy cloth for her habit.
p12 BUILDINGS: regular Convent hardly begun when Mother Elizabeth died; completed only in 1877, forty years after work began. First large wing built 1858 under Prioress Rev. Mother Aimée of Jesus, with Mother Teresa of Saint Joseph as architect; sacristies same date. Chapel: first stone laid June 10(?), 1845 (source prints "June loth"); solemnly blessed September 6, 1852; new nuns' choir added about the same time. 1876: Rev. Mother Mary Gonzaga (Marie de Gonzague; "the religious who later was to open the doors of the Carmel to Sister Teresa") built the second wing, containing the infirmary and the cell of the Servant of God; also erected the Calvary in the quadrangle and built the Oratory of the Sacred Heart with the fourth cloister, completing the Convent.
p12 The present parlours date from after Thérèse's entrance and even after her clothing; "the Servant of God never went into them, as many pilgrims to the Carmel suppose."
p13 By 1841 the Community was definitely established, in very straitened circumstances.
p13 Mother Elizabeth of Saint Louis: early in religious life a pulmonary disease threatened to be mortal; miraculously cured by the saintly Archbishop d'Aviau of Bordeaux ("who later was to baptise the father of the Little Flower" — i.e. Louis Martin, born Bordeaux 1823). He visited his sister at the Carmel of Poitiers; Bishop de Beauregard, with him, pointed out Sister Elizabeth; d'Aviau smiling: "Shall we give her the days of Ezechias?"; blessed her; complete cure.
p13 Her noviciate began "on the morrow of the Revolution" in troubled times; stern character, "a certain brusqueness of manner", redeemed by kindness, regularity, courage, zeal, trust in Providence. Rev. Mother Pauline of Poitiers on her death: "She had all the qualities necessary for the success of a foundation". She died January 1842 (day not given), aged sixty-five, after three and a half years as Prioress.
p13-14 Successor: Mother Geneviève of Saint Teresa, "in things spiritual the real Foundress and Mother of the Carmel of Lisieux". Bishop de Beauregard had foretold: "You are going to Lisieux, not to build a house of material stones, but to raise in Gods honour an edifice of living stones, of souls who are vowed to Him."

## Reverend Mother Geneviève of Saint Teresa (1805-1891)
p16 Clare Mary Radegunda Bertrand born at Poitiers July 19, 1805. Father: business agent in comfortable circumstances, upright, good Christian; mother exceedingly pious. Clare eldest; two younger brothers.
p16 Even temper; rarely cried; at two a serious illness, never fretful. At school very young, harshly and unjustly treated by her mistress, who incited other children to petty persecutions; she kept silent for years until her First Communion day, when it was discovered. Mother gently reproached her. Clare: "Oh, mother, how could I say anything unkind of my mistress? It was much better for me to bear it!"
p16 At barely three and a half she read perfectly; while others played she read apart; to a passing lady: "Yes, this is a very interesting book, for it is the Imitation of Our Lord Jesus Christ."
p17 Father de Beauregard (later Bishop of Orleans), a Confessor of the Faith, "denied the palm of martyrdom" among the priests deported to Cayenne (Revolution); prepared her for First Communion; wrote twenty years later: "From your earliest years I saw in you the makings of a Saint." Grounded her in humility. Scene: "My child, why all these curls? ... Do your hair more simply; it would look much better in bands. And, as for your dress, ask your mother to get you dark shades. You are not at all pretty, you are not rich, and you have not a pleasing manner, so whom are you hoping to captivate?"
p17 Clare was sixteen when her mother died (c. 1821); became mother to her brothers; kept them faithful to duty. Younger brother Jules especially devoted; she took him on calls; he said: "I only like being with you, my darling Clare!" At seven Jules unknowingly witnessed an extraordinary grace (p18).
p18 Grave financial difficulties; the home had to be sold. One evening M. Bertrand went out to arrange the sale; Clare, prostrate with grief, could not pray; hid tears from little Jules who was fondling her; opened a book at random on a little picture of Our Lord; saw the picture "grow luminous before her eyes"; understood "the true value of suffering ... and the nothingness of all that passes with time"; ready to beg her bread; tears of sweetness. Then a devoted cousin found M. Bertrand the post he wanted; the house was kept. [comment: author defends reality of her graces: calm imagination, sound judgement, humility.]
p19 Near her seventeenth year (c. 1822): in her room at morning prayer "suddenly the room seemed to disappear; I no longer knew where I was, nor whether or not I had a body. I seemed to be surrounded by a wonderful light ... Then I heard a voice so melodious that no earthly music can compare with it, and it sang these words, leaving a mysterious interval between them: 'To be the spouse of God what an honour is this! What a privilege!'" She came to herself "in floods of tears".
p19 She went at once to ask admission at the Carmel of Poitiers; her father had gone first and obtained a promise he would not be deprived of her. Prioress Mother Aimée of Jesus (of the d'Ulys family, known in Poitiers for benefactions): "You want to make a vow of obedience, do you not, my child? Very well, you are going to begin to practice it now by going home to your father. I will let you know when you may enter." Clare understood she must wait till her father's death.
p19-20 Father de Beauregard named Bishop of Orleans; she kept writing to him but took a director in Poitiers. To gain merit of obedience she asked her cousin Miss Theresa Bertrand (her counsellor since her mother's death) to choose; expected "some good old Canon of the Cathedral!"; the cousin chose Father de Rochemonteix, Superior of the Carmel, who tried her by continual reproofs and humiliations.
p20 SHAWL story: she wore a handsome shawl gracefully draped; felt God asked her to pin it simply: "But I refused Jesus the pin! ... How ungrateful I was!" Director: "My child, there is about you an air of fashion which I should like to see disappear." Clare: "Father, I really cannot dress more simply. Look, my dress is dark, and I have been given this shawl, so ought I not to wear it?" Years later Bishop de Beauregard wrote to her at Carmel: "Humble yourself, keep yourself very little; remember that you once wore a shawl which displeased your Divine Lord and me." She had told no one [comment: revealed by God].
p21 Several proposals of marriage; gentle, refined; somewhat cold manner but ardent nature; lively intelligence, common sense, childlike simplicity; though of humble position received in distinguished circles.
p21 SCENE: one Sunday with fashionable friends at Mass in a poor village church in a state of "misery and disorder bordering on sacrilege". Clare: "I am astonished you are not as grieved as I am, and I cannot understand how you could assist unmoved at Mass decked out in lace, and wearing beautiful hats, while Our Lord is worse lodged than a beggar in the streets, and worse clothed than one of your servants." The rich friends, confused, soon replaced "the poor barn" with a beautiful chapel.
p21 Loved the poor, who thought her "an angel come down from Heaven"; spent some months yearly in the country, teaching... (cont. p22).
p22 Taught catechism to village children. A poor woman: "Oh! Miss ... don't refuse to take charge of my daughter. I warn you she is dreadfully tiresome and half-witted, but that doesn't matter - you can give her sense; you only have to wish it!" Clare awakened some intelligence and piety in the child.
p22 Father saw her red eyes (she had wept for him): "What an unhappy man I am! I have made my daughter cry."
p22 A gloomy, surly uncle with whom she stayed, seeing her return from morning Mass: "If that were my daughter I would turn her out of the house!" She wept; her aunt advised going home; Clare refused so as not to reveal it and sow discord; the uncle was softened and later let his niece guide him spiritually.
p23 Father died when Clare was twenty-four (c. 1829). Her director then held her back, pretending not to believe in her vocation; this ended early 1830. March 26, 1830: admitted to the Carmel of Poitiers as Sister Geneviève of Saint Teresa.
p23 SCENE: she went at the appointed hour with her cousin (Miss Theresa Bertrand), almost flying; cousin could not keep up. An hour after the cloister doors closed, "an incident took place in her family, of so grave a nature" that it would have blocked her vocation; the cousin argued it was too late to go back and won that Clare be left in peace (incident not described).
p23 First words of Prioress Mother Aimée of Jesus to her: "My child, if you want to be holy and always happy remember this never let anyone know who pleases or displeases you, whom you find agreeable or disagreeable, what you like or dislike."
p23-24 Community included venerable nuns professed before the Terror who had suffered to keep their vows.
p24 In the world skilled at manual work; in Carmel "incapable of doing the most elementary needlework" ("the salutary dew of humiliation").
p24 CLOTHING at a threatening political time (fear of return of 1793); she declared she would joyfully follow her Sisters to prison and death; given the habit. [From dates: 1830 revolution.] Joy in regular life: "sometimes in her transports of joy she would kiss the walls of her cell."
p24-25 TRIAL: six months before profession, Father de Rochemonteix preached a retreat on three states of soul; she recognized herself in the soul in mortal sin; after Matins, in her cell, "a strange voice, which seemed to come from without, said to me, loudly and distinctly: 'You could have done it, and yet you would not!'" She held up her crucifix: "My God, forgive me! Forgive me! Here is my pledge." Whole night "in mortal agony". Morning confession; penance a novena of Communions; "I approached the Holy Table with a feeling that I was damned!" At the Host, tears and peace. Later she asked: "My Jesus, why did you allow such a storm? It was simply terrible!" Answer: "I allowed it through love, and to spare you ten years of Purgatory."
p25 Author links to Thérèse's saying: "The fire of love is more purifying than the fire of Purgatory."
p26 PROFESSION: July 22, 1831. No consolation; felt abandoned; begged God to accept her as a slave since He would not treat her as spouse. Asked for an office serving Sisters; made third infirmarian.
p26 Nursed a Sister "covered with offensive sores"; day and night in the polluted infirmary air. Doctor on the evening the patient died: "The Sister who has nursed this nun must be a Saint ... for without a special aid from God it would not have been possible for anyone to have stood for so long such a tainted atmosphere." Another patient: in high summer she dried linen daily before a roaring fire until "her face was literally scorched by the heat." Sisters thought her lack of appetite came from joy at her profession.
p26-27 Then a life of union founded on faith, not ecstasy. Uneventful till 1837. Death of Mother Aimée of Jesus grieved her. Cousin: "What is going to become of you now, my poor child? You will be so unhappy!" Geneviève: "If I had entered Carmel for the sake of our Mother ... I would without doubt be very miserable, but I came for God, whom I can never lose, and so my happiness cannot be taken from me."
p27 Sayings: "I have no attraction except for obedience." "In the matter of penances I only feel drawn towards those in general use in the Convent".
p27 As Prioress (later), consulted by Mother Mary Gonzaga (Marie de Gonzague) about extra penances for novices: "Be on your guard, Mother ... for that kind of thing without prudence and discretion is only vanity and food for self-love. Teach your children to break their own wills, to practice charity, and to keep their rule perfectly. This is the true penance which is always pleasing to God."
p27 LISIEUX DETAIL: despite her advice there was often excess in penance; "nettles were allowed to grow freely in the garden to serve as extra disciplines for the Community!" After seeing the results of such penances, "Sister Teresa tried to find a surer and more rapid method of obtaining sanctity." (Link to Thérèse's Little Way.)
p27-28 As Procuratrix (arranges meals, supervises kitchen) at Poitiers; "revenged" herself on a Sister who had hurt her by many little kindnesses; the nun threw herself at her feet: "I am conquered by your charity; please forgive me!" Geneviève late in life: "I did not act in that way in order to conquer myself, but through real affection, for after that Sister had caused me pain I loved her more than before."
p29 Still Procuratrix when the Lisieux foundation was announced; desired the greater poverty but kept silent: "If God wants me for this work, He will inspire my Superiors to send me." Rev. Mother Pauline (successor of Mother Aimée of Jesus) had decided to send her but only named her novice-mistress.
p29 Interior words during evening prayer: "My daughter, tomorrow you will be allowed to unite yourself to Me in Holy Communion; then the Prioress will send for you, and after making you read the sermon on obedience, which you have copied, she will name you Novice-Mistress." She feared it came from the Devil. Next morning told to go to Communion; Mother Pauline: "Take your note-book and read me the sermon on obedience which you have written out." She read trembling; then named mistress of the four novices.
p29-30 Letter of Bishop de Beauregard (Orleans) on her office — five rules: turn to God with confidence; make yourself pleasant to novices; be skilful in understanding souls and "above all, characters, for the character is the source of good as it is of evil"; great sweetness and unchanging patience; fidelity and humility. Key lines: "often tell them that God is not a fault-finder, but that He is ready to make excuses for souls which belong to Him." "make them begin by quite tiny sacrifices, above all interior sacrifices, but so small that they hardly count. Attack undue sensitiveness, touchiness, and self-love." "Teach them to despise temptations; to let the village dogs bark. Let nothing astonish you, let nothing discourage you; be your own consoler. Never speak of your novices except to the Superior, and to the Spiritual Father".
p30 Geneviève's favourite saying to novices: "God is no fault-finder." Novices marked by simplicity ("the stamp of Carmel").
p31 Mother Pauline named her Sub-Prioress for the foundation; Sister Elizabeth of Saint Louis Prioress; long fellow-workers, "consequently they got on together perfectly." Geneviève to Father Sauvage: "my heart and that of Sister Elizabeth are one, and what one thinks, the other has already thought." Both wanted perfect observance; poverty attracted them.
p31 Leaving Poitiers was heroic: native town, friends, relations, "more than thirty years of life" there; she wrote an account of the departure describing the Convent's tender charity and sorrow of parting.
p31-32 Bishop de Beauregard's prophetic letter (though he knew Elizabeth was named Prioress): "Read more than once the 20th chapter of the Imitation ... how can you keep solitude, for it seems better suited for an anchoress than for a Prioress who must be constantly accessible to all". Realised over four years later, after his death. Mother Pauline thought Geneviève incapable of being Prioress.
p32 Diligence passed through Orleans; Bishop de Beauregard saw his two spiritual daughters. At parting to Geneviève: "Child ... Do not fail to write the history of your foundation, and put in the pleasure which your visit has caused me. Gladly will I now say my Nunc Dimittis."
p33 INCOME: Bishop de Beauregard furnished them with everything needed to make altar breads; they lived at first by this, later adding manufacture of incense and church ornaments.
p33 Poitiers had lent the two nuns for three years only unless recall would injure the foundation; after three years Sauvage convinced Mother Pauline recall would ruin it. At Mother Elizabeth's death Sauvage feared Geneviève's recall; did not open a letter from Poitiers before the election; after she was elected Prioress he read it, and from then lost confidence in her temporal administration: "she could not have had so much as a lock mended without his leave." Lasted long. Eleven years later Sauvage died; going through his papers she found the mysterious letter (cont. p34).
p34 The letter (from Poitiers): "Do not name Genevieve Prioress, for she is not capable of filling such a difficult office." She was delighted: "Oh! if I am Prioress it is certainly only by the will of God." [comment: she had hidden her talents even from her Prioress.]
p34 Before the first election: customary three days of prayer with Blessed Sacrament exposed. First evening, Way of the Cross, second station, interior words: "Open your heart to your daughters, and I will open Mine to you."
p34 During her first term the present chapel was begun (built with alms raised by founder Sauvage; Ceremonial plan rejected as too costly but approximated). Land marshy and unstable: built on piles. 1847 building suspended for lack of funds. Interior decoration finished only in 1891 through Father Youf, the Chaplain.
p35 HOLY FACE: 1847 Mother Geneviève received from the Carmel of Tours an account of the Revelations on Reparation made to Sister Mary of Saint Peter (then living). "When I think ... that God has chosen the Carmel to do this for him!" Devotion to the Holy Face; through her communications to the Bishop the Archconfraternity of Reparation was established at Lisieux. She placed a picture of the veil of St Veronica in the Convent Chapel; a few weeks before her death Sister Adelaide of Providence had a vision of the Holy Face there. Author links to the wide circulation of the Holy Face picture painted by one of Thérèse's sisters (Céline).
p36 PRIORATES (as printed): first Priorate "from January 15th, 1842, to January 19th, 1848, as she was elected to a second term of office on January 16th, 1845." Sauvage obtained from Poitiers another three-year delay in case of re-election; she was re-elected unanimously. January 1848 she had to be deposed per Constitutions; renewed claims; another delay.
p36 ILLNESS: in 1849 "the malady from which she was to suffer for more than forty years declared itself in the form of internal tumour." Poitiers claimed the right to nurse her; Father Sauvage pleaded; she was given for ever to the Carmel of Lisieux. She wrote: "Now that the Cross is planted in the Carmel of Lisieux, how could I leave it?"
p36-37 SISTER MARY OF THE CROSS (younger Gosselin foundress) offered herself as victim to obtain the solemn definition of the Immaculate Conception; saw an immense luminous Cross; God revealed He accepted and showed her the coming suffering; she recoiled, then renewed the offering. Soon, with no personal or family history of it, "she went out of her mind". Fixed idea: she would never go to Heaven, having sacrificed it for Our Lady, and would live for ever in Limbo with unbaptised children. Would see no one, not even her sister, except the infirmarian and her "Little Mother Genevieve"; three times a day Mother Geneviève sat an hour with her. Never left her bed though not in physical pain. At first piercing cries heard outside; Geneviève prayed; promise that she would stop. One night after Matins Geneviève thought "Suppose she should scream!" — instantly a piercing shriek; nuns ran out; interior reproach: "Daughter of little faith! did I not tell you that she would not cry out any more?" Promise renewed and kept till death.
p37-38 The invalid kept before her a slip of paper with "Gentleness" written on it. Believed she could not receive the Sacraments.
p38 Illness lasted 33 years. SUPERIORS of the Carmel: founder Father Sauvage died 1853; replaced by Canon Cagnard, Rector of Saint Peter's Cathedral; from 1870 Father Delatroëtte, Rector of Saint James' Church. In the parlour near Sister Mary's end, Delatroëtte: "Do you mean to say you are going to let her die without the Sacraments? ... That is a dreadful thing for a Carmelite!" Geneviève: "Father ... she is in a most peaceful state; if I suggest her seeing a priest I should terrify her and gain nothing by it." At his insistence she went trembling; the sick nun started up: "How can you, who know everything, come to suggest such a thing to me? Go away. I never wish to see you again!" Geneviève could not stay with her to the end. After the death, weeping, she heard: "Do not weep ... she was not capable of agreeing to what was wanted of her, her mind was as if imprisoned.... Do not be anxious about her; she is with Me!"
p39 Geneviève: "You must not write anything about me when I am dead, but only send a simple circular to the Convents of the Order to ask for prayers. There is nothing else to say." Author cites Thérèse: "Many pages of this history will never be read on earth."
p39 Sister Mary of the Cross's trial lasted from 1849 till January 29, 1882 (her death).
p39 After the three-year term of Rev. Mother Teresa of Saint Joseph, Geneviève re-elected January 19, 1851. Interior words: "In the same way as I rest in my Father, the Holy Trinity will be your support." Prioress six successive years; out three years; again January 18, 1860 (preceding election January 19, 1857 — as printed; dates in book not fully consistent).
p40 1860 vote advanced 24 hours to the Feast of St Peter's Chair (Jan. 18). Interior words: "It is not without reason that I have changed the day. I did not confide my Church to Saint John, who was innocent, but to Saint Peter the sinner. I have taken the poor man from the dunghill, to place him with the princes of my people."
p40 CHARACTER: "the very embodiment of sweetness and simplicity, and of a peace which sorrow and care could never change." Thérèse's words: her love of God made her "soar above all created things." Sparing in signs of affection; greatest tenderness was to place her hands on daughters' heads, like the patriarchs blessing.
p40-41 Detachment: Bishop de Beauregard wrote her long letters but Elizabeth short ones; for the two years before his death Geneviève let Elizabeth alone correspond with him. On his deathbed he said: "Once I used to receive letters from Genevieve, which gave me great pleasure. Why does she no longer write to me?" Her reply when told: "He knows why now."
p41 Extreme meekness and kindness; used gentle persuasion; "at times she even erred on the side of conciliation". Features "not beautiful" but heavenly in expression; distinguished manner; imposing presence "in spite of her small stature". Chapter talks full of fire. Announced divine messages: "Our Lord has charged me to make this known to the Community. He wishes this from you, dear Sisters." Once assured all present they would meet in Heaven.
p41-42 Revelation of a deceased lay Sister suffering in Purgatory: "Pray for her, my children, for she is suffering terribly." Fault: in her last years she complained that, because of the house's poverty, she lacked remedies and comforts for her infirmities (instead of gratitude for vocation). Lesson: gratitude for vocation.
p42 THÉRÈSE AND GENEVIÈVE: "Sister Teresa did not find in Mother Genevieve the help which she might have hoped for in sounding the depths of the Divine Heart." Geneviève did not guess her destiny; "Her humility was even alarmed at different times by the boldness of Sister Teresa's aspirations, which she tried to check." Yet she "loved and esteemed her greatly" and gave the example of austere virtue.
p42 During Mr. Martin's illness: one day when his state caused his daughters "the most poignant anguish", Mother Geneviève, herself ill, sent for Sister Teresa and her sisters and calmed them by repeating words Our Lord had spoken to her, "which were verified on the following day."
p42-43 BUTTER MIRACLE: cook said the butter was finished. Geneviève: "My child, I haven't a farthing ... but if there is a little butter still left, go on using it, and let us put our trust in God." After two months Sister Madeleine: "Mother, I really can't understand it; my little piece of butter is always in the same state! ... I had only enough for two days, and now, however much I use, it never grows less." Geneviève: "Be at peace ... your little stock is on the point of being finished." Days later an alms came and the pot was empty.
p43 Timely sums of money received. APRICOT TREE: blossoms withered by hard frost; she prayed: "My Jesus, is it possible that you will not give our Sisters anything this summer to refresh them?" Interior voice: "Be not disturbed, there will be apricots." The tree bore so much fruit the leaves were hardly visible. WASHING DAY: bitter cold, community washing in the open air; while she prayed before the Tabernacle a thaw set in, like spring.
p43-44 [comment] Not a life of continual marvels: no ecstasies or visions; "a life of pure faith". On how Our Lord spoke to her: "I know that the voice I hear is the voice of a friend, but I know nothing else." At receiving novices' vows the mystery of Divine Espousals seemed revealed to her.
p45 In her last seven years, infirm, confined to armchair or bed. SCENE: a Prioress (one of her daughters), annoyed at advice that had bad results, came in while a young infirmarian read aloud: "Mother ... I beg of you not to interfere in the business of the house. You don't know what is going on, and your advice is worse than useless." Geneviève: "Thank you, dear Mother ... it is a great charity to tell me so. When one has been Prioress, one is inclined to interfere in matters that no longer concern one! It is quite true that I cannot see what is going on, and I may make foolish mistakes. Please, forgive me!" Then to the speechless infirmarian: "Well, dear, let us go on with our reading."
p45 GOLDEN JUBILEE: she dreaded having to appear in public without a veil (Carmelite custom). In her preparatory retreat Our Lord said: "Do not fear, daughter. I will not allow you to be distressed, for I ..." (cont. p46).
p46 (Jubilee cont.) "... I will give you on that day what I refused you fifty years ago on your Profession Day. There will appear even on your face some reflection of My workings in your soul." On the day her face shone with peace, "a youthfulness and beauty which it had never before possessed"; daughters felt indescribable happiness. She chose the sermon text: "The yoke of the Lord is sweet and His burden light." (Golden jubilee of profession = July 22, 1881, by dates.)
p47 ILLNESS: in 1884, "the second year of her last term as Prioress", she became a confirmed invalid; until then she kept the Rule though she had had "a serious tumour for more than thirty-five years." When the malady first declared itself she was told its nature. To a daughter: "At this news ... my heart was filled with delight. It was the Feast of Saint Andrew (Nov. 30), and, while reciting his Office, I kept repeating in a transport, 'O, good cross, so long desired ...'" "I looked on my soul as a rusty copper vase, and I thought that the suffering which was coming to me would be the kind hand which would rub the ugly, dull vase and make it bright and shining."
p47 Kept her illness secret while she could walk; daughters sometimes found her senseless on the ground. SCENE: one winter, with a severe headache, she went into the enclosure for a difficult act of charity, fainted and fell in the snow; Sister Adelaide, working in her cell, heard a voice: "Go into the garden!" and found her "frozen and almost lifeless."
p48 1884: serious swelling of the lower limbs made walking impossible; given a wheel-chair; went to Choir for Mass, then spent the day in a little room adjoining the Community room, present at recreations "of which she was the life and soul". Letter: "I have no hope of improvement; I am very well off on the Cross, blessed be God."
p48 Still kept the long Carmelite fast: for eight months of the year took her collation alone by her little lantern; mitigation of a few spoonfuls of soup, then bread with a little cheese: "My children, I must practise this small mortification, since I cannot share in the great ones which you do."
p48-49 Would have only the wooden bench of the Rule in her cell; later a wooden chair: "The harder it is, the better it suits me." Then a very hard, very high armchair, forcing her to sit on the edge. SCENE: while it was being mended she sat in an old soft easy-chair; the Prioress: "How are you going to manage all this time without it, Mother?" She said she could wait but let slip "an involuntary laugh, for she was very witty"; the young infirmarian guessed and told the Prioress, who did not believe it; Geneviève got back her hard armchair and kept it to the end.
p49 THÉRÈSE'S ENTRY: about this time, when the Father Superior came to the Community-room for a ceremony, Mother Geneviève said timidly: "Father, in honour of this Feast, I want to ask a favour of you." "Well, Mother, what is it?" "Oh, if you would only let little Teresa Martin enter!" Canon Delatroëtte turned away "in evident displeasure, with a peremptory refusal."
p50 January 1888 she nearly died. Before, to the Prioress: "Mother, why should I fear death? Our Lord has given me the grace never to judge anyone, and now I count on His promise, Judge not and you shall not be judged." In a meditation on Heaven she saw a luminous Cross: "The sight of this Cross makes the joy and glory of the elect." It announced a final combat of four years.
p50 Legs swollen, then mortification; beginning of 1890 gangrene. Still at daily Mass in her armchair; wheeled to the little grille for Communion. Holy Thursday 1890 during thanksgiving: "Henceforth, you will not come to Me, but I will come to you." Thereafter bedridden. Severe attack that evening during Tenebrae; next morning (Good Friday 1890) Last Sacraments. Symptoms: head encircled by painful swelling from violent sick headaches (like a crown of thorns); feet "horribly tumefied"; fever-parched lips repeating "I thirst"; gasping for breath.
p50-51 She had said: "I do not understand why there are Carmelites who offer themselves as victims to God. We are all victims by our consecration." Evening of Good Friday 1890, Reverend Mother Agnès of Jesus (Pauline, then "a humble Sister"), alone with her, saw her transfigured, eyes shut, radiant; she had heard: "My daughter, it is time to offer yourself as a victim."
p51 Gangrene disappeared days later but legs one large wound, deep broad cavities in the feet; before death "the heel and several toes of the left foot came away". Pain of internal tumour increased; could move only her arms; lost her sight from excruciating pain. Doctor suggested morphine; the Prioress, misinformed about its effects, refused it. Community doctor: "I defy any of my fellow-doctors to cite a case such as this; life could only be sustained by a miracle." He knelt, eyes in tears, asking her blessing; after extracting a tooth he asked to keep it as a relic.
p51-52 Her verses on her sufferings: "The light has left my darkened eyes. / But in my soul, my Lord, I see / The earthly beauty that men prize, / Can have no further charm for me." ... "All that I have and am is Thine, / With joy I give all back to Thee." ... "'From tortured head, to pierced foot / There is no soundness left in me.'"
p52 In unbearable pain: "My sweet Jesus, will you not help your poor Genevieve?" To visitors: "You know ... exterior wounds like mine cause more discomfort than actual pain." Anne of St Bartholomew on St Teresa applied to her: "The beauty of her soul was such that it made itself felt in everything."
p53 THÉRÈSE'S ACCOUNT (from her autobiography): "One Sunday, when I went to the infirmary to pay her my usual visit, I found two old Sisters by her side. I was retiring, discreetly when she called me, and said to me, as if inspired, 'Wait, my little one, I have only one word to say to you. You are always asking me for a spiritual bouquet. Well, today I will give you this one: "SERVE GOD IN PEACE AND IN JOY. REMEMBER, MY CHILD, THAT OUR GOD IS THE GOD OF PEACE."'" Thérèse had been "severely tried, almost to sadness ... I no longer felt certain that God loved me." Next Sunday Geneviève declared she had had no revelation. Thérèse: "that seems to me the most real and true saintliness; that is what I desire to attain to, for it is free from all illusion."
p53-54 Thérèse's eldest sister (Marie, Soeur Marie du Sacré-Coeur) was second infirmarian to Geneviève. Having missed her prayer through work, Geneviève told her Our Lord's words: "It is not the souls who spend all their time praying who are most pleasing to me, but those who prove their love by sacrifice and obedience."
p54 Same Sister resisted curiosity about the Prioress talking with nuns; on arriving Geneviève said: "I have a little secret to tell you, my child how to be always happy and to please Our Lord. Never try to find out what is going on. ... Have you not read in the lives of the Fathers of the Desert of a certain brother who was always in the moon and never did anything, and who was called in consequence 'Brother Fly'? Well, don't you imitate him, don't be Sister Fly." "I did not see you ... but Our Lord, no doubt, allows me to tell you this for your good."
p54 To a nun tempted on obedience: "As the bird spreads its wings and flies freely in the air, so the obedient soul takes her flight towards her Beloved, and she is like a Heaven, in which God finds his delight."
p54 An old Sister long doubted the salvation of her father (non-practising, sudden death). After a fault, Our Lord told Geneviève: "She will not humble herself! And yet I had mercy on her father for a single act of humility, because at the moment of death he cried to Me, 'Have pity on me.'"
p55 Dream the night before June 5, 1891 (Feast of the Sacred Heart): alone in a little bark on a boundless ocean; a youth of wondrous beauty handed her a note: "At full sail!" Next day: "I think, as today is the Feast of the Sacred Heart, that I am going full sail into the Heart of Jesus."
p55 July 22, 1891: sixtieth anniversary of her profession; interior martyrdom; evening: "O no, the greatest sufferings are nothing but not to see God!" Witnesses: "It was no longer Mother Genevieve ... but a soul in Purgatory." THÉRÈSE to her: "O Mother! you will not go to Purgatory." Geneviève: "I hope not!"
p55 "I do not desire to die so that I may suffer no more, but only to see my God!" Repeated St Teresa's dying words: "It is time, Lord, it is time! Take me to yourself."
p55-56 End of November 1891 worse; November 25: Extreme Unction for the third time. "Is not this losing patience? Ah; how unfortunate that would be! Pray, my children, that God may give me His Grace till the end!" About 6.30 p.m. to the Prioress: "Sixty-one years ago today my great pardon was granted to me, and perhaps this evening ... Heaven...!" (i.e. Nov. 25, 1830 — the night of terror/pardon before profession).
p56 Next morning Rev. Mother Gonzaga (Marie de Gonzague, Prioress) thought her better and might go to Mass. Geneviève: "My Jesus, as Reverend Mother finds me better, although I am still suffering so much, do show me what it is to be really better?" All pain vanished for half an hour ("as if she were in perfect health, and resting on a soft bed"), then returned.
p56 Pain unbearable from morning of December 4, 1891. "My sweet Jesus, have you abandoned me?" then a smile. Doctor in the afternoon: "Well, Mother ... you are always wanting me to tell you that you are going to die. The happy moment has now come." Night of agony — as Thérèse later said of herself, "pure suffering with no admixture of consolation." Towards morning, head supported by Mother Gonzaga: "Oh Jesus, I always said to You 'In te Domine speravi!'" Tears on closed eyelids. "O, how long my exile is!" "Jesus, Mary, Joseph, may I breathe forth my soul with you in peace! Virgin Mother help me!"
p56 DEATH: "Saturday was dawning when, at the first sound of the Angelus, the patient victim expired" (= Saturday December 5, 1891, by the dates given). All her daughters around her. Age 86 (born 1805).
p57 THÉRÈSE: "At the very moment of her birth in Heaven, I felt full of an unutterable joy and fervour ... for I am quite convinced that she went straight to Heaven." RELIC: Thérèse noticed a tear "sparkling like a beautiful diamond" in the dying Mother's eye: "This tear ... which was the last she was to shed on earth, did not fall, I still saw it shining when the mortal remains of Our Mother were exposed in the choir. Then taking a fine linen handkerchief, I dared to approach her in the evening without being noticed by anyone, and I now have the happiness of possessing a Saint's last tear."
p57 The nuns were allowed to keep her heart. Her remains rest in the chapel sanctuary near those of the founder (Father Sauvage, died 1853). Marble tablet: "From the heights of Heaven look down on us! See and visit the vine which your right hand has planted, and give it perfection." (Office of Saint Teresa).
p58 EPILOGUE — DAUGHTER HOUSES: Carmels of Saigon, Coutances and Caen. Saigon founded by Mother Geneviève "in tears", a source of anxiety and humiliation to her; its Prioress Rev. Mother Philomena of the Sacred Heart showed heroic courage; she was cousin and collaborator of Bishop Lefebvre (text "Lefebre"), Vicar Apostolic of Cochin China, who in prison for the faith awaiting martyrdom saw St Teresa, who announced his release and asked him "to establish her Order in Annam, because God would be greatly served and glorified there." All Carmels of the Far East were founded from Saigon under Mother Philomena, so look to Geneviève as Mother. (Coutances founded by Mother Aimée of Jesus as Prioress, p10.)

Geneviève, ill, sent for the chaplain Father Youf and asked him to write Sister Adelaide's life; their efforts came to nothing.
