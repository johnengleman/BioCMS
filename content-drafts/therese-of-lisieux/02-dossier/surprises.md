## The strongest surprises

Chosen from the parts' index lists. Each line gives the cards. Check the card before you use the fact.

1. The Church's own brief of 1923 says her family thought her childhood illness came from the devil. Attribute the belief to the family (F-OM-004, F-OM-005, F-OM-132).
2. Her father's words at Christmas 1886 are harsher in the manuscript than in the published book (F-OW-059; quotations OW-A-14 and OW-P-1).
3. She wrote Diana Vaughan into her play as a real convert and 'a new Joan of Arc', and her photograph was shown at Taxil's press conference. Pauline's account leaves this out (F-OW-572, F-OW-578, F-LF-226, F-EW-992).
4. She asked her sister not to believe what would be said about her after her death (F-OW-491).
5. She joked about the asylum where her father had been: 'the Bon-Sauveur will come and fetch you' (quotations OW-B-46).
6. The guards lifted her from the Pope's knees, and she climbed over a barrier in the Coliseum while her father was proud (F-LF-179, F-LF-176).
7. As a postulant she reported illness every day and risked dismissal (F-LF-192).
8. The community expected 'nothing to recount' at her death (F-LF-230), and a sister said she was not even a good nun (Q-EW-013).
9. Soldiers, not bishops, drove the dispensation; 26 petitions from 1916–18 (F-OM-135, F-OM-037).
10. The halos on the 1915 war drawings were added after beatification and canonization (F-OM-122, F-OM-135).
11. In 1910 the grave held only bones and a fresh palm (F-LF-249, F-OM-028). Her height from the bones was about 1.59–1.60 m (F-OM-116, F-OM-133).
12. A Roman official warned that the cause would be stopped if the publicity did not stop, according to de Teil (F-OM-107, F-OM-134).
13. The 1911 edition moved her farewell letters and changed why she was glad to die (F-OW-498).
14. She played Jesus in a play, 'Jesus at Bethany', on 29 July 1895 (F-OW-570).
15. Her sister Céline first doubted the perfumes, then smelled roses in Thérèse's own cell (F-EW-2486).
16. The Carmel's own modern definition of 'miracle' asks doctors not to declare one (F-OM-118, F-OM-136).

## Eyewitness: cards tagged 'A surprise'

### F-EW-2486 — Céline did not believe in the perfumes, then smelled roses in Thérèse's own cell
- Claim: Céline said that at the end of 1908 people began to speak of perfumes by which Thérèse made her presence known. She rebelled against this: she called it "coarse" because it pleased the senses, and concluded she did not believe in it. One evening near the end of Nov 1908, entering "our cell, which is the very one Sister Thérèse occupied", she found the oratory before the cell filled with a scent of roses. She stayed unbelieving ("now they perfume everything; it may be a letter soaked in scent"), though she knew there was none there, and left without checking. On the turn of the stairs, far from the cell, she felt "a breath" of roses. Then she believed and wanted to thank Thérèse, but it vanished at once.
- When / where: late Nov 1908, Lisieux Carmel
- People: Céline; Thérèse
- Source: PO 4, f. 410v, S34, W1
- New to English readers: yes
- Confidence: high that she testified it; single witness
- Notes: A surprise: a sister of Thérèse who was a sceptic about the perfumes. She shared Thérèse's cell in 1910.

### F-EW-2551 — TSA's dream of 8 Jan 1897: 'Sister Thérèse is called — she must be very beautiful'
- Claim: TSA dreamed on 8 Jan 1897 that she was in a very dark room closed by a heavy black door with a line of dazzling light under it. From the next room a voice said: 'Sister Thérèse of the Child Jesus is called!' She felt that beside her, in an even darker part, her little sister was being prepared. The voice insisted: 'She must be very beautiful!' She woke sure that Thérèse would soon leave for heaven. Some weeks later in recreation she told Thérèse. Thérèse was deeply moved and said God had sent the dream to encourage her through TSA: the dark room and black door were the image of her darkness; the line of light and the call were a consoling sign. Thérèse insisted on the words 'through you', with tears in her eyes, and told her of her trial of faith, which TSA had not known.
- When / where: 8 Jan 1897; Feb–Mar 1897, Lisieux Carmel
- People: TSA; Thérèse
- Source: Circular (obituary notice) of Sr Thérèse de Saint-Augustin (Julia Leroyer, 1856–1929), written by Mère Agnès de Jésus, dated 8 Sep 1929, https://archives.carmeldelisieux.fr/au-carmel-du-temps-de-therese/la-communaute/soeur-therese-de-st-augustin/circulaire-de-soeur-therese-de-st-augustin/ — circular, paragraphs 'Le Seigneur, qui devait se complaire…' and 'Le 8 janvier 1897…'
- New to English readers: yes
- Confidence: medium — TSA's own account, as retold by Agnès
- Notes: A surprise: TSA says she knew nothing of Thérèse's trial of faith until this talk. Thérèse's trial began at Easter 1896 (Ms C), so it had lasted about ten months. Compare F-EW-1131 (TSA's testimony on the trial of faith).

## Life-family: cards tagged 'Surprise'

### F-LF-128 — The vision of the veiled man
- Claim: Thérèse wrote that, while her father was away (at Alençon, on business, and not due back soon), she stood alone at a window over the kitchen garden at about 2–3 p.m. on a sunny day. By the wash-house she saw a man dressed like her father, of his height and gait, but "very bent and aged", with a thick veil over his head. He walked slowly past her little garden toward a clump of fir trees and did not come out again. She cried "Papa! Papa!" Marie, in the next room, was frightened and asked why she called for papa when he was at Alençon. The sisters suggested the maid with an apron over her head. Victoire said she had not left the kitchen. They searched behind the trees and found no one. Marie told her to think no more about it. Thérèse wrote that she understood the meaning only after fourteen years.
- When / where: 1879 or 1880; Les Buissonnets
- People: Thérèse; Marie; Pauline (in the next room, per Laveille); Victoire Pasquer; Louis (absent)
- Source: LAV p.115–116 (HA ch. ii p.32); Marie's preparatory notes (Carmel page: https://archives.carmeldelisieux.fr/au-carmel-du-temps-de-therese/la-communaute/soeur-marie-du-sacre-coeur/notes-preparatoires-de-soeur-marie-du-sacre-coeur/); HA 1898 text (local D1a, lines 2294–2345, checked by helper)
- New to English readers: partial (baseline lacks it)
- Confidence: high for the story as Thérèse and Marie tell it; medium for the year.
- Notes: CONFLICT on age: about six (Marie, Laveille) vs seven (Thérèse as quoted by Marie) — see F-LF-129. Setting is Les Buissonnets, not Alençon. Later readers linked the veil to Louis's illness (see F-LF-129). Listed as a surprise: the family searched behind the trees.

### F-LF-153 — The headache and "the Ass and the Dog"
- Claim: At Trouville cousin Marie Guérin, about fourteen, had a headache; her mother fondled her and got only tears. A few days later Thérèse, who had frequent headaches and never complained, sank on a chair in a corner of the sitting-room and cried. Asked why, she said, "I have a headache." No one sympathised. Her aunt reproved her gravely for playing the spoiled child. Jeanne, "slightly sarcastic", said Thérèse lacked confidence and simplicity and was probably tormented by a big scruple (Abbé Lepetit, *La Croix*, Nov 1913). Thérèse wrote that she understood the fable "The Ass and the Dog": she was the ass who put his hoof on the table to be kissed like the lap-dog, and the price "cured me for ever of the desire to attract attention" (HA ch. iv p.67).
- When / where: Sep 1885; Trouville
- People: Thérèse; Marie, Jeanne and Céline Guérin
- Source: LAV p.141–142 (Sr Joséphine de la Croix, Process of the Ordinary p.141; Lepetit article; HA)
- New to English readers: yes
- Confidence: high
- Notes: Surprise: her aunt scolded a child for a headache.

### F-LF-155 — Louis's journey to Germany, Austria, Constantinople and Italy, 1885
- Claim: At the wish of Abbé Marie, curate of Saint-Jacques, Lisieux, Louis toured Germany, Austria, Constantinople and Italy for about six weeks in Aug–Oct 1885 and wrote to his daughters. Dates from his letters: Paris 2 Aug; Munich 27 Aug; Vienna 30 Aug; Black Sea 7 Sep; Constantinople 11 and 16 Sep (Sultan, dervishes, Hagia Sophia, the bazaar; per family-letters notes); Naples 25 Sep (Pompeii); Rome 27 Sep; Milan 6 Oct. He gave pet names: Marie "my Diamond", Pauline "my fine Pearl", Céline "the Intrepid", Thérèse "ma Reine de France et de Navarre" and "ma petite Reine". From Vienna (30 Aug) he wrote he seemed to see them in the Belvedere and hear "Thérèse's sweet and sympathetic voice"; in the same letter he wrote: "Vanity of vanities, and all is vanity, except loving God and serving Him!" From Rome (27 Sep) he wrote to Marie: "St Peter's is for me the most beautiful thing in the world" and "how sad that the Holy Father (Leo XIII) is thus in captivity"; and, of Pauline, "Tell my 'Fine Pearl' that I am too happy, and that 'I offer my back', for this cannot last" ("je tends le dos"). From Milan (6 Oct): "Everything I see is splendid, but it is always an earthly beauty and our heart is satisfied with nothing until it sees the infinite beauty which is God… Soon the intimate pleasure of family; it is this beauty that brings us nearest."
- When / where: 2 Aug – 6 Oct 1885; Munich, Vienna, Constantinople, Naples, Rome, Milan
- People: Louis; Abbé Marie; Marie; Pauline; Céline; Thérèse
- Source: LAV p.142–144; family-letters notes (Louis's letters of 1885, Carmel archive; slugs in raw-notes/letters-text)
- New to English readers: yes
- Confidence: high
- Notes: Laveille asks if the Rome sentence was a presentiment; that is his comment. The dog Tom and the servant Félicité are named in the letters (see F-LF-111). Surprise: the "dear King" travelled to Constantinople.

### F-LF-165 — Pranzini's execution as *La Croix* reported it, 1 Sep 1887
- Claim: *La Croix* of 1 Sep 1887 reported the execution. Pranzini was handed to the executioner Deibler and his assistants, his hair cut and neck bared. It was "two minutes to five, while the birds sang in the trees around". He appeared with a "livid face", pushed away the priest and the executioners. The chaplain stood in front to hide the machine. Deibler pushed him onto the guillotine and an assistant held his head by the hair. "But before the final stroke… he asked for the chaplain's crucifix, and three times he kissed it." The report ends that if human justice is satisfied, perhaps this last kiss also satisfied Divine Justice "which demands, above all, repentance". Thérèse read it, was in tears and had to run away. She wrote that she had "the desired sign" and that the lips of "mon premier enfant" were pressed to the Divine Wounds (HA ch. v p.76–77).
- When / where: 31 Aug / 1 Sep 1887; Paris; Lisieux
- People: Pranzini; Deibler; the chaplain; Thérèse
- Source: LAV p.155–156 (La Croix, 1 Sep 1887)
- New to English readers: partial
- Confidence: high for the wording as Laveille prints it
- Notes: The date of the execution itself is not stated in the pages read; *La Croix* is dated 1 Sep 1887. Surprise: the report also says an assistant lifted the severed head "by one ear". Thérèse read it against her father's rule.

### F-LF-174 — Venice, Padua, Bologna, Loreto
- Claim: They reached Venice at about ten at night and were taken by gondola in the dark to the pre-booked hotel. Thérèse said the city "has many charms, but it is melancholy". She was struck with horror and pity at the underground prisons of the Doges' palace. Laveille states in a footnote that she stayed at the Hotel della Luna, "where the author of this book, a member of the same pilgrimage, also stayed. Without knowing her, he probably passed her often in the corridors or on the stairs." At Padua they saw the relic of St Anthony. At Bologna they hurried to the church of St Catherine, where the body shows the imprint of the Infant's kiss; Thérèse later told a sister that the embalmed body was "not very attractive". At Loreto she touched the "bowl of the Infant Jesus" with her rosary, and she and Céline, "by a holy daring", received Communion inside the Santa Casa (Sunday 13 Nov by the Carmel timetable).
- When / where: 9–13 Nov 1887; Venice, Padua, Bologna, Loreto
- People: Louis; Céline; Thérèse; Laveille (fellow pilgrim)
- Source: LAV p.175–177 and fn.
- New to English readers: yes
- Confidence: high
- Notes: Surprise: the future biographer stayed in the same hotel. Connection to other saints: Anthony of Padua, Catherine of Bologna.

### F-LF-176 — The Coliseum: over the barrier for martyrs' dust
- Claim: Thérèse and Céline wanted to kiss the arena ground and take dust stained with martyrs' blood. The original soil lay at least 25 feet down; one dug-out part was a gaping hole behind a strong barrier. Thérèse said, "Come, follow me… we shall be able to get down." They climbed over crumbling ruins while Louis, astonished, called from a distance, "but we no longer heard anything". Céline, who had listened to the guide, found the stone marked with a cross where the martyrs fought. They knelt. Thérèse wrote she asked "that I, too, might be a martyr for Jesus" and felt her prayer heard. They took small stones. Louis, seeing them delighted, "could not chide us"; she saw he was proud of their courage (HA ch. vi p.102).
- When / where: mid-Nov 1887; the Coliseum, Rome
- People: Thérèse; Céline; Louis
- Source: LAV p.179–180 (HA ch. vi p.102)
- New to English readers: yes
- Confidence: high
- Notes: Surprise: she climbed a barrier; Louis was proud, not angry.

### F-LF-179 — Audience with Leo XIII, Sunday 20 November 1887
- Claim: Early on 20 Nov, agency carriages fetched groups in a steady downpour. At 7.30 a.m. pilgrims assembled under Mgr Germain and Abbé Révérony, with the bishops of Nantes, Séez and Vannes, in a hall hung with red damask. At 8 a.m. Leo XIII entered in a red mantle over a white woollen cassock, deathly pale, with trembling hands, and said Mass slowly with an Italian accent. In the audience chamber he sat on an ordinary armchair on a low step, took each pilgrim's hand, said a few words and gave a medal. Laveille footnotes that Mgr Germain presented him as author of a manual of Thomistic philosophy and the Pope praised him. Bayeux pilgrims came next, presented by Révérony; over a hundred people were present. As the two sisters approached "with calm and resolute faces beneath their black mantillas", Révérony "announced in a loud voice that he absolutely forbade anyone to speak to the Holy Father". Thérèse looked at Céline, who said, "Speak!" Thérèse kissed the Pope's foot, took his hand, in tears: "Most Holy Father, I have a great favour to ask… in honour of your Jubilee, permit me to enter Carmel at fifteen." The Pope bent his head to her mantilla. Révérony said, "this is a child who desires the life of Carmel, but the superiors are, at the moment, considering the question." Leo XIII: "Well, my child, do whatever the superiors shall decide." Thérèse, hands on his knees: "O Holy Father, if you only said yes everyone else would agree." He looked at her fixedly and said gravely: "Well, my child, you shall enter if it be God's will." She tried to ask again; two noble guards told her to rise and, when she stayed, took her by the arms, and Révérony had to help before she left the Pope's knees. Louis had received his blessing earlier and met her at the door "bathed in tears, which she made no effort to dry".
- When / where: 20 Nov 1887; the Vatican
- People: Leo XIII; Thérèse; Céline; Louis; Révérony; Mgr Germain; two noble guards
- Source: LAV p.182–185 (audience dialogue has no footnote in Laveille; Laveille was present in the same pilgrimage and audience)
- New to English readers: partial (baseline: she asked the Pope)
- Confidence: high for the main exchange; medium for exact wording (Laveille's version is not footnoted).
- Notes: Laveille's account of the Pope's appearance may come from his own memory. Opponent: Révérony, at the moment of the audience. Surprise: the guards lifted her from the Pope's knees. Carmel notes that Thérèse's comment in LT 36 that the Pope seemed dead was later scraped off the letter (web-context [T8]).

### F-LF-192 — The cobweb, the weeding, "continually complaining"
- Claim: Thérèse wrote that Marie de Gonzague treated her severely and she "could never meet her without receiving some reproof" (HA ch. vii p.117). She left a cobweb in the cloister and the Prioress said before the community, "It can be easily seen that our cloisters have been swept by a child of fifteen. Too bad! Go, sweep away that cobweb…" In the rare hours of direction Thérèse was "reprimanded nearly all the time". As postulant she was sent at 4.30 p.m. to weed the garden, and often met the Prioress, who said, "After all, this child does absolutely nothing. What sort of novice would she be who must be sent out every day for a walk?" Thérèse also plied her needle in her free time, without raising her eyes, because she thought the Prioress wanted it. Told to report every sickness, she did so daily "through obedience"; the Prioress said, "This child is continually complaining… If she cannot bear her pains and aches, her place is not amongst us."
- When / where: 1888–1890; Lisieux Carmel
- People: Marie de Gonzague; Mère Marie des Anges; Thérèse
- Source: LAV p.200, p.316–317 (HA ch. vii p.117; Summarium 1919 p.518 §1393)
- New to English readers: yes (baseline names Marie de Gonzague only)
- Confidence: high
- Notes: Health detail: the "attacks of pain" of the postulant are not named. Surprise: postulant reported illness daily and risked dismissal.

### F-LF-270 — Clothing, 10 January 1889: white velvet, Alençon lace and snow
- Claim: Her reception of the habit came on 10 Jan 1889, later than the usual six months after entry; Laveille's footnote attributes the delay to Delatroëtte's continued opposition on grounds of her youth. Before it she made a retreat "in aridity" and wrote to Mère Agnès: "In my soul's intercourse with Jesus there is nothing, nothing but dryness and sleep! Since my Well-beloved wills to remain asleep, I will not prevent Him." At Louis's wish she wore a dress of white velvet edged with swansdown and trimmed with point d'Alençon lace, her long fair hair in natural curls, and white lilies as her only ornament. She wrote that Louis waited at the enclosure door, moved to tears, "pressed me to his heart, saying: 'Ah, here is my little Queen'", gave her his arm and they entered the chapel together: "This day was his triumph, his last feast on earth" (HA ch. vii p.124). Céline told Louis she too would enter Carmel; he said, "Let us go together before the Blessed Sacrament to thank the Saviour… Yes, the good God greatly honours me in claiming my children. If I possessed anything better, I would hasten to offer it to Him." Thérèse wrote that snow covered the quadrangle when she entered the cloister, though the weather had been mild; the community spoke of "the little miracle". On 2 Jan 1889 she wrote to Mme Guérin thanking her for the wreath of lilies sent by her cousins for the clothing.
- When / where: 10 Jan 1889; Lisieux Carmel
- People: Thérèse; Louis; Céline; Mère Agnès; Delatroëtte; Mme Guérin
- Source: LAV p.212–215, p.451 (letter of 2 Jan 1889, Appendix)
- New to English readers: partial (baseline: "habit 10 Jan 1889")
- Confidence: high
- Notes: Laveille says the weather was "extremely mild" that day; no weather record was checked. Thérèse's snow remark: see M-LF-005. Surprise: the dress used Alençon lace, the product of her mother's trade. It is the last feast Louis attended before the illness (third attack "a month after her reception", LAV p.216).

### F-LF-205 — Jeanne's wedding and the mock invitation
- Claim: Jeanne Guérin married Dr Francis La Néele on 1 Oct 1890, eight days after the veiling. On the next parlour visit she spoke of the attentions she gave her husband. ### F-LF-214 — Céline and the dance
- Claim: Laveille says that, at a wedding in Lisieux, Céline felt she could not refuse an invitation to a dance. Thérèse summoned her to the parlour and warned her with the three young Hebrews in the fiery furnace: would the spouse of Jesus "adore the world's golden statue by giving yourself to dangerous pleasures?" At the soirée her uncle brought her, and she refused for a long time, "even at the risk of offending". At last, "literally forced" (her words) by a young man, both found themselves "as though paralyzed" and could only walk with solemn steps. He led her back and "quietly slipped away much abashed, and did not dare to appear again". Céline credited Thérèse's prayers (Summarium 1919 p.400 §1013). Céline had already made a private vow of chastity.
- When / where: c. 1890–1894; Lisieux
- People: Céline; Thérèse; Isidore Guérin; an unnamed young man
- Source: LAV p.249–250
- New to English readers: yes
- Confidence: medium — the year is not given; the account is Céline's.
- Notes: See M-LF-009 for the "extraordinary" reading. Surprise: Céline says she was "forced" to dance.

### F-LF-226 — The photo sent to "Diana", and the press conference of 19 April 1897
- Claim: Mère Agnès suggested that Thérèse send photo no. 14 (Joan in prison) to Diana in summer 1896, after retouching; "Diana" replied. From August 1896 doubts rose: a German Jesuit called the texts fabrication; a French Jesuit's first article in *Études* on 1 Nov 1896; Mère Agnès asked Isidore on 6 Dec 1896 for "new documents"; *La Revue catholique de Coutances* said on 18 Dec 1896 Diana seemed fictitious; a Roman commission (Mgr Lazzareschi) was set up in Dec 1896; *Le Normand* (Lisieux) printed doubts on 30 Jan 1897. On Easter Monday 19 Apr 1897 Taxil told a full hall at the Société de Géographie, Paris, that he had invented Diana and called it the grandest hoax of his life. Only one image was shown, Thérèse's photo of Joan in prison, "from a Carmelite convent". *Le Normand* gave a notice on 21 Apr and a summary on 24 Apr. Per FR Wikipedia she learned on 21 Apr, tore up and burned Diana's letter, and tried to remove references from her writings. The Carmel suggests possible echoes (Céline to Br Siméon 25 Apr 1897; Ms C "there are truly souls who have no faith"; on that day Thérèse first called Bellière "my dear little brother", LT 224), but not as proof.

### F-LF-230 — The infirmary: nothing "worth recounting"
- Claim: Laveille says that while she lay in her cell after cautery during recreation, she heard a sister in the kitchen say, "Sœur Thérèse… will soon die, and in truth, I ask myself what can our Mother say of her after her death? She will be embarrassed, for this little Sister, amiable though she is, has surely done nothing worth recounting." (The prioress writes a circular for each dead nun.) Thérèse said to the infirmarian, "Ah, happily the good God has always given me the grace of being absolutely indifferent to [the opinion of creatures]." Another lay sister, temporary infirmarian, offered her food that would have made her sick; Thérèse refused gently. The sister said afterwards, "I do not know why they talk so much of Sœur Thérèse; she does nothing remarkable; one cannot even say that she is exactly a good religious." Thérèse smiled: "To hear on my bed of death that I am not a good religious, what joy!"
- When / where: 1897; Lisieux Carmel
- People: Thérèse; unnamed nuns
- Source: LAV p.344, p.372–373
- New to English readers: yes
- Confidence: medium — nuns unnamed; the "circular" note is Laveille's.
