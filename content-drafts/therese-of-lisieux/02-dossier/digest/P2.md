# Digest P2 — Les Buissonnets, 1877–1886

Saint: St. Thérèse of Lisieux. Period agent: P2 (stage 2b). Source: `02-dossier/_by-period/P2.md` (456 cards). The cards stay in `parts/`; this digest points to them by ID.

The file holds 130 events (E-P2-001 to E-P2-130) in time order. Some cards in the source fall before 1877 (Zélie's last year, Alençon) or after 1886 (Carmel notes, the 1887 Poor Clare and Visitation attempts). They stay in the event they explain.

## Period summary

### Turning points
1. 28 Aug 1877: Zélie dies. Thérèse chooses Pauline as "Mama" (E-P2-017, E-P2-018, E-P2-020).
2. 15 Nov 1877 (date disputed): the family moves to Les Buissonnets, Lisieux (E-P2-021, E-P2-025).
3. Oct 1881: she starts at the Benedictine Abbey school. The years there are the "saddest" of her life in her own words (E-P2-063, E-P2-064).
4. 2 Oct 1882: Pauline enters Carmel, and Thérèse's grief follows (E-P2-079, E-P2-081).
5. Late Mar–13 May 1883: the illness and the cure by the smile of the Virgin (E-P2-085, E-P2-089).
6. 8 May and 14 Jun 1884: First Communion and Confirmation (E-P2-099, E-P2-103).
7. May 1885–Oct 1886: scruples. Marie enters Carmel on 15 Oct 1886 and the scruples end after prayers to the four dead children (E-P2-106, E-P2-125).
8. 25 Dec 1886: Christmas night, the "conversion" (E-P2-128).

### The five best scenes
1. E-P2-017 with E-P2-020: the death night, Louis with the candle behind the Viaticum, "Your little Mother is gone!", the coffin in the corridor.
2. E-P2-085: the 1883 illness, the hat that becomes "the big black beast", Louis leaving in tears.
3. E-P2-089: the cure, "They want to poison me", the two tears, "because I could not see her any more".
4. E-P2-128: Christmas night 1886 with Louis's unedited words, "thank goodness it's the last year".
5. E-P2-081: the petticoat in the parlour, "Look, Pauline, I have on the little petticoat you made me".

More strong scenes: E-P2-043 (the thirsty child), E-P2-051 (Victoire the "brat"), E-P2-122 (Louis: "Ah!… but without you!"), E-P2-087 (Thérèse on the floor during High Mass), E-P2-080 (the stratagem to see the prioress alone), E-P2-124 (Louis waits at Calais and Dover), E-P2-014 (the prize-giving at home).

### The five best "new to English" facts
1. E-P2-128: in the manuscript, Louis says « Enfin, heureusement que c'est la dernière année!... ». The 1911 text and Laveille soften it.
2. E-P2-097: Marie refused Thérèse a daily half hour, then a quarter hour, of mental prayer, because she feared God would take her too soon (F-EW-305, F-EW-306, F-EW-316).
3. E-P2-083: Marie stopped Thérèse's drawing lessons ("money thrown away"), and Thérèse kept silent (F-EW-253, F-EW-255, F-EW-261).
4. E-P2-091: after the cure, Thérèse feared she had lied and, long after, feared she had faked the illness. The 1911 text cuts the second fear (F-OW-041, F-OW-042).
5. E-P2-085: Dr Notta said "for me, it is not hysteria" (F-EW-286). The daily showers were a torment to her modesty (F-EW-262). Also new: Pauline admits she worked to draw Thérèse to Lisieux Carmel (E-P2-076, F-EW-217).

## Events

### E-P2-001 — c. 1877, Alençon / Lisieux — The string of "practices"
- What happened: At about four Thérèse counted her small acts of virtue and sacrifices on a kind of rosary with movable beads made for the purpose. She called them "pratiques". The word came up so often in games with her sisters that a neighbour was puzzled. Her practices were mostly giving way to her sister, which cost her much because she was then very headstrong. In Ms A 11r a shopkeeper (and neighbour Mme Gaucherin) puzzled over the child's constant talk of "pratiques" in the garden.
- Cards: F-EW-101, F-EW-108, F-OW-012
- Best details: the neighbour puzzled by "practices" (F-EW-101). "son caractère était alors très arrêté", her character was then very set (F-EW-101 note). Geneviève mentions a later notebook of 818 sacrifices before First Communion (F-EW-108 note).
- Evidence: eyewitness (Marie); high.
- Conflicts: none. It may identify the puzzled neighbour of F-EW-101; the cards do not say so.
- New to English readers: partial (the beads are known in specialist literature; the neighbour is new)
- Scene potential: medium.

### E-P2-002 — before 1877, Alençon — Zélie's dream: all her daughters Carmelites (and Madame Acarie)
- What happened: Pauline, Marie and Céline all report that Zélie, reading the life of Madame Acarie (Blessed Marie of the Incarnation), said how honoured and happy a mother would be to give all her daughters to God. Zélie also told Pauline that if Louis died first she would end her days in a Visitation monastery once the children were settled. Both Marie and Pauline say the parents never pressed the girls toward religious life. Pauline adds that the mother was firm and let nothing pass, especially vanity, while the father was gentler and loved Thérèse specially.
- Cards: F-EW-090, F-EW-094, F-EW-099
- Best details: « Toutes ses filles carmélites! Est-il possible qu'une mère ait tant d'honneur? » (F-EW-094). « Est-elle heureuse d'avoir donné ses trois filles au bon Dieu! » (F-EW-099). Zélie had the girls lift their hearts to God during the day and took them to visit the Blessed Sacrament (F-EW-090). Mother to father about Thérèse: « Tu la perdras! » "You'll spoil her!" (F-EW-090). Marie insists the parents never pressed the girls (F-EW-099 note).
- Evidence: eyewitness (Pauline, Marie), depositions 1910 and 1915; high.
- Conflicts: none. Detail differs: Pauline (F-EW-094) says "all daughters Carmelites"; Marie (F-EW-099) quotes "three daughters".
- New to English readers: yes (the Visitation plan and the exact quotations)
- Scene potential: medium, a mother's spoken wish that the future story fulfils.

### E-P2-003 — before 1877, Alençon — Louis closes his shop on Sundays; the parents' daily rule
- What happened: Louis Martin closed his jewellery shop on Sundays although country people came to town that day to buy wedding jewellery. Friends urged him to leave a side door open; he answered that he preferred God's blessing. Louis and Zélie rose at 5 a.m. to hear Mass daily, fasted all of Lent, and never travelled on Sunday. Zélie went to the 6 a.m. Mass until a few days before she died, despite painful cancer.
- Cards: F-EW-093, F-EW-105, F-EW-137
- Best details: « Si vous laissiez seulement une porte de côté ouverte » (F-EW-093). Louis lost good sales though his confessor allowed Sunday trading and other Alençon jewellers did it (F-EW-105). Louis also joined monthly nocturnal adoration and had it set up in Lisieux (F-EW-105). Jeanne, a child, saw Zélie go to 6 a.m. Mass daily until days before her death (F-EW-137).
- Evidence: eyewitness (Pauline 1915, Marie, Jeanne as child); high, Jeanne medium (childhood memory).
- Conflicts: none. The 1910 deposition of Pauline also mentions the Sunday rest (F-EW-093 note).
- New to English readers: partial
- Scene potential: low, background of the family's piety.

### E-P2-004 — 1860s–1877, Alençon — Household of Alençon: the maid Louise, Léonie, and the older girls' schooling
- What happened: Marie recalls that the maid Louise could freeze Pauline, Léonie and Hélène with a look; only Marie defied her. Léonie, from age five, was "fascinated" by Louise and preferred her to her mother, and Zélie said sadly that she had a child who did not love her. Marie and Pauline were day-boarders at the Providence school, then boarders at the Visitation of Le Mans where an aunt was a nun. Léonie was tried at Le Mans for three months and sent home as too undisciplined. Zélie also nursed a sick servant herself for weeks. Zélie's letters (CF 193–196, 205–206) say that in March 1877 Marie discovered the servant Louise Marais had been secretly mistreating Léonie, who dared not complain "for fear of getting it double". Léonie (b. 3 Jun 1863) was a sickly, slow, difficult child sent back several times from the Le Mans school (her aunt called her a "terrible little girl") and for years obeyed only Louise and avoided her mother. Zélie: « la brutalité n'a jamais converti personne, elle fait seulement des esclaves » (CF 195). Léonie now clung to her mother and "is now like a normal child". Zélie called it "the greatest grief I have ever had in my life". In June 1877 she wrote "This servant I trusted deceived me" and "It was only to Léonie that she was so cruel; the others she spoils more than I do." Louise Marais was hired in 1865 aged 16 from Le Merlerault; Zélie kept her on to nurse her to the end; Louise later married (Mme Legendre).
- Cards: F-EW-110, F-EW-126, F-EW-127, F-EW-129, F-LF-085
- Best details: Marie answered Louise « Je suis bien libre moi » (F-EW-126). Louise tore up the dress of a doll Marie had made (F-EW-126). Louise nursed Zélie to the end and left only when the family moved to Lisieux (F-EW-126). At Le Mans holidays were New Year (8 days), Easter (15 days), summer (6 weeks to 2 months) (F-EW-127). The Providence teacher who ate sweets and pulled ears; Fr Pichon later told Marie he had "converted" her at a retreat (F-EW-127). Zélie nursed a servant with rheumatic fever day and night rather than send her back to poor parents (F-EW-110). Zélie's letter of 8 Jan 1877 told her dying sister Marie-Dosithée to ask the Virgin to "put right" the gift of "this poor Léonie"; Pauline later called Léonie "fallen from the nest" (F-EW-129). « la brutalité n'a jamais converti personne, elle fait seulement des esclaves » (F-LF-085).
- Evidence: Marie's own writing (Cahier) and deposition (PO 3, PA 7); Zélie's letter cited in a modern lecture; high.
- Conflicts: duration of the mistreatment: "more than two years" (Piat) against "about nine years" (FR Wikipedia). Marie's Cahier (F-EW-126) says Zélie trusted Louise and forbade scolding for trifles; the letters of March–June 1877 show Zélie then discovering the cruelty. Louise Marais is the servant who carried Thérèse home from her baptism (LAV p.461).
- New to English readers: yes (Louise, the Le Mans detail); partial for the letter.
- Scene potential: medium, the maid Louise and the child Léonie.

### E-P2-005 — c. 1877, Alençon — The dream of two little devils on a barrel of lime
- What happened: At about four Thérèse dreamed that two ugly little devils danced on a barrel of lime in the garden despite flat-irons on their feet. When they saw her they fled in fright into the laundry room and ran about on the tables, peering out to see if she was still there. She later read it as proof that devils are "cowards" who flee from a child's look.
- Cards: F-OW-010
- Best details: the flat-irons on their feet (dropped from the 1911 text) (F-OW-010 note). « des démons qui sont des lâches, capables de fuir devant le regard d'un enfant » (F-OW-010).
- Evidence: own writing (Ms A 10v); high as her report of a dream.
- Conflicts: none.
- New to English readers: partial
- Scene potential: high, comic and vivid.

### E-P2-006 — to 24 Feb 1877; letters of the 1870s, Le Mans — Sr Marie-Dosithée: Zélie's sister, her letters, her death
- What happened: Zélie's elder sister Marie-Louise (two years older) became Sr Marie-Dosithée at the Visitation of Le Mans, taught Marie and Pauline there, and died of tuberculosis on the morning of 24 Feb 1877, a few months before Zélie. Near the end, fully conscious, she told her superior "I have no other thoughts but of love, trust, and abandonment." Zélie urged her children, especially Thérèse, to pray to her aunt, "a protectress in heaven", and began to hope for her own cure through her. Fr Piat quotes her earlier letters to Isidore as already carrying traits of the Little Way: "I go to God just as I would to my Father, even with much more trust and abandonment"; a mother does not have as much tenderness for her small child as the Lord for the abandoned soul; "Our Lord wants no galley slaves in his service"; for his 1875 pilgrimage to Lourdes she wrote that "the most miserable but with most trust" are heard; motto "I will do God's business and he will do mine". Her testament: God alone will be your reward, keep your taste and household modest in prosperity, share your abundance with the poor. Isidore, ten years younger than Zélie, was the family's spoiled boy. Pauline dreamed she saw and spoke with the dead aunt, and Zélie credits the aunt with the discovery about Léonie's mistreatment.
- Cards: F-EW-2584, F-LF-014, F-LF-089
- Best details: "I have no other thoughts but of love, trust, and abandonment." (F-LF-089). "Our Lord wants no galley slaves in his service" (F-EW-2584). She sent him the Introduction to the Devout Life and the twelve volumes of L'Année sainte de la Visitation (F-EW-2584). Taylor 1912 cites her via Dom Guéranger (F-LF-014 note). The link to Francis de Sales (F-EW-2584 note). See M-EW-002 for her recovery.
- Evidence: Visitation nun's letter cited by Zélie (F-LF-089); Piat (1967) for the letters, an anachronistic view that they anticipate the Little Way; medium-high.
- Conflicts: Zélie's letter of 8 Jan 1877 (F-EW-129) has her still dying in Jan, death on 24 Feb.
- New to English readers: yes
- Scene potential: medium.

### E-P2-007 — Feb–May 1877, rue Saint-Blaise, Alençon — Zélie's letters about Thérèse, Feb–May 1877
- What happened: On 13 Feb 1877 Zélie told Pauline that one evening, when she bent to kiss the child "pretending" to sleep, Thérèse hid under the blanket saying "I don't want anyone to look at me"; Zélie showed displeasure; two minutes later the child came down the stairs alone, barefoot, tripping on her long nightdress, face wet with tears: "Mama, I have been naughty; forgive me." In the same letter Thérèse said at a long church feast "It's more beautiful than usual, but it bores me all the same"; the sermon "was Hebrew" to her; she made up for it with the torchlight procession. On 4 Mar 1877 Zélie wrote that when Marie took Céline for lessons Thérèse cried; Marie let her sit in for two or three hours with beads to thread or a rag to sew; she did not dare move, and when her needle came unthreaded two big tears ran down her cheeks until Marie rethreaded it "and the poor little angel smiles through her tears". She also recited poems with "the exact expression and tone". On 22 Mar 1877 Zélie wrote of the two youngest: "Céline never commits the smallest wilful fault. The little one will be good too; she would not tell a lie for all the gold in the world; she has wit such as I have never seen in any of you." On 10 May 1877 she wrote "Thérèse is Marie's joy, happiness and glory", with retorts "very rare at her age", and the "little host" dialogue (God is almighty, "He can do whatever He wants"). Thérèse copied the late letter of Zélie in Ms A 11r: "the little one will be good too, she would not lie for all the gold in the world; she has wit such as I have never seen in any of you." A shopkeeper asked what the child meant by always talking about "pratiques" (little acts of virtue) in the garden; Mme Gaucherin leaned out of her window to understand the debate. Zélie described Thérèse reciting a poem with eyes raised "with an angelic expression".
- Cards: F-LF-082, F-LF-086, F-LF-087, F-OW-012
- Best details: « Mama, I have been naughty; forgive me. » (F-LF-082). The barefoot child on the stairs (F-LF-082). « elle ne mentirait pas pour tout l'or du monde, elle a de l'esprit comme je n'en ai jamais vu à aucune de vous » (F-LF-086). The needle and the two big tears (F-LF-087). "The sermon was Hebrew" (F-LF-082 note). Mme Gaucherin leaning out of her window to follow the debate about "pratiques" (F-OW-012).
- Evidence: own writing of Zélie (CF 188, 192, 195, 201); high.
- Conflicts: none. The neighbour puzzled by "practices" in F-EW-101 is unnamed; Ms A names Mme Gaucherin.
- New to English readers: yes (barefoot stairs, beads); partial (Ms A 11r cites CF 195).
- Scene potential: high.

### E-P2-008 — 4 and 12–17 Apr 1877, Alençon — Her first letters: Pauline holds her on her lap and guides her hand
- What happened: At age four Thérèse "wrote" her first letters with help. In LT 1 (to Louise Magdelaine, 4 Apr 1877) she says "elle me tient sur ses genoux car je ne sais pas seulement tenir un porte-plume". She says Pauline wants her to say she is "a little lazybones" but it is not true, because she works all day playing tricks on her poor little sisters: "je suis un petit lutin qui rit toujours" ("a little imp who always laughs"). In LT 2 (to Jeanne and Marie Guérin, 12–17 Apr 1877) she says "Marie ne veut plus me conduire la main et je ne sais pas écrire toute seule". She adds that she knows no other people than the two Visitation nuns.

- Evidence: own writing (dictated, hand-guided); high, probably Pauline's pen with the child's dictation.
- Conflicts: none.

### E-P2-009 — 1877; Aug–Sep 1897, Lisieux Carmel — Circular notes: Canon Delatroëtte delays TSA's profession; Abbé Youf and the dying Thérèse
- What happened: The circular of Sr Thérèse de Saint-Augustin says the date of her profession (1 May 1877) was put back by the superior, Canon Delatroëtte, who was "little favourable" to early commitments and wished to wait for her majority; he gave way on condition that her mother ratified the consent, which she did gladly. The same circular says the chaplain Abbé Louis-Auguste Youf led souls firmly and was not always good at comforting them. On her deathbed Thérèse spoke to him of temptations against faith and he answered "Do not dwell on those thoughts, it is very dangerous!" After the talk she sighed patiently: "You see how little I am consoled!" Youf still admired his "angelic penitent" without reserve. After TSA's clothing sermon he told her "My poor child, you already have one foot in hell and, if you go on, you will soon put in the second"; only the Virgin ended her alarm.
- Cards: F-EW-2546, F-EW-2547
- Best details: « Ne vous arrêtez pas à ces pensées » in English: "Do not dwell on those thoughts, it is very dangerous!" (F-EW-2547). "You see how little I am consoled!" (F-EW-2547). Delatroëtte's caution was an earlier instance of what Thérèse met in 1887–88 (F-EW-2546 note).
- Evidence: circular by Mère Agnès de Jésus, 8 Sep 1929, drawing on TSA's notes; medium ("You know, my Reverend Mother, his answer…" shows it was known in the house).
- Conflicts: the circular gives no date for the Youf exchange beyond "on her deathbed".
- New to English readers: yes
- Scene potential: medium (the Youf exchange; outside this period).

### E-P2-010 — 1850s–Nov 1877, Alençon — Point d'Alençon: what the lace was, and winding up the business
- What happened: Point d'Alençon is needle lace of very fine hand-spun linen thread in small pieces of 20–30 cm joined by invisible stitches; the industry began at Alençon about 1664 under Colbert with 30 workers from Venice. Zélie specialised as an "assembleuse", the worker who joins the pieces. Her outworkers worked at home; she took orders, supplied designs, checked work and did the final joining, and 500 francs a metre was a normal price. Her bureau was open on Thursdays to deliver, receive and pay work. In May 1877 she wrote "I have given up in good earnest my point d'Alençon and now begin to live on my income … it has cost me dear," yet in late July she was still completing four orders and had taken back 11 metres; she refused 18 metres of flounce in June and had 24 metres started for her own account or for Mme Deverny. Isidore scolded her for working. Louis was still finishing the last pieces in Nov 1877. No source found for the exact number of Zélie's lace workers; only FR Wikipedia says "about twenty". A count from her letters would need a full read (gap card F-LF-263).
- Cards: F-LF-017, F-LF-094, F-LF-263
- Best details: « J'avais de l'ouvrage pour quatre […] J'ai mené rude vie » "I had work for four … I have led a hard life" (F-LF-094 note). 500 francs a metre (F-LF-017).
- Evidence: Laveille, Carmel "Le couple Martin", Zélie's letters (CF 215, 24 Jul 1877); Louis's letter of 25 Nov 1877; high (process), medium (price and workforce).
- Conflicts: number of workers: "about twenty" appears only in Wikipedia (F-LF-017 note). Zélie says she gave up in May but kept working until near death (F-LF-094). Gap.
- New to English readers: yes
- Scene potential: medium.

### E-P2-011 — June–Aug 1877, Lourdes / Alençon — Zélie goes to Lourdes and comes back sicker
- What happened: Zélie went to Lourdes in June 1877 to ask for her own cure and came back sicker. People in the house heard her praying through nights of terrible pain. She died on 28 Aug 1877. Zélie's letters give the journey. She joined the Angers diocesan pilgrimage to Lourdes (train from Angers 18 Jun 1877, 7.50 a.m.), taking Marie, Pauline and Léonie; Louis first disapproved of taking all three, then wished it. Leaking water cans; lodging changed by Abbé Martignon to the Sisters of Nevers' orphanage; Marie with grit in her eye for four hours; Léonie's swollen feet; Léonie sleepwalking in the night train and throwing herself on her mother and against the carriage door. At the piscina Zélie plunged into "icy water and marble cold as death", nearly choked, and went in four times. She came home with five francs and had lost her dead sister's rosary, "the only relic I had". Louis waited an hour at Alençon station with Céline and Thérèse; he had spent the week jumping at every ring of the bell for the hoped-for telegram; he was surprised to see Zélie come back as happy as if she had been granted the grace, which "gave him back his courage". Some townspeople showed "an air of disbelief". She wrote to Pauline: "Do not hope for much joy on earth." She wrote that God "does not think only of himself; he could well work a miracle for me even if nobody in the world knew of it," and that she hoped at least for Léonie's cure; a young Alençon woman of 20 had died at Lourdes two days after arriving.
- Cards: F-EW-130, F-LF-096, F-LF-097
- Best details: she was heard praying during nights of terrible pain (F-EW-130). the lost rosary, "the only relic I had" (F-LF-096). "Do not hope for much joy on earth" (F-LF-097). Zélie came home as gay as if cured (F-LF-097).
- Evidence: eyewitness (Pauline, present in the house, 1915); high.
- Conflicts: Pauline (F-EW-130) says Zélie came back sicker; Louis and the letters say she came back gay. Not a contradiction of health, of mood.
- New to English readers: partial
- Scene potential: medium.

### E-P2-013 — Jul 1877, Alençon — Léonie wants to die in her mother's place
- What happened: Léonie read in the Semaine Catholique of a soul who offered her life for the Pope and was heard. ### E-P2-023 — 1877–1889, Lisieux — The Guérin household and the two families' routine
- What happened: Isidore Guérin was a pharmacist and his wife was Céline Fournet. Their daughters were Jeanne (b. 1868) and Marie (b. 1870, later Sr Marie de l'Eucharistie, d. 1905). They employed a young maid-nursemaid, Marcelline Husé, from 1880. Thérèse came daily to their house, and the families met every Thursday and Sunday. Les Buissonnets was about 1 km away. Thérèse, five years younger than Jeanne, went with Marie Guérin while Jeanne and Céline were always together. Fr Piat says Céline Martin became Jeanne's chosen friend while Marie attached herself to Thérèse, three years her junior. The cousins went to the Benedictine school, meeting at the Place Saint-Pierre under the eye of the two families' maids, Victoire and Marcelline. On Thursdays they often joined the Maudelonde girls (children of Mme Maudelonde, Céline Guérin's sister); on Sundays the Martin girls took turns being invited to the pharmacy, and Louis fetched them in the evening. The grandmother Fournet had a modest country house at Saint-Ouen-le-Pin, reached in a hired break. In 1878, 1885, 1886 and 1887 Isidore rented on the coast for one or two months (the chalet Colombe at Deauville, the villa Marie-Rose, the chalet des Lilas at Trouville) and invited the nieces in turn; Thérèse went several times. Thérèse wrote that, wishing to attract notice by imitating Marie's chronic migraines, she only repeated the fable of the donkey and the lapdog, and people thought her tears hid a scruple. ### E-P2-026 — 1877–Dec 1886, Les Buissonnets — Shy, tearful, headachy: the "darkening" after her mother's death
- What happened: Every family witness says Thérèse changed after Aug 1877. Léonie noticed the sudden contrast between the playfulness before and the excessive sensitivity after. She was cheerful only with the family at Les Buissonnets and excessively timid elsewhere. She cried at trifles, mostly from fear of having hurt her father, her sisters or above all God. Other girls found her clumsy at games and despised her, and she began to like hiding, believing herself inferior. She had "almost continual headaches" and, once consoled, she cried at having cried. In society she passed for incapable and clumsy, and Uncle Guérin said her education had been left incomplete. Marguerite Leroy (Sr Marie du Saint-Rosaire) saw her as shy and extremely sensitive, but with a gentle, pleasant gaiety, and anxious in the smallest details not to sadden Pauline. Marcelline says that alone with her on the walk or at home Thérèse was very affectionate and trusting and told little secrets, mostly about piety. Abbé Pichon says he never saw a cloud on her face and that she had no whims, though she would have had desires had she listened to herself. Jeanne found her not expansive. Thérèse later wrote that after her mother's death "my happy disposition changed completely. I, who had been so lively, so expansive, became timid and shy and sensitive to excess; a look sufficed to make the tears flow". Céline testified that from 1877 to Christmas 1886 she went through "a period of darkness", excessively timid, with continual headaches, "she cried at the least thing … and when she had been consoled, she cried for having cried." (Apostolic Process p.126.) The bull says she was barely four years and seven months old when her mother died, that "seized by great sorrow, she lost all sense of joy" (« magno dolore correpta, omnem iucunditatis sensum amisit »), and that her education was entrusted to her elder sisters Marie and Pauline under her father's watchful care (AAS 17 p. 338). Office lesson IV says "in her fifth year". Benedict XV says the loss produced in her "a seriousness and maturity of mind far in advance of her years". Thérèse wrote in Ms A 13r that from her mother's death "my happy character changed completely; I, so lively, so outgoing, became timid and gentle, oversensitive. A look was enough to make me burst into tears." She could not bear strangers and was happy only in the family. She dates this "second period" from age four and a half to fourteen, "the most painful of the three".

### E-P2-056 — 29 Mar 1880; 16 Jun 1881; 1882, France — The anticlerical laws in the background
- What happened: On 29 Mar 1880 Jules Ferry (Minister of Public Instruction since Feb 1879) promoted two decrees: the Jesuits were to be expelled from France, and other religious congregations had three months to ask authorisation or face dissolution and dispersion. Most refused, in solidarity with the Jesuits, and the unauthorised ones, among them Benedictines, Capuchins, Carmes (men), Franciscans and Assumptionists, were expelled. Some Dominican houses were closed and some anticlerical town councils removed nursing sisters from hospitals. On 16 Jun 1881 the first Jules Ferry law made primary education free for boys and girls; a law of 1882 made it compulsory and secular. Members of Catholic orders could no longer teach in public schools after a five-year transition. Thérèse entered the Benedictine abbey school, a private Catholic school, on 3 Oct 1881, four months after the first law. Laveille describes Isidore Guérin as "a formidable polemical opponent of the Church's enemies".

The servant told Dolan that when they first heard workmen swearing, Thérèse said the men had not had the graces they had and were perhaps more to be pitied than blamed. Thérèse later told Pauline she suffered from a schoolmate's jealousy but never complained, and that she tried to attach herself to a teacher as other girls did, failed, and later saw it as a grace. Sr Saint-André (arrived Jan 1882) confirms Thérèse entered in October 1881 as a demi-pensionnaire (in the morning, home in the evening) and says her father withdrew her in January 1886. Abbé Domin believes she left around Easter 1886 and is not on his register after October 1886; about four and a half years, cut by many absences. From Oct 1881 Marcelline took her to school each day with the Guérin girls; in the evening she brought her back to the Guérin house where her father or sisters fetched her. Marcelline says that even before her First Communion, hearing workmen blaspheme, she explained that one must not judge souls: they had received fewer graces and were more unhappy than guilty (« ils étaient plus malheureux que coupables »). Blanche Dupont, older and sometimes put in charge, asked Thérèse in 1885–86 if she had broken silence; Thérèse said no; when Blanche teased by pretending to doubt her, Thérèse looked very hurt and said "Marie!... I never lie". Blanche adds Thérèse kept a little apart from games and was far more serious than others. Alice Dumoulin, later a Sister of the Holy Family at La Délivande, came to the Abbey school at five when Thérèse was 12 or 13. Because she was so young she was placed especially in Thérèse's care, and remembered Thérèse as very intelligent and very kind to her small charge. Léonie was a boarder at the Benedictine abbey of Notre-Dame-du-Pré from early 1878 to the 1881 holidays and Céline a half-boarder from early 1878 to mid-1885. Louis placed Thérèse there as a half-boarder on 3 Oct 1881, aged eight and a half. Laveille says about sixty pupils from the town and "the best families of the neighbourhood", with friction between farmers' daughters and refined town girls; high grey walls, austere classrooms, a chapel of Louis-Philippe's time and a walled garden. The Carmel school page says one year in the green class, two in the violet and two in the orange. Each morning Céline and Thérèse called at the Guérin pharmacy and walked to the Abbey with Jeanne and Marie Guérin, led by a Guérin servant who later became a Benedictine, Sr Joséphine de la Croix, and testified at the Process; she said the child, alone with her, was "affectionate and confiding" and spoke of spiritual things. The Abbey's manuscript notice says "A winning and delicate smile was her habitual expression of countenance, as soon as her tears, too ready and too frequent it must indeed be said, were dried", with sweet manners, tender piety, obedience in the smallest things, all "veiled by excessive timidity and sensitiveness"; Laveille adds that the nuns did not fully see her worth and took her timidity for a fault. She gave "honourable burial" under the Abbey chestnut trees to young birds fallen from nests, and in 1884–85 her stories drew the older girls into a circle until the sister in charge broke it up. She had two special friends; one bond was short; the other friend came back after months away and gave her a look of indifference, and Thérèse wrote that her heart, once it loved, "loves always" and she still prayed for her; she kept clear of pupils' attachments to particular mistresses. She told invented serial stories that lasted days; older girls joined the listeners, until the mistress forbade "my trade of orator", wanting them to run and not "discourse". Abbé Domin called her "his little doctor" because of her name; she wept if she lost first place by one word of catechism. Her uncle's family thought her "a little ignoramus, good and gentle... but incapable and clumsy"; she blamed her « écriture de chat et mon orthographe qui n'est rien moins que naturelle » (the 1911 text drops "cat's handwriting"). Ms A 38r: she tried to make friends with two girls; one left school for some months and Thérèse kept a little ring she had given her; on her return she got « un regard indifférent »; "I did not beg for an affection that was refused me", but she kept praying for her "and I love her still". She tried to win a teacher's favour as Céline did, and failed.

### E-P2-065 — 1881–1886, Benedictine Abbey school — Her lessons: first in history and composition, weak at rote, arithmetic and spelling
- What happened: Thérèse loved study, especially sacred and Church history and French composition, and was always first in tests. Grammar and arithmetic were dry to her. She belonged to the Association of the Holy Angels, which admitted only model pupils; Marie gave her private instruction each evening before First Communion. The Benedictine teachers thought her very intelligent, very pious, "a little timid", and were fond of her. Laveille says Thérèse was put at once in a class of much older girls, some fourteen. ### E-P2-085 — Oct 1882–May 1883, Les Buissonnets — The 1883 illness: headaches, terror, convulsions, doctors
- What happened: Léonie says violent headaches began just after Pauline left (Oct 1882). Marie dates the onset to the end of March 1883, and from 7 April to 10 May Thérèse was in a pitiful state with crises of terror several times a week. There were fits of delirium and convulsions with wild movements; she screamed, her eyes were terrified and her face contracted. Nails in the wall took horrible shapes. She struggled to speak to Marie and no sound came. The doctor (Dr Notta) treated her with hydrotherapy and daily showers, without success, and admitted he understood nothing. Her bed stood in a large alcove; she tried to throw herself into the gaps and fell several times onto the stone floor without a scratch, or beat her head on the wooden bed. Céline describes her standing on the bed and rolling head first over the rail. Marie and Céline hold that Thérèse kept the use of her faculties and heard everything. Léonie never heard the doctor herself but heard in family gatherings that he said it was a nervous illness and he understood nothing, and Jeanne reports he called it "St Vitus' dance" (chorea), suspecting something else. Jeanne saw the illness begin at the Guérins' house while M. Martin was away, at first taken for fever, with violent trembling; then depression and a half-hallucinated state in which objects and people's postures looked frightening, and at the peak rotating movements of the whole body she could never have done well. Marcelline places the start at Easter 1883, when Louis took Marie and Léonie to Paris and left Céline and Thérèse with the Guérins; after a conversation with Uncle Isidore came nervous trembling, then crises of fright several times a day. Louis, holding his hat at her bed, was met with the cry "Oh, the big black beast!" and left in tears saying his poor little girl was mad. Marie's ruse: she pretended not to be afraid, and Thérèse calmed but watched her face with "unheard-of perspicacity". ### E-P2-128 — 25 Dec 1886, Les Buissonnets — Christmas night 1886: the shoes on the hearth and the "conversion"
- What happened: Céline witnessed the sudden change of Christmas night 1886. Thérèse held back her tears after a remark by their father and even appeared joyful, mastering a sorrow that would earlier have crushed her and cheering him with charming grace. Céline "thought she was in a dream". Afterwards she was never again ruled by her feelings. She then had a great desire for knowledge but limited her study hours. Céline calls it the starting point of her "conversion" and an "abrupt" change. Thérèse wrote that after midnight Mass she expected her shoes on the hearth filled with gifts as in her "infant days", proof that she had been "treated like a little baby". Louis, "contrary to his usual habit of indulging me in everything", was annoyed. As she went up the stairs she heard him say "For a big girl like Thérèse, this is too childish; I hope this will be the last time." Céline said in a low voice "Do not go down immediately; wait a little; you would surely cry when looking at the presents before papa." Thérèse drove back her tears, went down and took gifts from the shoes "as happy as a queen". Louis laughed and Céline "thought she was in a dream". She wrote that Jesus "accomplished the work that I had failed to do in many years" and she had regained the strength of soul lost at four and a half; "The fountain of my tears was dried up." The brief says she was subject to bodily weakness and anguish of soul, troubled by spiritual dryness (« spiritus ariditate devexata, saepe erumpebat in lacrymas »), often burst into tears and prayed constantly; on Christmas night 1886, at Mass, she felt herself freed from her inner anguish (AAS 15 p. 204). Ms A 44v–45v (unedited words): coming home from midnight Mass (where she received Communion) she looked forward to her shoes in the fireplace, a childhood custom Céline kept up. Her father, tired, was annoyed to see the shoes and said words that "pierced my heart": « Enfin, heureusement que c'est la dernière année!... » ("Well, thank goodness it's the last year!"). Going upstairs to take off her hat, with tears in her eyes, Céline said "Oh Thérèse, don't go down, it would hurt you too much to look in your shoes right away." But "Jesus had changed her heart": she held back her tears, went down, took the shoes, set them before her father and joyfully pulled out the gifts, "looking as happy as a queen". Papa laughed; Céline thought she was dreaming. She calls it "the grace of my complete conversion"; "the work I had not been able to do in ten years, Jesus did in an instant". The 1911 edited text changes Louis's words to « Pour une grande fille comme Thérèse, c'est là une surprise trop enfantine; je l'espère, ce sera la dernière année » and changes Céline's words too.
