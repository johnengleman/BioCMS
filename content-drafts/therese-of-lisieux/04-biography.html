The Nun Nobody Could Write About

In the summer of 1897 a nun of twenty-four lay in her cell beside the kitchen of the Carmel of Lisieux, in Normandy. It was recreation hour, and she was resting after a medical treatment. Through the wall she heard a sister say that the prioress would be puzzled about what to write of her after her death. This little sister, the voice went on, "amiable as she is, has certainly never done anything worth speaking about."

The infirmarian heard it too. The dying nun answered that she had long been indifferent to what people thought of her.

The remark carried weight because of a custom. When a nun died, her Carmel wrote a short letter about her life, called a circular, and sent it to every other Carmel in France. The sister in the kitchen did not expect this circular to have much in it. The lawyers who later argued the nun's cause in Rome admitted that several of her sisters had been mistaken about her.

Not quite twenty-eight years after that afternoon, on 17 May 1925, Pope Pius XI declared her a saint.

Her name was Thérèse Martin. She entered a French Carmel at fifteen. At the order of two prioresses she wrote the story of her life in cheap notebooks, and she died of tuberculosis at twenty-four. The Catholic Church canonized her in 1925 and named her a Doctor of the Church in 1997. Benedict XV said that her way held "the secret of sanctity" for all the faithful. She wrote about it while her own sense of faith was failing.

The story does not start with her. It starts with her father, a young man on a mountain pass, who was turned away at a monastery door.

A Midnight Wedding in Alençon

Louis Martin wanted to be a monk. Around 1843 he climbed to the hospice of the Great St Bernard, in the Alps, and asked to join the canons there. The prior asked whether he had finished his Latin. He had not. The prior told him to go home, finish his studies, and come back. Louis studied for more than a year, fell ill, and gave it up.

Zélie Guérin wanted to be a nun. About 1850 she asked to join the Sisters of Charity who ran the hospital in Alençon. The superior told her at once that God did not want her there. Two refused vocations led to one marriage. Louis, by then a watchmaker, and Zélie, who made Alençon lace, married on 13 July 1858. The church ceremony took place at midnight, with no display.

They lived as if they had made a cloister at home. They went to the 5:30 morning Mass every day. Louis closed his shop on Sundays, at a real loss in money. Zélie ran her lace business herself, and a jury at the Alençon exhibition praised the "intelligent direction" of the young woman who managed it.

They had nine children and named every one Marie, after the Virgin. Four died young. When one baby boy died, Zélie set a crown of white roses on his small coffin. A baby girl, the first to carry the name Thérèse, starved at a wet nurse's house. Marie, ten, had seen the baby throw herself on the nurse's breast as if she were starving. She spoke up too late, and blamed herself.

The ninth child was born late on 2 January 1873. The family disagreed later about the exact hour. Zélie was forty-one and ill, and she could not nurse the baby. By January she feared an illness with "the same alarming symptoms as in my other children who died." She wrote to her brother: "Must I lose this one too?"

By March two doctors had given the baby up. Zélie's sister, a nun of the Visitation order, vowed that the child would take the name Françoise if she lived. If Zélie refused, the nun added, the saint was free to take the child back, and Zélie need only "have a coffin made." Zélie refused to change the name. She asked her brother to tell her what to call the baby "so that she does not die." Then she added, "If anyone saw this letter, they would think I had lost my head!"

The doctor said the only hope was a healthy wet nurse. After a night in which the baby would hardly drink, Zélie left at daybreak, alone, and walked eight kilometres to the farm of a woman named Rose Taillé. On the empty road she met two men. "Even though they should kill me," she thought, "I do not care." Rose would not leave her own children, but she took the baby home.

At the farm the baby lived outdoors. Rose wheeled her to the fields in a wheelbarrow, lying on bundles of grass. "She is a big baby," Zélie wrote in July. "She is browned by the sun." Two months after the baby came home, Zélie wrote that this child was the strongest she had had, except the first. "I think she will be the last," she said. Zélie had three years left to live.

The Child Who Chose Everything

Zélie's letters, about two hundred of which survive, are the best record of these years. On 5 December 1875 she wrote to Pauline, at boarding school, about the youngest. The little girl was sweet and clever, and she remembered everything. She was also, Zélie wrote, a child who "flies into dreadful furies" and "rolls on the floor in despair as if all were lost."

The same letter records a stranger scene. Nearly three years old, the girl was caressing her mother when she said, "Oh! how I wish you would die, my poor little Mother!" Zélie scolded her. The child explained, "But it is so that you go to Heaven, since you say you must die to go there."

In her memoir Thérèse copied both passages and added, "You see, Mother, how far I was from being a little girl without faults!" The book that made her famous kept the wish and cut the furies. At night she kicked off her covers and knocked her head on the wooden bed frame. She woke her parents calling that she was toquée, a word that means both bumped and a little mad. In the end the family tied her into bed with cords. Every evening her sister Céline, nearly four years older, came to knot them.

She would not climb the stairs alone without calling "Mama!" at every step. In October 1876 her mother told her she would go to heaven if she was good. If she was not good, the child said, she would go to hell, "but I know what I'd do, I'd fly up to you in heaven; how could God take me then?" Zélie saw in her eyes that she believed it. "You'd hold me tight in your arms."

That same year her sister Léonie, who thought herself too old for dolls, offered Céline and Thérèse a basket of doll clothes and scraps, with her doll on top. Céline took a small bundle of braid. Thérèse thought for a moment and took the whole basket. In her memoir she called the moment the summary of her whole life. Later, she wrote, when she saw what holiness asks, she cried the same words to God.

“I choose everything!” … “My God, I choose everything. I don’t want to be a saint by halves.”

— Thérèse, Manuscript A, the first part of her memoir, written 1895: the child's cry of 1876 or 1877, then her cry as a young woman

In December 1876 Zélie went to a doctor. He told her she had a fibrous tumour. She said an operation would only shorten her life, and he agreed. She asked what use a prescription would be. "None," he said. "It is to please the patients." Louis gave up fishing and put his lines away in the attic.

Zélie died shortly after midnight on 28 August 1877, at forty-five. Louis had walked to the church and escorted the priest who brought her the last sacraments, carrying a candle. He came to Marie, sobbing: "Your little Mother is gone!" Marie remembered it as about eleven o'clock. Thérèse was four. Louis lifted her to kiss her mother's forehead, and she did it without a word. Later she stood a long time in front of her mother's coffin. In her memoir, the lid seemed very big. She did not cry much, and she told nobody what she felt. No one had time to ask her.

Marie saw it and did not ask. She thought the child too serious and too advanced for her age, and she chose not to draw her out. After the funeral the five sisters stood together. The maid said, "Poor little things, you have no mother any more!" Céline threw herself into Marie's arms and said, "Well, you'll be Mama." Thérèse usually copied Céline. This time she turned to Pauline. "Well, for me, Pauline will be Mama!" She told Céline later why. Pauline had been away at school, and Thérèse did not want her to feel left out.

In mid-November Zélie's brother Isidore brought the five girls to Lisieux, and Louis followed after he had closed up his business. They moved to a house called Les Buissonnets, near the Guérins. Five years later, Pauline would close a convent door behind her.

The Hat Like a Black Beast

On a sunny afternoon, when she was six or seven, Thérèse stood alone at an upstairs window of Les Buissonnets. Her father was away in Alençon. In the garden she saw a man walk past her little flower bed toward a clump of fir trees. He was dressed like her father and had his height and his walk, but he was very bent and old, and a thick veil covered his head. She cried out, "Papa! Papa!" in a trembling voice. The man did not turn. He vanished behind the trees.

Marie heard the cry from the next room and was frightened. They searched behind the trees and found no one. Thérèse did not know what it meant. She called it a prophetic vision.

At eight and a half she began school at the Benedictine abbey in Lisieux. The nuns put her in a class of girls of thirteen and fourteen, and she was often first. One jealous girl made her pay in a thousand ways for her small successes. Thérèse cried in silence and told no one, because she knew Céline would have put a stop to it. Years later she called those five years the saddest of her life. Her teacher confirmed in 1911 that one classmate gave her a small persecution, but in 1915 said that calling it persecution would be an exaggeration.

She was already thinking about Carmel. One Sunday in July 1882, at nine, she slipped away from her cousin and found the prioress alone in the parlour. She told Mère Marie de Gonzague that she wanted to enter. The prioress said that postulants, or candidates, of nine were not received. She would have to wait until sixteen. A sister at the turn, the revolving hatch at the door, joked that she would need to eat many plates of soup first.

On 2 October 1882 her sister Pauline, her second mother, entered that same Carmel. Carmel is an enclosed order, so the family saw her only through a grille. Thérèse got two or three minutes at the end of each visit, and she spent them crying. One day she said, "Look, Pauline, I have on the little petticoat you made me." No one took notice. Afterward Marie saw tears in her eyes.

The headaches began that autumn. By spring she was seized by fits of terror. Nails on the wall looked to her like "big charred fingers." She threw herself around the bed, and fell more than once onto the stone floor without a scratch. One day Louis sat by her bed holding his hat. She stared at him in silence, then gave a mournful cry: "Oh! the big black beast!" Louis left the room in tears.

The family doctor, Dr Notta, told the family he had never met such a case. "Call it what you like," Marie heard him say, "but for me it is not hysteria." Marie and Céline believed that the illness came from the devil, and the Church's own brief on her cause records that belief as the family's.

The crisis came on Pentecost Sunday, 13 May 1883. Marie offered her a drink, and Thérèse cried that they wanted to poison her. Marie then knelt with Léonie and Céline before the statue of the Virgin in the room and prayed three times. At the third prayer, Marie said, Thérèse fixed her eyes on the statue with a bright, calm look that lasted four or five minutes. Two big tears ran down her face. Alone with her later, Marie asked why she had wept. After hesitating, Thérèse answered, "It is because I could not see her any more."

Her sisters said the Virgin had cured her. Thérèse was careful in her own account, written twelve years later. She wrote that the Virgin "seemed" beautiful to her, and that she smiled.

Then the trouble began again, in her conscience. She had decided to tell no one, but Marie guessed, and passed the story to the Carmel. In the parlour the nuns asked whether the Virgin had carried the Child Jesus, and whether there had been much light. Thérèse could only say that the Virgin had seemed very beautiful and had smiled. She saw that they had imagined something grander. "I imagined I had lied," she wrote. She also came to believe that she had made herself ill on purpose. She told Marie and her confessor that she feared she had feigned the illness. Both tried to calm her, but the doubt stayed until she entered Carmel. The first fear lasted four years.

The Night the Tears Stopped

Around her First Communion, in 1884, she asked Marie, who now ran the house, for half an hour of prayer a day, then for a quarter of an hour. Marie later swore she refused both, because she feared God would take the child too soon. Céline remembered that Marie allowed the quarter of an hour. On half-holidays, Marie said, Thérèse hid behind her bed curtains and "thought" about God and about eternity.

Then came the scruples. In May 1885, during a retreat, she became tormented by every detail of her conduct. For about a year and a half she saw sin everywhere. Before confession she brought Marie a long list of supposed faults. Marie said she would take the sins on herself, and allowed the girl to confess only two or three. Her confessor never learned what she was going through.

By thirteen she cried at almost everything. On 15 October 1886 Marie entered Carmel. Thérèse had no one left to tell. She prayed to the four brothers and sisters who had died as babies. Peace came, and with it a thought: "If I was loved on earth I was also loved in Heaven." The tears did not stop. She wrote that she was unbearable because of her sensitivity. If she hurt someone she cried, and when someone began to comfort her, "I cried for having cried."

On Christmas night 1886 the family came home from midnight Mass. In France, children found small gifts in their shoes by the hearth on that night. Louis, tired, saw the shoes waiting and was annoyed. Thérèse heard him on the stairs, in the words she set down in her manuscript.

“Well, thank goodness it’s the last year!”

— Louis Martin, as Thérèse heard him, Christmas night 1886, Manuscript A

The words pierced her. Céline, with tears in her own eyes, whispered, "Oh Thérèse, don't go down, it would hurt you too much to look in your shoes right away." This time she held back her tears. She went down, took the shoes, set them in front of her father, and pulled out the gifts, "as happy as a queen." Louis laughed. Céline thought she was dreaming. Thérèse later said that in that moment Jesus did what she had failed to do in ten years. She called it her conversion. Pauline softened his words in the printed book.

Once her own tears stopped, she noticed other people's. On Pentecost Sunday, 29 May 1887, she told her father in the garden that she wanted to enter Carmel. He wept, and said she was very young. Then he told her that God did him a great honour by asking for his children. He reached down, plucked a tiny white flower from the low wall, roots and all, and explained how God had made it grow. She pressed it into her copy of the Imitation of Christ, and it stayed there for ten years.

That summer all France talked about a murderer. Henri Pranzini had killed two women and a girl in Paris, and he showed no sign of regret. Louis did not let his daughters read newspapers, but the story reached the house anyway. Thérèse decided to save Pranzini's soul. She was too shy to ask her confessor for a Mass, so she gave Céline a coin for it, blushing, and did not name the intention. Céline guessed, and joined her. She asked God for one thing: a sign that he had repented. Against her habit, she began to look for a newspaper.

Pranzini was guillotined on 31 August. The next morning she opened La Croix and read that just before the last stroke he had asked for the chaplain's crucifix and kissed it three times. She ran away to cry. Years later the chaplain's successor, Abbé Valadier, told Céline a more careful story. Pranzini's arms were tied, so he could not have seized the cross. He demanded it and kissed it with passion. Either way, the sign had come. Thérèse wrote that her "first child" had pressed his lips to the sacred wounds. Now she wanted Carmel, and she wanted it soon.

A Fourteen-Year-Old Before the Pope

She wanted to enter Carmel by Christmas. She was fourteen. Her father was sixty-four and had already given three daughters to the cloister. Her family and the Church both had good reasons to say wait.

Three Men Say No

The first was her uncle Isidore, her legal guardian. On 8 October he told her kindly that it would be a public scandal, "a unique case in all France." She should wait until seventeen or eighteen. She spent three days in what she called a night of the soul. On Saturday, 22 October, he called her into his study and gave way. He said she was a little flower that God wanted to pick.

The second was Canon Delatroëtte, the superior of the Carmel. Marie de Gonzague had asked the dying foundress, Mère Geneviève, to plead for her in front of the community. Pauline later said that the canon answered with an outburst: did the salvation of the community hang on this child? Louis took Thérèse to see him in the rain. The canon received them coldly and cut her short with a "No." He was only the bishop's delegate, he said. She left in tears under her umbrella and told her father that she would go to the Pope if the bishop refused.

The rule of Carmel set no minimum age, as Laveille notes.

The third was Bishop Hugonin, at Bayeux, on 31 October. Thérèse had put up her hair to look older. Abbé Révérony, the bishop's vicar general, saw her tears and said, "Ah, I see diamonds. You must not show these to Monseigneur." The bishop was surprised that her father supported her. He suggested she stay at home several years to console him, and said he would consult the canon. Louis answered that they would go to Rome.

The Road to Rome

The diocese of Bayeux was sending a pilgrimage to Rome for the priestly jubilee of Pope Leo XIII, and Louis took his two youngest daughters. In Paris, in the church of Notre-Dame des Victoires, Thérèse found peace about the smile of 1883. In Rome she and Céline wanted to kiss the ground of the Coliseum, where the martyrs had died. It lay behind a strong barrier. "Come, follow me," Thérèse said, and climbed over. Their father called after them, astonished, and could not bring himself to scold them afterward.

Céline Whispers, "Speak!"

In the account she wrote in 1895, it went like this. Révérony had a rule for the audience of 20 November: no pilgrim was to speak to the Pope. Thérèse knelt in front of Leo XIII. Céline whispered, "Speak!" Thérèse did not kiss his hand. She joined her own hands and said, "Most Holy Father, in honour of your Jubilee, allow me to enter Carmel at fifteen!" The Pope said he did not understand well, and Révérony explained that the superiors were looking into it. "Well, my child, do what the superiors tell you." She put her hands on the old man's knees. "Oh Holy Father, if you said yes, everyone would agree!" He looked at her and said, stressing each syllable, "Come, come… you will enter if God wills it."

In the same account, two guards and Révérony then lifted her by the arms and carried her almost to the door, while the Pope put a hand on her lips and blessed her. That detail is missing from the letter she wrote to Pauline the same evening, and the letter is plainer and sadder than the memoir.

“The good Pope is so old you would think he was dead … it is M. Révérony who does the talking. … I am the little Ball of the Child Jesus; if he wants to break his toy, he is quite free.”

— Thérèse to her sister Pauline, 20 November 1887

She wrote that she felt crushed and abandoned, and that she was afraid her father would ask to read the letter.

The bishop said yes on 28 December. Marie de Gonzague answered that she should wait until after Lent.

A Te Deum in a Cold Voice

Christmas 1887 passed with no answer, and Thérèse wrote that Jesus "did not wake up." Céline set a toy boat in a basin at home. On the white sail she wrote "I sleep but my heart watches." On the hull she wrote "Abandon!" The bishop had said yes, but the prioress wanted the entry put off until Lent was over. It was said that she wished to soothe the canon. Pauline later admitted that she had asked for the delay too. She feared the Lenten fast for Thérèse's first weeks.

The entry was fixed for Monday, 9 April 1888. At Mass that morning everyone in the family sobbed except Thérèse. She walked ahead to the cloister door, her heart beating so hard that she thought she might die. She knelt for her father's blessing. Louis knelt too, and blessed her, weeping.

The enclosure door stood open, and Canon Delatroëtte was waiting. Witnesses remembered his words a little differently. All agreed on the meaning. The nuns could now chant a Te Deum, he said, and he presented them with this child of fifteen whom they had wanted. If it went badly, the responsibility would be theirs alone. The nuns were chilled. Louis stood there.

Inside, she was led to the dark choir, where she saw the eyes of the foundress, Mère Geneviève, fixed on her. In her cell she said to herself, "Now I am here for ever." The next day Louis wrote to a friend: "Thérèse, my little queen, entered Carmel yesterday!… Do not pity me, for my heart overflows with joy."

The nuns had expected a child. They saw a girl who carried herself, one of them said, with a sort of majesty. After a month the prioress, Marie de Gonzague, wrote to Mme Guérin that she had "not a word to say to her" and that everything was perfect. She said something different to Pauline. Pauline told the court in 1915 that Gonzague had answered her sharply when she complained about how her sister was treated. Thérèse was "much prouder than you think," the prioress said, and needed to be humbled all the time. The remark is not in Pauline's testimony of 1910.

The novice mistress, Marie des Anges, was absent-minded. She ordered the postulant to report every stomach ache. Thérèse had one every day, and she came each day, blushing, to say so. After a while the mistress forgot her own order, and Thérèse did not remind her.

One evening in those first months, the mistress went to console her in her cell. Thérèse sat on the straw mattress in a long nightgown, her hair loose, with moonlight on her face. "I suffer much," she said, "but I feel I can suffer still more." The mistress said later that she felt she was speaking to a saint, and held back her tears.

Her Father's Long Night

On Saturday morning, 23 June 1888, Louis walked out of Les Buissonnets after a spell of confusion. On Sunday a letter came from the post office at Le Havre asking for money. Isidore Guérin and Céline found him there on Wednesday. He had shaved off his beard, and he spoke of wanting to be alone. His sister-in-law wrote, "How heavy the Cross is!"

Thérèse's clothing, the ceremony in which she received the habit, was set for 10 January 1889. She had waited longer than usual, partly to please the superior and partly in hope that her father could come. He came, dressed in his best, and waited at the enclosure door, moved to tears. "Ah, here is my little Queen," he said. She wore white velvet trimmed with point d'Alençon lace, the trade her mother had worked at. Later she wrote that he had never looked more handsome or more dignified. It was, she said, his last feast on earth. On her way back into the cloister, snow began to fall. "The courtyard was white like me," she wrote.

A month later, on 12 February, Louis was taken to the Bon-Sauveur, an asylum at Caen. Céline wrote that in his mind he saw carnage and battles, and that he heard the cannon and the drum. He wanted to carry his revolver in case of danger. He would not wish to harm even a cat, she wrote. In Lisieux he had been known as "le saint Patriarche." In June, lawyers came to the asylum and had him sign the papers that took away his control of his property. They told him it was at his children's request. He wept, said his children were abandoning him, and signed. Céline called it the sharpest wound of all.

The daughters heard more than they should have. Pauline testified in 1915 that at recreation the prioress spoke of what madmen do, and of straitjackets, in front of the sisters. She also said that careless people blamed the daughters' entry into Carmel for his illness. In the passages the researchers read, no other witness repeats that claim. Céline speaks only of hurtful remarks.

Pauline said that Thérèse, sixteen, did not seek comfort from Marie or Pauline; they came to her for it. She told them, calmly, that this trial was one of the greatest graces of their lives. In her memoir she never named the asylum. She called that day the moment when her father drank the most bitter and most humiliating of all cups, and the 1911 edition dropped the word "humiliating." Near the end of her life she joked to Céline, "If you go mad, nursie, the 'Bon Sauveur' will come and fetch you!"

Her vows (her profession) were delayed too. In January 1890 Gonzague told her to wait, and Pauline, who was present, joined in the refusal. She made her vows on 8 September 1890, carrying a note: "Take me, O Jesus, before I commit the smallest deliberate fault."

The public veiling, when she received the black veil, followed on 24 September. Louis seemed a little better, and the family hoped he could come. At the last moment those in charge feared that strong emotion would set him back, and he stayed at Caen. The day before, she wrote to Céline that Jesus wanted her to be an orphan.

“On the day of my wedding I was truly an orphan, having no Father on earth any more but able to look to Heaven with confidence and say in all truth: ‘Our Father who art in Heaven.’” … “That day Jesus let me be unable to hold back my tears, and my tears were not understood.”

— Thérèse, Manuscript A, 75v and 77r, about her veiling, 24 September 1890

She still found room for a joke. That autumn she wrote a mock wedding invitation for her own spiritual marriage. Her parents announced the wedding too, and the invitation named "Monsieur Louis Martin, owner and master of the Lordships of suffering and humiliation." The 1911 edition cut that line. Louis was still at Caen. Would he ever come home?

Hidden Among Difficult People

In October 1891 a Franciscan, Père Alexis Prou, preached the community retreat. Thérèse was eighteen. She had made her vows carrying a note that asked to die before committing one deliberate fault, and she could hardly bear to live with the fear. She wrote later that she suffered great trials that year, "even to asking myself at times whether there was a Heaven." The preacher told her that her faults did not grieve God. It freed her.

“I'm made so that fear makes me back away; with love I don't just go forward, I fly.”

— Thérèse, Manuscript A, about the retreat of October 1891

She went back to confession, and the confession ran long. According to Pauline, the prioress, Marie de Gonzague, was angry. She could not understand why anyone was not content with her own direction, and what could "this child" have to say to a priest? She forbade Thérèse to speak to Père Alexis again. As second sacristan, Thérèse could hear him pacing in the next room, saying his breviary, free to any sister but her. She chose silence and obeyed. The novice mistress, Marie des Anges, wrote that she could not recall it without her heart tightening.

On 5 December 1891 the foundress, Mère Geneviève, died. Thérèse took the last tear shining on her eyelid on a handkerchief. Many wreaths came, and Thérèse arranged them around the coffin. A lay sister who was watching said she knew how to put her own family's wreaths in front and the poor people's bouquets behind. Pauline overheard the answer. Thérèse thanked the sister, said she was right, and asked for the moss cross that some workmen had sent. She put it in front.

At the end of December influenza struck the house. Three nuns died, nearly the whole community lay in bed, and Thérèse went between the sick and the dying, almost the only healthy sister. The gardener, who could not see her face under her veil, said he knew her by her walk. "I never see her run." Canon Delatroëtte, who had fought her entry, saw in her a great hope for the community's future.

Her temper was still hers. Once, while she was returning the keys of the communion grille, a young sister tried to take them so that Thérèse would not wake the prioress. Thérèse insisted, the door banged, and the prioress woke. The sister said loudly, "How disagreeable she is!" Thérèse could not stay silent gracefully, so she fled "without drum or trumpet," and sat on the stairs with her heart pounding "to enjoy in peace the fruits of my victory."

In February 1893 the community elected Pauline as prioress, and Pauline gave her sister a hidden task with the novices, without a title. Even before she had that task, Thérèse told the truth to people who could hurt her. On 8 December 1892, while Gonzague was still prioress, she took a young nun, Marthe, laid Marthe's head on her own heart, and told her that her love for the prioress was "a poison." She said the same to another sister. She would rather be sent out of the monastery than fail in her duty, she added. Pauline was frightened when she heard, because Thérèse risked being sent to another Carmel.

Her father had come back to Lisieux in May 1892. He died on 29 July 1894 at La Musse, where Isidore had taken him for the summer. Céline received his last breath. Around that time Thérèse and Marie, talking in Carmel, finally understood the bent, veiled man in the garden. He was her father in his illness, with his face hidden like the face of Christ in the Passion. Louis had covered his head with a handkerchief when the illness began. The vision had come fourteen years earlier.

Six weeks after Louis died, Céline entered the Carmel on 14 September 1894. She brought her camera. Over the next three years she photographed Thérèse in the cloister.

Cheap Paper, Read Two Months Late

One winter evening in 1894 or early 1895, after Matins, four sisters warmed themselves together: Marie, Céline, Thérèse, and Pauline, who was the prioress. Thérèse told two or three stories from her childhood. Marie pushed Pauline to order her to write them down instead of writing only little poems. "She is an angel who will not stay long on earth," Marie said. "We will have lost all these details." Pauline hesitated, then gave the order. Thérèse laughed, as if they were making fun of her.

She had refused to write about herself before. A young novice, Marie de la Trinité, said she meant to write down the story of her own vocation. Thérèse told her not to. She herself, she said, would write nothing about her life without an order she had not asked for. She filled cheap two-sou notebooks of the worst paper.

The house was a place for plays. On Pauline's feast, 21 January 1895, Thérèse played Joan of Arc at the stake. A careless act set fire near her feet. Pauline had ordered her not to move while others put the flames out. She stayed still, with the flames crackling at her feet, and offered her life to God, as she told the community afterward. Céline, who had entered Carmel a few months before, photographed her in costume in the courtyard. Two years later, that photograph would turn up in a place none of them imagined.

On Trinity Sunday, 9 June 1895, during Mass, she felt pressed to offer herself as a victim to God's merciful love. After Mass she led Céline in silence, as if beside herself, to find Pauline. Her face was lit up. She asked leave to offer herself, with Céline. Céline said Pauline was in a hurry and did not seem to understand much of what it was about. Pauline herself later said she agreed "without enthusiasm." Two days later Thérèse and Céline knelt before the statue of the Virgin of the Smile, and Thérèse said the Act aloud. She wrote the text herself and carried it on her heart for the rest of her life.

Meanwhile the notebook, Manuscript A, the first part of her memoir, went on. On 20 January 1896, Pauline's feast, Thérèse walked toward her stall in the choir during evening prayer. She knelt and gave the notebook to her sister. Pauline nodded. She put it on her stall and did not open it.

Two months passed. In that time, Pauline recalled, Thérèse did not mention the notebook once. When Pauline finally said she had not found time to read it, Thérèse did not seem hurt.

Blood on Good Friday

On 21 March 1896 the community voted for a prioress, and it took seven ballots to give Marie de Gonzague her majority. Thérèse did not vote, since she was still counted among the novices. She heard the result and, for an instant, stood stunned. Then she went to console the new prioress, who was hurt. On 29 June she gave her a story called "Legend of a little Lamb." In it Jesus tells a shepherdess that it took seven ballots, and that he willed it, because he loved her.

Twelve days after the vote, on the night of Holy Thursday, 2 April, Thérèse lay down in her cell after watching at the altar until midnight. She had just put out her small lamp. She felt something like a wave rising, rising, bubbling up to her lips. She did not light the lamp to look, so as to mortify her curiosity. At dawn her handkerchief was full of blood.

She was glad. In her own account she thought she might be dying, and "my soul was flooded with joy." She told the prioress and asked for no special treatment. On Good Friday she fasted on dry bread and water and did heavy cleaning. Marie de la Trinité found her washing windows, pale and drawn. She said that Thérèse had told her that morning, with a face full of joy, and made her promise to say nothing, so as not to grieve Pauline. That night she coughed blood again. Pauline said later that she had been kept in the dark.

Something else began that spring. During the Easter season, she wrote, Jesus let her feel that there are people who have no faith. Thick darkness filled her soul, and the thought of Heaven, which had always been her comfort, became a subject of combat and torment. To Céline she let slip, "If you spent only five minutes in the trials I endure!" One chaplain told her that her state was very dangerous. On the advice of a wise director she copied out the Creed and carried it with her.

She feared her prioress would think she exaggerated, since the poems she wrote that year sounded flooded with consolation. Yet it was no longer a veil between her and the sky. It was "a wall that reaches to the heavens and hides the starry sky." When she sang of Heaven she felt no joy, she wrote, because she was singing "what I want to believe."

Gonzague also gave her a task and told her to hide it. A young missionary priest, Adolphe Roulland, was about to leave for the missions and wanted a Carmelite to pray for him. The prioress chose Thérèse, whom he did not know. "She is the best of my good ones," she said. Then she forbade Thérèse to tell Pauline. Thérèse obeyed. She barred her cell door when she wrote to him, and painted him an image in the library, with Pauline's brushes, borrowing them only when Pauline was out of the room.

On 8 September 1896, on retreat, she wrote to Marie. She listed everything she wished she could be: a warrior, a priest, an apostle, a martyr in every century. She wished to be flayed like Bartholomew and to murmur the name of Jesus at the stake, like Joan of Arc. Then she read the twelfth and thirteenth chapters of Paul's first letter to the Corinthians, and found peace.

“My vocation, I've found it at last: my vocation is love!”

— Thérèse, Manuscript B, 8 September 1896

She pictured herself as a weak little bird covered only with down, with the eyes and heart of an eagle. When clouds hide the Sun, the bird falls asleep, still believing it is looking at its dear Star.

Three months earlier, on Gonzague's feast, she had written a play in which she spoke of Diana Vaughan, a convert from Satanism, as "a new Joan of Arc." She hoped to see Diana enter the Lisieux Carmel. Diana Vaughan did not exist.

The Slow Last Summer

On 8 January 1897 a young nun, Thérèse de Saint-Augustin, dreamed she stood in a dark room shut by a heavy black door, with a line of bright light beneath it. A voice called for "Sister Thérèse of the Child Jesus" and said, "She must be very beautiful!" Some weeks later the young nun told Thérèse about it. Thérèse was moved, and she answered with the words of her trial, as the young nun recorded them. "I don't believe in eternal life," she said. "It seems to me that after this mortal life there is nothing more." The black door, she said, was the picture of what was happening inside her.

The day after the dream she had written to Pauline, who had been in tears. She hoped soon to go "up there," she said. "If there is a Heaven, it is for me." The words are in quotation marks in the letter, as if she were quoting someone. The line is missing from the 1911 edition of her letters. Years later someone scratched it out of the original.

On Easter Monday, 19 April, a man named Léo Taxil stood in front of a full hall at the Geographical Society in Paris. He told the audience that "Diana Vaughan," the Satanist who had converted through Joan of Arc, had never existed. He had invented her, he said, and called it the greatest hoax of his life. The only image he showed was a photograph of a young nun playing Joan of Arc in prison, "from a Carmelite convent." The Carmel's own editors say it was Céline's photograph of Thérèse, sent to "Diana" the summer before at Pauline's suggestion. Thérèse had believed in Diana. Her play of June 1896 shows it. Pauline and Céline would say afterward that she had dropped the writings when she learned that the author spoke against a bishop. The Carmel editors' chronicle records that she learned from it "that there are people who do evil only for amusement."

By June she was too weak to climb the stairs. She told Marie de la Trinité that the night before it had taken her more than half an hour, sitting on almost every step. Against her wish, Marie de la Trinité told the prioress. On the night of 2 June, near midnight, Pauline went to Gonzague. She said the childhood notebook was "nice," but would not help much with a death circular. She suggested that Thérèse be asked to write about her religious life. Gonzague gave the order the next morning. Thérèse found the notebook Pauline brought too beautiful, and asked whether she should at least crowd the lines to save paper.

Pauline began to write down her sister's sayings on 6 April, in what became the Yellow Notebook. The notebook we have is a copy she made in the 1920s from an earlier one, destroyed in 1904 or 1905. Four versions exist, of different lengths. Most of the sayings here come from Pauline's record. A few come from notes by Céline, Marie, and Thérèse de Saint-Augustin.

On 7 June, in the garden, leaning on Pauline, she stopped by a medlar tree. A white hen was sheltering her chicks under her wings. Thérèse's eyes filled with tears, and she covered her face. That evening she said that God had chosen this picture to show his tenderness. "He has hidden me entirely under his wings," she said. Two days later Marie said how sad they would be when she left. She is reported to have answered, "Oh no, you'll see, it'll be like a shower of roses." The saying exists in six versions.

On 8 July, after new haemorrhages and a night of high fever, she was carried down to the infirmary. In the room stood the statue of the Virgin from Les Buissonnets, the one that had "seemed" to smile on her at ten. She would never go back to her cell.

Twelve Weeks in the Infirmary

The infirmary had its comedy. On 9 July the superior, Canon Maupas, came to see whether she should be anointed. He found her so pleasant and smiling that he refused. "You do not have the face of a dying woman," he told her. Pauline said afterward that Thérèse did not know how to get what she wanted out of the superior. Thérèse answered gently, "I don't know the trade!" She would use a little pretence next time, she said. She would drink a cup of milk before he came, because she always looked worse after.

On 17 July, after coughing blood at two in the morning, she told Pauline that she felt her mission was about to begin. It was a mission to make God loved as she loved him, and to give her little way, the plain road of trust and small acts, to souls.

“Yes, I want to spend my Heaven doing good on earth. That is not impossible, since even in the very bosom of the beatific vision the Angels watch over us.”

— Thérèse, 17 July 1897, as recorded by Pauline in the Yellow Notebook

On 30 July she received the last sacraments. That same day she held up a glass of very bad medicine that looked like a delicious liqueur. A sister had said she hoped Thérèse was drinking good liqueur. Thérèse said that the glass was the image of her life.

“It always seemed to them that I drank exquisite liqueurs, and it was bitterness. I say bitterness, but no! for my life was not bitter, because I knew how to make my joy and sweetness of all bitterness.”

— Thérèse, 30 July 1897, as recorded by Pauline in the Yellow Notebook

In August the community doctor, Dr de Cornière, went on holiday. Marie and Céline testified that the sisters asked Gonzague to let their cousin by marriage, Dr La Néele, see Thérèse in his place, and that she refused. Marie called those weeks "the cruellest tortures." When the sisters complained, Marie said, Thérèse told them, "My little sisters, you must not grumble against God's will." Pauline gave a different count. She said Gonzague let La Néele in only three times in five weeks. When de Cornière came back in September, Pauline said, he spoke of morphine injections, and the prioress did not allow them. Céline said the sisters gave small doses of morphine syrup by stratagem, because Gonzague thought such relief shameful for a Carmelite. Marie des Anges wrote instead that God allowed the doctor not to think of painkillers. The Carmel's editors warn that the sisters' charges against Gonzague should be checked against the historian Guy Gaucher.

Then there was the meat juice. At the end of July she had an unconquerable disgust for food. A lay sister, Sister Saint-Vincent de Paul, brought her a cup of meat juice. Pauline named her as the sister who had criticized the wreaths, and Thérèse de Saint-Augustin named her as the sister who brought the juice. Thérèse refused it, because she had no appetite and because the prioress had not ordered it. The sister went back to the kitchen. She said that Sister Thérèse of the Child Jesus was "not even a good nun," much less a saint. Someone told Thérèse. She was happy. "To hear on my bed of death that I am not a good religious, what joy!" Laveille joins this scene to the remark she heard through the kitchen wall. Pauline and Thérèse de Saint-Augustin place them apart. Either way, one of the sisters later told Pauline it was her most edifying memory of Thérèse.

On 25 August she sent Abbé Bellière a card: "I cannot fear a God who made himself so small for me… I love him!… for he is only love and mercy!" On 28 August she pointed at a black gap under the chestnut trees. "That's the kind of hole I'm in, body and soul," she said. "Ah yes, what darkness! But I'm at peace there."

On 22 September someone said to her, "What a terrible illness, and how much you've suffered!" She answered, "Yes! What a grace to have faith! If I had not had faith, I would have taken my own life without hesitating for a moment." A week later, at noon on 29 September, she turned to Pauline. "Mother, is this the agony? … I'll never know how to die!"

The Thirtieth of September

Pauline read the office of St Michael and the prayers for the dying. At the word "demons" Thérèse made a small threatening gesture, like a child, and smiled. After the doctor's visit she asked, "Is it today, Mother?" The prioress said yes. One of the sisters added that God was glad. "Me too!" Thérèse said. That night she joined her hands. "Yes, my God, I accept everything!"

On the morning of 30 September, with Pauline beside her during Mass, she looked at the statue of the Virgin. "Oh, I prayed to her so fervently!" she said. "But it's pure agony, with no consolation." Pauline felt there was nothing bitter in it. Marie recorded that her tongue was dry, that she seemed abandoned by heaven and earth, and that Gonzague let all three of her blood sisters stay at the bed.

In the afternoon she put one arm on Pauline's shoulder and gave the other to Céline. Three o'clock struck, and her sisters saw a likeness to Jesus on the cross. She told them that she would never have believed one could suffer so much, and that she could explain it only by her burning wish to save souls. "I do not regret," she said, "having given myself up to Love." The cup, she said, was full to the brim.

Her sisters disagree about when the agony began. Some say three o'clock, others four thirty, one says five. All agree on what followed. For over two hours a rattle tore at her chest, and cold sweat soaked her clothes and the pillows. She held her crucifix so tightly that it had to be pulled from her hands later. Céline put a little piece of ice on her lips. Thérèse gave her a long look and a smile, as if to say goodbye. Outside the window, Marie des Anges said later, small birds gathered to sing.

Just after seven the prioress thought her state was stable and sent the community away. Céline called this a cruel trial. Thérèse asked, "Isn't this the agony yet? Am I not going to die?" Told it might last some hours, she said, "Well… come on… come on… Oh, I would not want to suffer less!" Then she fell back on the pillow. The prioress rang the infirmary bell, and the sisters ran back. Aimée alone did not hear it. She regretted that all her life. They had hardly knelt when Thérèse looked at her crucifix and spoke clearly.

“Oh! I love Him!… My God… I love you…”

— Thérèse, 30 September 1897, as her sisters recorded it

Céline records a pause in the second sentence: "My God… I… love you!" Marie remembered that she had not spoken since five o'clock, and that at about a quarter past seven she said, in a broken voice, "My God, I love you."

Then she sank back, and raised her head as if she had been called. She opened her eyes and fixed them a little above the statue of the Virgin. It lasted about as long as it takes to say the Creed slowly. Her face, which had been purple, lost its color and looked like a lily. A sister held a candle near her eyes, and the lids did not move. Then she closed her eyes, sighed several times, and died a little after seven.

Nobody had to close her eyes. Aimée, Marie and Pauline laid out her body. Her face looked like a child's. Pauline said she seemed about twelve. Her head still leaned to the right, and she was smiling.

A Book Instead of an Obituary

Sister Saint-Vincent de Paul, a lay sister, had often said cutting things where Thérèse could hear them. "Whatever will they be able to say about Sister Thérèse after her death?" was one of them, Céline reported. In the first days of October the body lay exposed in the choir. The lay sister came in, put her forehead on the dead nun's feet, and begged pardon for misjudging her. She said she was cured of a cerebral anaemia that had lasted years. Céline and Thérèse de Saint-Augustin both told the story, and the sister, Céline said, told every nun herself. It is her own report, passed on by two sisters. No witness in the dossier claims to have seen the cure.

The funeral took place on Monday, 4 October 1897. Céline called the procession to the town cemetery very small. Marie and Aimée remembered a large crowd. Isidore Guérin had just bought a plot for the Carmelites, and she was buried in its first grave. The dean of Saint-Pierre was heard to say, "Here is a burial at which nobody weeps." A few days later a wooden cross went up with her name and her dates.

Marie wanted to keep Thérèse's rope sandals as relics. They were so patched that no one else would wear them. A lay sister snatched them and said, "You won't keep that filth!" She threw them on the fire, and was sorry afterward.

Then there was the circular. Instead of a short letter, the Carmel sent every other Carmel a book. Isidore Guérin paid for the printing. Bishop Hugonin gave his approval on 7 March 1898, after first remarking that one must beware of the imagination of women. Godefroy Madelaine, a priest of the Premonstratensian order at Mondaye, revised the text. The prioress, Marie de Gonzague, wanted the whole story to seem addressed to her. Forms of address and some private family details were scratched out. The book of 1898 carried Pauline's editing hand as well. Marie restored the original text after Gonzague died. When Pauline was asked under oath in 1910, she said the changes were "of little importance." The judges ordered a copy of the original to be added to the record. The Carmel's editors and later critical editions show that the changes were larger.

The book was called Story of a Soul, and it appeared in October 1898. Carmels lent it from house to house. Pauline was put in charge of shipping and had at least five orders a day. Céline said it spread "like a trail of powder." By the Carmel's own count, 9,741 letters came in the year to July 1910. Not everyone liked it. Some called it "a rose-water life." One theologian who first thought it insipid and childish changed his mind.

Alexander Grant, a Scottish Presbyterian minister, read it in bed with influenza about Christmas 1909 and felt he held the work of a genius. In 1910 he hung an enlarged photograph of her over his fireplace. Expecting a Protestant minister and his wife, he was tempted to take it down, and found that he could not touch it. He was received into the Catholic Church on 20 April 1911, and later became keeper of her birthplace house in Alençon. The editors of the process record report that when he died in July 1917, he murmured, "Little Thérèse, come and fetch me, if it is God's will, and take me with you."

Marie de Gonzague had died on 17 December 1904 of a tumour of the tongue. On the day before, she told Pauline that no soul in the house had been as guilty as she. "And yet," she said, "I trust in God and in my little Thérèse." Pauline is the only witness to the remark, and she gave the words a little differently in 1915.

The community had expected a circular with nothing in it. The book that went out in its place turned up on tables in Scotland, and people began to walk to a grave in a Lisieux cemetery. Sooner or later someone would ask Rome to look into the matter.

From a Wooden Cross to Saint Peter's

On 15 November 1909 a priest named Dumaine walked into the town cemetery at Lisieux. The keeper spoke before he could ask a question. "I know why you've come: it's for the little saint. Take the left-hand path." The grave was covered with flowers. Another priest, who visited the grave that year, found himself alone at it. By 1912 he found a very large crowd.

Bishop Lemonnier of Bayeux had asked the nuns of the Carmel what they remembered. On 9 November 1907 one of them, a Polish choir nun who later left the community, sent him her answer.

“I never saw anything that could get her put on the altars.” … “I saw a child who was fêted, pampered, adulated, always set on a pedestal. The mother prioress doted on her.”

— Sister Thérèse de Jésus, letter to Bishop Lemonnier, 9 November 1907

Other nuns remembered a prioress who was cold and hard on Thérèse, and the two accounts do not fit together. The Church opened its first inquiry at Bayeux in 1910. Nuns, priests, and relatives swore to what they remembered.

Marie's letters show that the sisters meant to correct Rome's portrait of her. On 13 March 1915, before she testified, Marie wrote to Léonie that the Roman lawyer had failed to paint a portrait "simple enough, while still being a saint's portrait." "We will be sure to set it right," she wrote. In the same year she swore that no one had influenced her testimony. Céline told the court that she would rather Thérèse were not beatified than see her painted falsely. She thought the Roman articles made rare gifts sound frequent, when Thérèse's life had to be simple to be a model for little souls.

The war pushed the cause along. An officer named Pierre Mestre found the book while stationed at Lisieux, and in 1916 he proposed that soldiers write to the Pope. The Carmel keeps twenty-six petitions from officers and soldiers, dated from July 1916 to February 1918. Its archivists say these petitions were decisive for a dispensation from part of the usual inquiry. Pope Benedict XV said later that he himself had received many letters from French soldiers who credited her with saving their lives.

On 14 August 1921 he declared her virtues heroic. The decree answered an objection that her childhood illness made her holiness suspect. It answered that the witnesses who described the illness also described the sudden cure, and both must be believed. It called hers "a special kind of holiness." Speaking after the decree was read, the Pope said that spiritual childhood is a child running to its mother's arms. "There lies the secret of sanctity," he said.

Her remains were still in the cemetery. The mayor of Lisieux refused to permit an exhumation in 1922. Then he demanded six million francs, and finally approved on 8 March 1923. On Monday, 26 March, her coffin was carried in procession to the Carmel chapel. Laveille says fifty thousand pilgrims arrived in the town that morning. Taylor says fifty thousand escorted the coffin.

Pope Pius XI beatified her, the step before sainthood, on 29 April 1923 and canonized her on 17 May 1925. The Church had approved four cures for her cause, two before each step. At Lisieux on 30 September 1925, the twenty-eighth anniversary of her death, a cardinal placed a golden rose from the Pope in the hand of the marble figure over her tomb.

What She Left Behind

Her one long trip was the pilgrimage of 1887. On 14 December 1927 Pius XI named her patron of all missionaries and all missions, equal with Francis Xavier. The decree said that even in the far lands of unbelievers she had sent the rain of roses she had promised. Her first wooden cross, painted by Pauline in 1897, carried a line from her last summer: "I want to spend my Heaven doing good on earth."

The basilica at Lisieux was begun in 1929. Pius XI wanted it "very big, very beautiful, and as quickly as possible." It was blessed on 11 July 1937. At the blessing Bishop Picaud spoke, and he praised Thérèse and her three sisters who had become Carmelites. He forgot Léonie. A convent at Caen had borrowed a radio, and Léonie, who lived there as a nun of the Visitation order, heard the homily. She said only that Pauline would be more hurt than she was.

On 3 May 1944 Pius XII made Thérèse the second patron of France, beside Joan of Arc.

On 19 October 1997 John Paul II named her a Doctor of the Church, a title the Church gives to a few saints whose teaching it commends to all. She is the youngest of them. He had announced his intention at World Youth Day in Paris that August. The document that proclaimed her counted her writings: three manuscripts, 266 letters, 54 poems, eight plays, and 21 prayers. A hundred years earlier, a sister in the kitchen had wondered what her prioress could find to say.

Her parents followed her. Louis and Zélie Martin were beatified at Lisieux on 19 October 2008 and canonized on 18 October 2015, the first married couple canonized together.

Pope Paul VI liked to recall that he had been baptized on 30 September 1897, the day she died.

How This Life Was Researched

This life rests on 3,661 numbered fact cards. The sources include Thérèse's three manuscripts and all 266 of her surviving letters, her sisters' records of her last words, 33 sworn depositions from the inquiry of 1910–1911, and 26 from the inquiry of 1915–1917. They also include church documents in Latin, early English books, and hundreds of web pages, in French, English, Latin, Italian, and Spanish. The researchers compared the cards with five popular English-language accounts and found hundreds of facts that those accounts leave out. Where witnesses disagree, the text usually says so.
