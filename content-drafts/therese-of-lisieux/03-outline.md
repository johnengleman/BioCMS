## Part 1. Portrait, threads, points of connection, what set her apart, why famous

### Portrait (about 340 words; not for publication)

Thérèse was a small girl with a large will. Her mother wrote that at two or three she flew into "dreadful furies" and rolled on the floor as if all were lost (F-LF-063, F-OW-004). Céline called her "naturally proud and stubborn" as a child (E-P1-064, E-P1-078). After her mother died she became shy, tearful, and often ill with headaches (E-P2-026, F-EW-2300). At school she cried in silence and told no one (F-OW-031).

Others saw a quiet, thoughtful girl. A lady said she had "Heaven in her eyes" (F-EW-2030, F-EW-2172). The nuns saw "a sort of majesty" in the fifteen-year-old postulant (F-EW-630). Yet her sisters said that little in her life looked extraordinary (F-EW-2188, F-EW-2176, F-EW-2140). Marie sighed, "And to think no one will ever know her" (F-EW-2172).

She was funny. The community said, "Sister Thérèse is not here; we shall have no laugh today" (F-EW-2041). She wrote to a missionary about a lobster "possessed by the devil" (F-OW-476). She wrote "Amour du lucre" on her sister's fingernail (F-EW-2356). She said her cough sounded like a railway engine (MQ-036). She had a Mass said for the murderer Pranzini: "after the tricks he played, he must need it" (F-EW-2153).

She had faults and fears. She cried "for having cried" (F-OW-058, MQ-013). Scruples troubled her for about eighteen months (F-EW-335, F-OW-051). Her prioress said she was "prouder than you think" (F-EW-471). She fled a quarrel "without drum or trumpet" (F-OW-092). She admitted that one sister had "a knack for getting on my nerves in everything" (MQ-032). She feared spiders (F-EW-2256). She loved beauty and good taste, and minded damaged things (F-EW-2202).

She was bold and blunt. She climbed the Coliseum barrier (F-LF-176). She spoke to the Pope against orders (F-EW-435). She said, "I love only simplicity; I loathe pretending" (MQ-043). She could be fooled: she believed a fake convert, Diana Vaughan, was real (F-OW-572). She also doubted. She wrote of wondering "whether there was a Heaven" as early as 1891 (F-OW-126). She kept her jokes in the infirmary (F-EW-1457, F-EW-2622).

### Points of connection (five)

1. **Grief for a parent, and losing a second mother.** Zélie dies when Thérèse is four. Pauline enters Carmel when she is nine. (F-OW-014, F-OW-015, F-EW-134, F-EW-235.)
2. **A parent's mental illness, and the shame around it.** Louis's committal, the gossip, and her joke that the "Bon Sauveur" would come for her sister. (F-LF-198, F-EW-649, F-OW-087, MQ-046.)
3. **Feeling small, unnoticed, or judged by co-workers.** The kitchen remark, the meat juice, the sister she could not like. (F-EW-1218, F-EW-1227, MQ-032, F-EW-537.)
4. **A hard boss.** Direction hours of scolding, a confessor taken away, a prioress who refuses a doctor. (F-EW-458, F-EW-471, F-EW-741, F-EW-1523.)
5. **Doubt, long illness, and dark humor.** The trial of faith, the daily fever, and "I don't know the trade!" (F-OW-213, F-OW-321, MQ-036, F-EW-1457.)

### What set her apart

In a Church that often measured holiness by great deeds, penances, and visions, Thérèse offered a road for people who have none of these. Her road was small acts, done with love and trust (F-OM-043, F-OM-044). A bishop at her process called it a "new way" (F-EW-2450). She lived it without consolation, and she wrote it down while her sense of faith was failing (MQ-025, F-OW-213). She also gave a hidden life to strangers: a murderer, priests, two missionaries she met once or never (F-LF-164, F-EW-486, F-EW-1087). The Church made this enclosed nun patron of all missions (F-OM-087).

### Why is she a famous saint? (one sentence)

Thérèse is a saint because a young French Carmelite, unknown when she died at twenty-four, left a plain account of a "little way" of trust and love, and the Church named that way a new road to holiness, made her patron of the missions, and made her a Doctor of the Church.

## Part 2. Three hooks, the recommended hook, and the thesis

### Hook A. Paradox (RECOMMENDED)

**Draft.** In the summer of 1897 a nun of twenty-four lay in her cell next to the kitchen of the Carmel of Lisieux. At recreation she heard a sister say that the prioress would not know what to write about her after her death. This little sister, the voice said, amiable as she was, had never done anything worth speaking about. The dying nun said she had long been indifferent to what people thought. Not quite twenty-eight years later a pope declared her a saint.

- **Cards.** E-P6-085 (F-EW-1218, F-EW-1215, F-LF-230). The custom of a death circular for each nun: F-LF-230 and E-P7-004. The canonization date: F-OM-071, F-LF-252.
- **Question it opens.** What was there to say about her, and who ended up saying it?
- **Why a modern reader cares.** Most people fear that their life will leave nothing to say. This reader meets a person who heard that verdict about herself.
- **Where the biography answers it.** S15: the book goes out in place of the circular, and the sister who said "not even a good nun" kneels at her feet. S17: the last image.
- **Hedges.** The speaker is unnamed. Laveille joins two remarks that Pauline and others place apart (E-P6-085, E-P6-234). Write "a sister." Use Taylor's English (public domain) for the words.

### Hook B. Scene at the edge

**Draft.** On the morning of 20 November 1887 a fourteen-year-old girl from Normandy knelt before Pope Leo XIII in the Vatican. Her vicar general had forbidden every pilgrim to speak to him. She put her hands on the old man's knees and asked for something no girl of her age could have: leave to enter a convent at fifteen. In her own later account, two guards were already reaching for her arms.

- **Cards.** E-P3-034 (F-LF-179, F-OW-076, F-OW-363, F-EW-435, F-EW-436).
- **Question.** Will the Pope say yes?
- **Why a reader cares.** A teenager defies an order for what she wants.
- **Where answered.** S6, about 2,900 words into the text. That is too early for a long pull.
- **Hedge.** The guards come from Ms A (1895). Her same-evening letter (LT 36) does not mention them (F-OW-362). Say "in her later account."

### Hook C. Broken picture

**Draft.** Her mother wrote that the little girl "flies into dreadful furies" and "rolls on the floor in despair as if all were lost." At three, told that one must die to go to heaven, she wished her mother dead. At night her sisters tied her into bed with cords. The world knows her as the Little Flower. The edited book that made her famous left the furies out.

- **Cards.** E-P1-061 (F-LF-062, F-LF-063, F-OW-003, F-OW-004), E-P1-063 (F-LF-070, F-OW-005).
- **Question.** How did this child become that saint?
- **Why a reader cares.** Any parent of a difficult child. Any reader who thinks saints are born sweet.
- **Where answered.** S5 (Christmas 1886) and S9 (the fight goes on in Carmel).
- **Hedge.** Say "the edited text." The 1898 and 1911 texts differ from each other. Check which one cut each line (F-OW-004, F-OW-005).

### Why Hook A

- It carries the whole shape of the life: obscurity, then fame.
- It pays off twice: at the book (S15) and at the end (S17).
- It rests on an admission against interest. The Church's own advocates admitted that several nuns "were mistaken about her" (F-EW-1215).
- It uses her real reaction: she is not hurt. That makes the reader curious about her.
- Hook C is the best material to place inside S3 and S5. Do not spend it in the opening.
- A spare: her own line "If there is a Heaven, it is for me" (F-OW-470). Keep it for S12.

### Thesis (draft, three sentences)

Thérèse Martin entered a French Carmel at fifteen, wrote the story of her soul in school notebooks because her prioress ordered it, and died of tuberculosis at twenty-four. The Church canonized her in 1925 and named her a Doctor of the Church in 1997. Its popes said she had found a simple road to holiness that needs no great deeds, and she wrote it down while her own sense of faith was failing.

- **Cards.** F-OM-098 (Doctor, 1997), F-OM-071 (canonization), F-OM-043 (decree: "a special kind of holiness"), F-OM-044 (Benedict XV), F-OW-213 (the trial), F-OW-217 (Ms B).
- **Hardship hint.** The illness of her father and her own illness shape the rest of the story.
- **Move back to the life.** Pull forward: "Her father had once wanted to be a monk. At the Great St Bernard a prior asked whether he had finished his Latin." (E-P1-011, F-LF-008.)

## S2 — "A Midnight Wedding in Alençon" (1843–1874)
- Subheadlines: none.
- Narrative job: show who her parents were, why four children were buried, and how a baby lived who should not have.
- Stakes: Louis and Zélie both wanted the cloister and were refused. Nine children were born and four died. The ninth could not be nursed by her sick mother.
- Opposition: illness and death. Wet nurses who starved babies (F-EW-039).
- What set them apart here: Zélie ran the lace business herself and won a jury's praise for "intelligent direction" (E-P1-014). Both parents went to the 5:30 a.m. Mass daily (F-EW-016). Louis closed his shop on Sundays at a loss (F-LF-024).
- Foreshadowing: Zélie's wish that all her daughters be Carmelites (F-LF-024). The aunt's letter about a coffin (F-LF-042). The nurse Rose Taillé (later: hens as a gift, F-LF-077).
- Pull forward: end on Zélie's letter of 1 June 1874, "I think she will be the last" (F-LF-052), and on the child in her sky-blue outfit (F-LF-051). The reader then wants to meet the child.
- Moment worth sharing: Zélie walks alone eight kilometres at dawn to fetch a wet nurse. "Even though they should kill me, I do not care" (F-LF-043, F-EW-051, F-EW-065).
- Key scenes:
  1. Louis is turned away at the Great St Bernard for lack of Latin (E-P1-011). Zélie is refused by the Sisters of Charity (E-P1-013). They marry at midnight, 13 July 1858 (F-LF-022).
  2. Two coffin scenes: white roses on the small coffin (F-EW-031). The aunt's letter: if Zélie will not change the baby's name, she should "have a coffin made" (F-LF-042). Then the walk to Semallé (E-P1-044, E-P1-045).
  3. The baby in Rose Taillé's wheelbarrow among the hay (E-P1-049, F-LF-044).
- Details to use:
  - Louis kept coins in a dried orange peel on his workbench (F-EW-019). A devout lady, Félicité Baudouin, paid his first shop costs (F-EW-019).
  - All nine children carry the name Marie (F-EW-020, F-EW-023).
  - Ten-year-old Marie saw the first baby Thérèse starve at a nurse's and spoke too late (F-EW-039).
  - Zélie on the four deaths: people said "better they had never been born." She could not agree (F-LF-029).
  - The baptism came 36 hours after birth, because the family waited for a fourteen-year-old godfather. Zélie was in anguish (F-EW-061).
  - "Must I lose this one too?" (F-LF-041).
  - The black bread of the farm, which the older girls begged for (F-EW-055).
  - She hid and screamed at a fashionably dressed visitor, but kissed country women dressed like her nurse (F-LF-049).
- People: Louis (49), Zélie (41), Marie (godmother, 12), Sr Marie-Dosithée (Zélie's sister, Visitation nun at Le Mans), Rose Taillé, Abbé Dumaine, the maid Louise Marais.
- Featured quotations: Zélie's "Faudra-t-il encore perdre celle-là ?" (F-LF-041; letter of Jan–Mar 1873; public domain; use the dossier translation and check the French in the card).
- Context to explain: Point d'Alençon is an expensive hand-made lace (E-P1-014). Babies of working families were often sent to a wet nurse (F-EW-039).
- New to English readers: the aunt's coffin letter (F-LF-042); the 36-hour baptism delay (F-EW-061); Louis and Latin (F-LF-008); the Baudouin loan (F-EW-019).
- Threads: T2.
- Uncertain or disputed: hour of birth (11 p.m., 11:30 p.m., about midnight; say "late on 2 January"); which saint saved her (Joseph or Francis de Sales; say the family disagreed); Louis's visits to the Great St Bernard (Laveille: one; Carmel: two); return from Semallé (use 2 April 1874, from the letters).
- Target length: about 450 words.

## S3 — "The Child Who Chose Everything" (1874–1877)
- Subheadlines: only if needed: "Tied to the Bed"; "A Doctor's Verdict"; "Pauline Will Be Mama". Prefer none.

- Stakes: a four-year-old loses the person she is most attached to. A mother tells the girls what she has.
- Opposition: Zélie's tumour and doctors who could do nothing. Louis's collapse. The child's own temper.

## S4 — "The Hat Like a Black Beast" (1877–1883)
- Subheadlines: none.
- Narrative job: an ordinary childhood in Lisieux breaks into an illness no one understands, and a cure she then doubts.
- Stakes: at nine she loses her second mother. At ten she falls ill. Later she fears she lied.
- Opposition: the illness; a doctor who admits he understands nothing; the nuns' questions that turn her small memory into a story she cannot control (F-OW-041).
- What set her apart here: she does not call the cure a miracle. She says the Virgin "seemed" beautiful (Ms A). She wept, she told Marie, "because I could not see her any more" (F-EW-285, F-EW-294). In 1897 she says of the same statue: "That other day it was not the statue" (F-EW-296).
- Foreshadowing: the veiled figure she saw in the garden at six or seven (E-P2-048; F-EW-157, F-OW-028; opens loop L4). Dr Notta will later sign the papers for Louis's committal (F-LF-093 note). Gonzague first appears (July 1882).
- Pull forward: "She told no one about her fear of having lied. It lasted four years" (F-OW-041, F-OW-042).
- Moment worth sharing: in the worst crisis she cries "They want to poison me." Then the two tears, and why (E-P2-089: F-EW-293, F-EW-285).
- Key scenes:
  1. 2 October 1882: Pauline enters Carmel. In the parlour Thérèse says "Look, Pauline, I have on the little petticoat you made me." Marie later sees tears in her eyes (E-P2-081, F-EW-235).
  2. The illness: her father's hat looks like "the big black beast." The showers she hated. Louis leaves in tears (E-P2-085: F-EW-250, F-EW-262).
  3. The cure, 13 May 1883: three prayers before the statue, the radiant look, the two tears (E-P2-089).
- Details to use:
  - The "postulant of nine": in July 1882 she slips away from her cousin to tell the prioress, Marie de Gonzague, that she wants Carmel. A sister jokes she must eat "many plates of soup" first (E-P2-080, F-EW-240).

  - Nails on the wall looked like "big charred fingers" (F-EW-249).

  - Léonie, in the room, buried her head and saw nothing (F-EW-295, F-EW-297).
  - Uncle Isidore, a trained pharmacist, said no one had seen such a case (F-EW-256).

- People: Louis (called her his "little queen"), Marie (nurse and witness), Léonie, Céline, Pauline, Gonzague, Dr Notta, Isidore Guérin, the maid Marcelline Husé.
- Featured quotations: none.
- Context to explain: the Carmel is enclosed. Families see a nun only at the parlour grille, and Thérèse had "two or three minutes" (F-OW-036). The abbey school was a Benedictine day school for girls of Lisieux.

## S5 — "The Night the Tears Stopped" (1884–1887)
- Subheadlines: none.
- Narrative job: the oversensitive girl who cannot stop crying changes in one night, and at once turns her attention outward.
- Stakes: she is thirteen. Her sisters have left. Her father is tired. A second refusal by Marie leaves her alone with scruples.
- Opposition: her own tears; Marie's fear that God would take her too soon (F-EW-305).

## Part 7. Accuracy notes for the writer

**Sources and editing**
- Pauline edited Ms A, the letters, and the book (1898, 1911). Quote the manuscripts and dated letters, not Laveille or Taylor (F-LF-241). Ms A and C were re-addressed to Gonzague in 1898. Marie restored the original in April 1910 (E-P7-002).
- The 1911 edition redates and merges letters and cuts humor and family conflict (gaps §13). Example: the farewell letters of 9 June and 13 July 1897 appear on 14 August and 26 July (E-P6-165).
- The Yellow Notebook (Carnet jaune) was recopied by Pauline in 1921–23 from a destroyed 1904–05 notebook. Four versions exist (306, 275, 362, and 714 sayings). Say this once (S12). Pauline scratched out the end of a saying of 16 July 1897 (F-OW-263). Sayings that give her authority over the writings are the least safe (F-OW-223).
- The sisters shaped their process testimony. Marie's letter of 13 March 1915 says they will "set right" the lawyer's portrait, and she swears no one influenced her (F-EW-1976, F-EW-1981). Céline prepared from notes. Judges asked leading questions about her "nervous temperament" (F-EW-2519). Write "her sister testified."
- Pauline and Céline say Thérèse distrusted Diana Vaughan. Her own play shows she believed (F-EW-992, F-OW-572). The photograph link rests on the Carmel editors' chronicle only (F-OW-578).
- Céline's and Marie's claims on Gonzague and the last illness must be checked against Gaucher. Editors flag them (F-EW-508).

**Dates and numbers**
- Birth: 2 January 1873, late evening (hour disputed). Baptism 4 January 1873 (not 2 April 1874, which is wrong on English Wikipedia; F-LF-040, plan §1).
- Mother's death: 28 August 1877, about 12:30 a.m. Move to Lisieux: mid-November 1877 (14, 15, or 16).
- Pauline enters Carmel 2 October 1882. Cure 13 May 1883 (Pentecost). First Communion 8 May 1884. Confirmation 14 June 1884. Marie enters 15 October 1886.
- Pentecost 1887 was 29 May. Pranzini was executed 31 August 1887. Bayeux 31 October 1887. Audience 20 November 1887. Entry 9 April 1888 at 15 years 3 months. Clothing 10 January 1889. Profession 8 September 1890. Veil 24 September 1890.
- Louis: committed 12 February 1889; the Carmel says he left the Bon-Sauveur on 10 May 1892; he died 29 July 1894.

- Holy Thursday 1896 was 2 April, so the first blood was the night of 2–3 April. Easter 1897 fell on 18 April. Taxil's press conference was Easter Monday, 19 April 1897. Ms B is dated 8 September 1896. Ms C begins 3–4 June 1897.
- Infirmary 8 July 1897. Extreme Unction 30 July. Last Communion 19 August. Death about 7:20 p.m. on 30 September 1897, aged 24 years 9 months. Burial 4 October 1897.

Miracles decree 11 February 1923 (not 13). Beatification 29 April 1923. Canonization 17 May 1925. Missions 14 December 1927. France 3 May 1944. Doctor 19 October 1997. Basilica blessed 11 July 1937 (not 1936).
