One day in Ávila, when Saint Teresa was about seven years old, she and her brother Rodrigo took a little food and left their father's house. They went out through the Adaja gate and over the bridge, on the road toward the land of the Moors.1

The children did not get far. An uncle met them and brought them home to their mother, who had people searching everywhere because she feared the children had fallen into the well. Rodrigo had his excuse ready: she had made him take that road.2

Many years later Saint Teresa wrote down why they had gone. The two children had been reading the lives of the saints together.

"I had one brother almost my age. [...] When I read of the martyrdoms the saints suffered for God, it seemed to me that they paid very little to go and enjoy God. [...] We agreed to go to the land of the Moors, begging for the love of God, so that they would cut off our heads there. [...] Having parents seemed to us the greatest obstacle. It frightened us very much to read that pain and glory last forever. [...] We liked to say it over and over: 'Forever, forever, forever!'"

— Saint Teresa of Jesus, The Book of Her Life, chapter 13

It had not been love, and she did not pretend it had. She wrote that she did not yet love God, as far as she could tell. She wanted to enjoy the good things of heaven, and to enjoy them soon. But the word "forever" stayed with her. She wrote that through that word the way of truth was printed on her as a child. When they could not go to the Moors, the two of them tried to be hermits instead. They piled up little stones to make cells in the garden, and the stones kept falling down.4

That was the wish of her whole life: to go and enjoy God, and to enjoy Him forever. Yet for nearly twenty years she could not live it, because her heart was divided between God and the world. It was a long road from the Adaja bridge.5

Saint Teresa Leaves Her Father's House

Saint Teresa was born in Ávila on 28 March 1515. Her name was Teresa de Cepeda y Ahumada. Her father was Alonso Sánchez de Cepeda, and her mother Beatriz de Ahumada was his second wife. He had three children by his first marriage and nine by his second, so the house was full. Rodrigo would die years later as a captain in the Río de la Plata.6

As a girl she kept a picture of Christ with the Samaritan woman at the well. Under it were the words "Lord, give me water." She remembered that she was about twelve, or a little less, when her mother died. In her grief she went to an image of Our Lady and begged her with many tears to be her mother.7

The child who had wanted to die for God grew into a young woman who liked to be liked. She read romances about knights and ladies. She also shared small vanities with a relative, and the friendship worried her father and her sister. In 1531 her half-sister María married and left home. Her father then sent Saint Teresa to board with the Augustinian nuns of Santa María de Gracia in Ávila.8

One of those nuns spoke so well of God that Saint Teresa loved to listen to her. Little by little her thoughts turned back to eternal things, and her great dislike of becoming a nun grew weaker. Still she did not want to be one. After a year and a half a serious illness sent her home. When she was well again she went to visit her married sister in the country. On the way she stayed a few days with an uncle who read good books and talked of God. There she understood again the truth she had learned as a child: that all things pass, and the world is nothing.9

For three months she fought with herself. She admitted that fear moved her more than love. She argued that the hardships of a nun could not be worse than purgatory, and that afterward she would go straight to heaven, "which was my desire." The letters of Saint Jerome gave her the courage to tell her father. She felt that telling him was almost like taking the nun's habit, because she never went back on her word. But he loved her too much to agree, and he would not listen even when others pleaded for her. The most he would grant was that she could do as she liked after his death. She feared her own weakness and would not wait that long.10

She had already persuaded her brother Antonio to become a friar. The two of them agreed to set out early one morning, and she told no one else. On All Souls' Day, 2 November 1535, her brother walked with her from their father's house to the Carmelite convent of the Incarnation. She was twenty years and seven months old.11

"I remember clearly, and it is true, that the grief I felt when I left my father's house will not, I think, be greater when I die. It felt as if every bone in me was pulling away from the others. No love of God had yet taken away my love for my father and family, and the pull was so strong that, without the Lord's help, my own reasons would not have carried me on. There He gave me courage against myself, and I did it."

— Saint Teresa of Jesus, The Book of Her Life, chapter 412

The moment she put on the habit, the pain turned to joy. She wrote that the happiness of being a nun came to her at once and never left her, up to the day she wrote those words. Her soul had found its home, but her body did not agree. Within the first year her health broke, with fainting fits and a weak heart.13

Saint Teresa Nearly Dies and Cannot Walk

No doctor could help her, so her father brought her home to his house in Ávila. The doctors gave up hope for her. When the August feast of Our Lady came, Saint Teresa asked to make her confession. Her father thought she asked only out of fear of death, and he would not allow it. That night she fell into a fit and lay without her senses for almost four days.14

They gave her the sacrament of the sick. Every hour they thought she was dying. They kept saying the Creed over her as if she could understand anything. At times they were so sure she was dead that she found wax on her eyes afterward. At the Incarnation her grave stood open for a day and a half, and friars elsewhere held her funeral rites.15

Her father would not let them bury her. He felt her pulse and said, "This daughter is not for burying." He grieved bitterly that he had not let her confess. Her brother Lorenzo sat up with her one night and fell asleep. A candle burned down and set the pillows and covers alight, and the smoke woke him. At last the Lord was pleased to bring her back to herself. She asked at once to confess, and she received Holy Communion with many tears.16

She was alive, and she could not move. Her tongue was bitten to pieces. Her throat was so dry that she could not swallow even water. She could move one finger of her right hand and nothing else, so two people shifted her in a sheet, one at each end. As soon as she could, she had herself carried back to her convent just as she was. "They had been waiting for a dead woman," she wrote, "and they received one with a living soul, but with a body worse than dead, painful to see." She was nothing but bones.17

She lay like that for more than eight months, and she was crippled for almost three years. A nun of the Incarnation later told the Church what she had heard: that Saint Teresa brought joy to everyone who nursed her. She left one line about the day she could first crawl: "When I began to crawl on my hands and knees, I praised God."18

The doctors of the earth had failed her, so she turned to the doctors of heaven. She took Saint Joseph as her advocate and lord. She wrote that he had been the guardian of Jesus on earth, and that in heaven Jesus does whatever he asks. In her own sharp way she told what followed: "He did what he does. He made it possible for me to get up and walk and not be crippled. And I did what I do. I misused the favour."19

Saint Teresa Wavers Between God and the World

The Incarnation was a large and easy house, and its parlour was open to visitors. Such visits were the custom in many convents, and she told herself they would do her no more harm than they did the other nuns. People came to talk with her for her good grace and her wit. She wept over that for the rest of her life.20

One day in the parlour she was talking with someone she had only begun to know. Christ appeared before her with great severity and made her understand that this friendship displeased Him. She saw Him with the eyes of the soul, more clearly than she could have seen Him with the eyes of her body. More than twenty-six years later she seemed to see Him still. Badly shaken, she did not want to see that person again.21

It did not last. The devil helped her believe she had imagined it, though deep down she felt it had come from God. "But as it was not to my liking," she wrote, "I made myself deny it." She was urged to come back, and assured that such visits brought her honour and did her no harm. So she went back to the same friend, and in time to others. No other friend drew her heart away like this one, because she loved that person very much.22

Another day the two of them saw a creature like a large toad coming toward them, much faster than toads move. It was the middle of the day, and she could not understand how such a thing could have come from that place. She never forgot it. "Oh greatness of God," she wrote, "with what care and pity You were warning me in every way, and how little good it did me!" An old nun of the house, a relative of hers, warned her too. Saint Teresa thought the old nun was shocked over nothing, and she was annoyed with her.23

Worse followed. For a year and more she gave up mental prayer, the quiet prayer of the heart. She told herself it was humility. Later she called it the greatest temptation she ever had.24

There was a sad twist in it. Because she loved her father so much, she had wanted him to have the good she found in prayer. She had given him books, and in five or six years he went so far in prayer that she praised God for it. He came often to the convent to talk with her about God. Now she could not bear to let him think she still prayed as before. She told him she had stopped, but not why. She blamed her illness. He never told a lie himself, so he believed her, and he felt sorry for her. Since he had risen so high in prayer, he no longer stayed long. Once he had seen her he went away, saying it was time lost. "As I was wasting it on other vanities," she wrote, "I cared little."25

At the end of 1543 her father fell ill. Saint Teresa went home to nurse him. She was ill herself, and she wrote that she was sicker in soul than he was in body.26

"I suffered much in his illness. I think I gave him back a little of what he had suffered in mine. Though I was very ill, I forced myself. In losing him I was losing all my good and all my comfort, because he was all of it to me. Yet I found such courage that I did not show him my grief, and until he died I behaved as if I felt nothing. When I saw his life ending, it seemed to me that my soul was being torn out, because I loved him very much."

— Saint Teresa of Jesus, The Book of Her Life, chapter 727

His worst pain was in his shoulders, and it never left him. She reminded him how he loved to think of the Lord carrying the cross on His shoulders. She told him that perhaps God wanted him to feel a little of that pain. He was so comforted that she did not think she ever heard him complain again. He lay three days without his senses. On the day he died the Lord gave him back his mind so fully that those around him were amazed. He was saying the Creed himself, and in the middle of it he died. "He lay there like an angel," she wrote.28

His confessor was a learned Dominican, and Saint Teresa began to confess to him too. He showed her the danger she was in. When she told him about her prayer, he said she must never leave it, because it could only do her good. She went back to it, and this time she never gave it up. But she did not give up the world either. "On one side God was calling me," she wrote. "On the other, I was following the world."29

She called her life in those days a stormy sea, and she sailed it for nearly twenty years. She would fall, and rise badly, and fall again. When she was enjoying the world, the memory of what she owed God hurt her. When she was with God, her love of the world gave her no rest. The child who had wanted to enjoy God forever summed up those years in one line: "I did not enjoy God, and I was not content in the world."30

Saint Teresa Weeps Before the Wounded Christ

By now she was nearly forty, and she was worn out. "I wanted to live," she wrote, "for I knew well that I was not living. I was fighting with a shadow of death. There was no one to give me life, and I could not take it for myself." Her soul was tired, but her old ways would not let it rest. The turn came in Lent, about 1554 or 1555. One day she went into the small chapel of the convent and saw an image that had been brought there for a feast.31

"It showed Christ covered with wounds, so moving that when I looked at it, it shook me to see Him so, because it showed well what He suffered for us. I felt so strongly how badly I had thanked Him for those wounds that my heart seemed to break. I threw myself down beside Him, weeping hard, begging Him to give me strength once and for all not to offend Him. [...] I think I told Him then that I would not get up from there until He did what I asked."

— Saint Teresa of Jesus, The Book of Her Life, chapter 932

She believed it helped her, for from then on she grew much better. This time she had put no trust in herself and all her trust in God. She could not meditate by reasoning, so she tried to picture Christ within her. She did best with the moments when He was most alone. In the garden of His agony she kept Him company. She wished she could wipe away His painful sweat, but she never dared, because her sins seemed so great.33

About the same time someone gave her the Confessions of Saint Augustine. She had not asked for the book, and she took that as the Lord's doing. When she read how Saint Augustine heard a voice in the garden and gave himself to God, she wept for a long time.34

Out of all those years came her plainest teaching about prayer. She wrote that mental prayer is nothing other than a friendship. It means often spending time alone with the One who we know loves us. That was what she had been missing in the parlour. She had found a Friend, and soon He began to answer.35

Saint Teresa's Visions Frighten Her Confessors

The graces she now received in prayer troubled her, for she was afraid of being deceived. She asked two men she trusted for their judgement, and she waited for it in great fear. At last one of them came and told her what they both thought: it was the devil. "It gave me such fear and pain that I did not know what to do," she wrote. "All I could do was weep." She was in great distress in a small chapel when she read some words of Saint Paul in a book. It seemed to her that the Lord had put the book into her hands. The words said that God is faithful, and that He never lets those who love Him be deceived by the devil. That comforted her very much.36

Later she spoke with Saint Francis Borgia, who had once been a duke and was now a Jesuit. He told her the visions were the work of God, and she was greatly consoled. On the feast of Saint Peter in 1557 she felt Christ close beside her. She saw nothing with her eyes, but she knew He was there.37

One thing still held her back: friendships she could not bring herself to give up. She did not think she offended God by them, and breaking them off seemed ungrateful. Her Jesuit confessor saw how attached she was and did not dare to order her to end them. He told her to pray the hymn Veni Creator, "Come, Creator Spirit," for a few days. One day, after long prayer, she began the hymn, and a rapture came over her so suddenly that it almost took her out of herself. It was the first rapture the Lord ever gave her. She heard these words: "I no longer want you to talk with men, but with angels." The words frightened her and comforted her. From that day she had the courage to leave everything for God. She could never again settle into a friendship except with people who loved Him.38

Her fear of deception stayed with her. She trusted learned confessors more than great mystics, and the harder her Dominican confessor Domingo Báñez was with her, the more she wanted his advice. When he began to relax, she begged him not to. "For the love of God, Father, do not be so free of fear. You make me afraid. Look again. I would not want to deceive you."39

About 1559 she saw an angel in bodily form at her left side. She rarely saw angels that way.

"He was not large but small, very beautiful, his face so aflame that he seemed one of the highest angels, who seem all on fire. [...] I saw in his hands a long golden spear, and at the iron's tip there seemed to be a little fire. He seemed to thrust it several times through my heart, down to my entrails. When he drew it out, it seemed he took them with him and left me all on fire with great love of God. The pain was so great that it made me moan, and the sweetness of this great pain so excessive that one cannot wish it to end, and the soul is content with nothing less than God."

— Saint Teresa of Jesus, The Book of Her Life, chapter 2940

She called it a caress of love between the soul and God. For anyone who doubted her she had one prayer: "I ask His goodness to let anyone who thinks I am lying taste it." For days afterward she walked about as if dazed. She wanted to see no one and speak to no one, only to embrace her pain. Soon her raptures grew too strong to hide in front of other people. They began to be talked about, to her great distress.41

Some time after these graces she was praying one day when she found herself plunged into hell in a moment. She understood that the Lord wanted her to see the place the devils had ready for her, the place she had deserved by her sins. The way in was a long and narrow passage like a low, dark furnace. The ground was foul mud full of vermin. At the end was a hollow in the wall, and she saw herself shut inside it. It lasted only a moment, but she knew she could never forget it.42

After that she longed to do something for God. She thought the first thing was to keep her own Rule as perfectly as she could. At the Incarnation the Rule was kept in its eased form, the nuns often went out, and the house was large and pleasant. She herself went out most of all, because important people asked for her company. One day someone suggested that a few of them could live like the barefoot nuns, in a small house of their own. A widowed friend shared the wish, and they began to talk of it. But Saint Teresa held back. "I was so very happy in the house where I was," she wrote, "because it was much to my liking, and so was my cell."43

One day after Holy Communion the Lord told her to work for it with all her strength. The house would be called Saint Joseph's, He said. Saint Joseph would guard one door and Our Lady the other, and Christ would be among them. It would be a star that gave great light. She suffered when she heard it. She could see some of the trouble it would cost, and she loved the house where she lived. God had given her all these graces in that large and easy convent. Now He was asking her to leave it.44

Saint Teresa Founds a Poor Convent in Ávila

The plan had to be kept secret. Her sister Juana and Juana's husband Juan de Ovalle bought a small house in Ávila as if for themselves. Four poor women took the habit there without any dowry on 24 August 1562, the feast of Saint Bartholomew. A priest named Gaspar Daza gave them the habit and placed the Blessed Sacrament in the house. Two of Saint Teresa's cousins from the Incarnation were there with a handful of friends.45

She felt as if she were in heaven. Three or four hours later a storm of doubt broke over her. Had she disobeyed her superior, the Provincial? Could these women bear so hard a life? Had she done something foolish? She went before the Blessed Sacrament and could not pray. She wrote that she was like someone in the agony of death. At last she promised God to ask leave to live in the new house herself, and at that moment the devil fled and left her calm.46

The news ran through the city. That same day the prioress of the Incarnation sent for her. Saint Teresa went back at once and left the new nuns in distress. She expected to be put in prison. When the Provincial came, she went to be judged, glad to suffer something for the Lord. She thought of how Christ had been judged, and her own trial seemed nothing. "I admitted my fault as if I were very guilty," she wrote. The Provincial rebuked her severely. She did not try to excuse herself, and she asked him to forgive her and punish her. Next she was told to explain herself before the nuns, and no one found cause to blame her. Afterward she spoke to the Provincial alone, and he was satisfied.47

The city was not. Two or three days later the governor, the city council, and the cathedral chapter met and resolved that the convent must not exist. Every religious order in the city sent two learned men. Most of them were silent or spoke against the house. One Dominican said that the matter belonged to the bishop, and that the house should be looked into, not dissolved. For two days she was in distress, until Christ spoke to her: "Do you not know that I am almighty? What are you afraid of?" He assured her that the house would not be undone.48

The city took its case to the royal court. There was no money for a lawsuit, and she did not know what to do. The Provincial would not yet let her go to the new house. The four nuns were alone there, and she thought their prayers did more than all her efforts. Sometimes everything seemed lost. The day before a visit from the Provincial, the prioress ordered her to have nothing more to do with the matter. That meant giving it all up.49

"I turned to God and said: Lord, this house is not mine. It was made for You. Now that there is no one left to plead for it, You do it. I was left as much at rest and free of worry as if the whole world were pleading for me, and at once I took the matter as safe."

— Saint Teresa of Jesus, The Book of Her Life, chapter 3650

Someone did plead for it. At a great meeting a priest sent by the bishop stood alone against everyone and won a short delay. He was the same priest who had given the nuns the habit. The attack went on for about six months and then died away. In time the Provincial let her live at Saint Joseph's, and from then on she called herself Teresa of Jesus.51

She wrote the house's rules, which the Church calls Constitutions. The nuns would give two hours a day to mental prayer and live on alms and the work of their hands. Their beds were straw sacks, "since weak and unhealthy people have tried it and it can be done." The sick were another matter: "Let the healthy go without what they need before the sick go without some kindness." For them she wanted good beds, "I mean mattresses." She allowed no games. "The Lord will give some sisters the grace to entertain the others," she wrote, "and on that ground all the time is well spent."52

She had finished the first draft of her Life in June 1562, and she kept reworking it. Her confessor Báñez told her to write a book for her nuns, and she wrote The Way of Perfection at Saint Joseph's. In April 1567 the head of the whole Carmelite Order came to Ávila. Father Rubeo saw the house, and she showed him its Constitutions. He told her she should found as many monasteries as she had hairs on her head.53

Saint John of the Cross Joins the Reform

Her second house was at Medina del Campo in 1567. There she met a young Carmelite friar who was studying at Salamanca. Saint John of the Cross was then called John of Saint Matthias, and he was thinking of leaving to become a Carthusian monk. Saint Teresa told him what she hoped to do, and she asked him to wait until she had a house for friars. He promised, as long as it did not take too long. She was very glad. Now she had "two living stones" for the house she wanted to build for Christ, the Spouse she loved so much. One was this student. The other was Antonio de Heredia, a prior of the older branch who was nearly sixty.54

A gentleman gave her a house in a hamlet called Duruelo, with a decent porch, a small kitchen, and one room with a loft. The head of the Order had allowed her two houses of friars, but only if two superiors of the older branch consented. She told Antonio plainly that they would not give leave if they saw the friars well housed, while in a little hamlet the two would take no notice of them. God had given Antonio more courage than her. He answered that he would live there, and in a pigsty too.55

She took Saint John to her next foundation at Valladolid to learn how her nuns lived. "He was so good," she wrote, "that I, at least, could have learned far more from him than he from me." By God's will the old Provincial was there. She talked to him so much about the account he would give to God if he hindered so good a work that he softened. Then the bishop of Ávila came with his sister, Doña María de Mendoza. Together they won over the former Provincial too, the man she had feared would make every difficulty.56

Saint Teresa gave Saint John the new habit herself. He reached Duruelo at the end of September 1568 and spent the night in prayer. He laid the habit on the altar, blessed it, and put it on. He was the first friar of the reform. Antonio came in November. The first Mass was said on the first Sunday of Advent, 28 November 1568, in a place they called the porch of Bethlehem. Antonio took the name Antonio of Jesus, and Saint John took the name John of the Cross. They went in sandals, so people called them the Discalced, which means unshod. The friars of the older branch were the Calced, the shod. For now the two lived side by side in one Order.57

Saint Teresa saw the house in Lent 1569, on her way to Toledo.

"I arrived one morning. Father Antonio of Jesus was sweeping the church door, with the joyful face he always has. I said to him: 'What is this, Father? What has become of your honour?' He answered, telling me how glad he was: 'I curse the time I had any.' When I went into the little church I was amazed at the spirit the Lord had put there. And I was not alone: two merchants, friends of mine, who had come with me from Medina, did nothing but cry. There were so many crosses, so many skulls!"

— Saint Teresa of Jesus, The Book of the Foundations, chapter 1458

By the holy water stood a small wooden cross with a paper picture of Christ pasted on it. It moved her more than fine carving would have done. The choir was in the attic, so low that the friars had to stoop to hear Mass. Once they went in to morning prayers without noticing that their habits were covered in snow. Antonio had five hour-glasses to keep the hours, and that made her laugh.59

She asked them not to be so hard on themselves, and they made light of her advice. She wrote that God's gift in founding those friars was greater than His gift in founding her houses of nuns. From Duruelo she went on toward Toledo with almost nothing in her purse.60

Saint Teresa Founds Convents with Almost Nothing

At Toledo her licence was held up for more than two months. In the end she went to a church near the house of the official who could grant it and asked to speak with him. She told him it was hard that women who wanted to live a strict life should be stopped by people who lived at ease. She wrote that she spoke with a certain firmness. He gave her the licence before she left the church.61

She had perhaps three or four ducats. There was nothing to put on the altar, so she bought two pictures painted on canvas, and then two straw mattresses and a blanket. "I left very glad," she wrote, "for it seemed I had everything while I had nothing." A poor student found her a house and brought her the keys, and at nightfall the nuns moved in. On the first day they did not have even a dry leaf to roast a sardine. When gifts came soon after, the nuns grew sad. "What should be wrong, Mother?" they answered when she asked them why. "We no longer seem to be poor." She felt the same. She wrote that it was as if she had owned many gold jewels and been robbed of them.62

On the eve of Pentecost that year Saint Teresa was at table in Toledo, and she could hardly eat for the joy in her soul. A servant arrived from the Princess of Éboli, the wife of one of the most powerful men at the royal court. The Princess wanted a convent in her town of Pastrana. Saint Teresa refused to go. But when she prayed, Christ told her she was going for something more than that foundation. In Madrid on the way she won two hermits for her reform. One of them was Fray Juan de la Miseria.63

Saint Teresa stayed at Pastrana three months. The Princess insisted on things that did not belong in the Order, and Saint Teresa was ready to leave rather than agree. Ruy Gómez de Silva, the Princess's husband, calmed her at last. Worse was to come. The Princess found out that Saint Teresa had written an account of her own soul, the story of her sins and of God's mercies to her. She insisted on reading it. She laughed at it and left it lying about for her servants. Carmelite histories say that they talked, and the Inquisition came to hear of the book. Before Saint Teresa left, she ordered that every gift the Princess made to the convent be written down with the date and the prioress's signature.64

Early the next year she wrote to Lorenzo, the brother who had fallen asleep by her bed, now in Peru. "I have become such a bargainer and business woman," she told him, "from having to do with these houses and the Order, that I know how to deal with such matters." She also said she had not felt so well for forty years.65

She arrived at Salamanca in 1570 with only one companion. The house had been full of students, who had to be coaxed out. The two nuns slept on straw while the bells tolled for All Souls. Her companion kept looking around the room. "Mother," she said, "I am thinking. If I were to die now, what would you do alone?" Saint Teresa answered, "Sister, when that happens I will think about what to do. Now let me sleep." She disliked travelling, especially on long roads. As far as she remembered, she never held back from a foundation out of fear of the trouble.66

Saint Teresa Returns to the Incarnation

In 1571 her superiors sent her back to the Incarnation as its prioress. She had thought she would never return. The great convent was so poor that it could not give its nuns enough to eat. The Pope's visitor, a Dominican, could think of no help for it but her. Saint Teresa did not want to go, and the Lord rebuked her for holding back before she gave in. The nuns did not want her either. They had not chosen her, and they feared she would end their pastimes and add to their penances.67

While Saint Teresa knelt before the Blessed Sacrament, the visitor's letter was read to the nuns, and the house was in an uproar. Some said they would never obey her, and others heaped insults on her. Others took up the cross and went out to receive her, singing the Te Deum. At the first meeting of the community, the nuns found an image of Our Lady in the prioress's seat, with Saint Teresa sitting at her feet. From then on they obeyed her. She gave the care of their souls to Saint John of the Cross.68

In 1573 she went back to Salamanca, and a Jesuit confessor there told her to write the story of her houses. In that book she told her nuns where to look for God: "The Lord walks among the pots and pans, helping you inside and out."69

That autumn she moved the Salamanca nuns to a better house on the eve of Saint Michael, with a Mass and a sermon announced for the feast. Heavy rain poured through the new chapel roof. "I tell you, daughters," she wrote, "I found myself very imperfect that day." After eight that evening Blessed Anne of Jesus, one of the nuns, asked her to beg God to stop the rain. Saint Teresa told her, "Ask Him yourself, if you think He will do it so fast because I say so." Blessed Anne of Jesus went out and saw a clear sky full of stars. "Your Reverence could have asked God for this sooner," she said, and Saint Teresa went off to her cell laughing.70

At Pastrana, meanwhile, the Princess of Éboli had lost her husband. Ruy Gómez died on 29 July 1573, and in the storm of her grief the Princess decided at once to become a nun. Word came ahead of her in the night. "The Princess a nun? The monastery is lost," cried the prioress. The Princess took the lowest seat at table, but she kept her own maids and asked what the friars had to do with her monastery. At last she moved into a hermitage in the garden and received her friends there in a nun's habit. Saint Teresa wrote of it all very mildly. She said that the devil had sent the Princess there, or perhaps the Lord had allowed it, and God knew why.71

Saint Teresa pleaded with her superiors to move the convent, and they agreed. In 1574 the prioress called in the town magistrate and a notary. She handed back every gift the Princess had ever made and took a written receipt. At midnight the nuns left Pastrana with nothing but the beds and small mats they had brought with them. They reached the new house at Segovia in Holy Week. A bishop followed them there and threatened a lawsuit over the gifts. The prioress showed him the receipt, and he said no more. "I felt the greatest joy in the world," Saint Teresa wrote, "to see them at peace."72

In October 1574 her three years at the Incarnation ended. Many of the nuns wanted to elect her again, though not all of them did. She longed to go home to Saint Joseph's, and she went.73

The book came back to trouble her. Early in 1575 she was at Veas when a messenger came from the bishop who had protected Saint Joseph's and from her nuns. Officers of the Inquisition were searching for her Life. Báñez took it to the Inquisition in Madrid himself, and he wrote his judgement on it on 7 July 1575. The book stayed in the Inquisition's keeping for about twelve years. For years she could only wait, not knowing how they judged it.74

That spring a new superior came to Veas. Jerónimo Gracián was twenty-nine years old and had been a Discalced friar for three years. "He pleased me so much," Saint Teresa wrote, "that I did not think the people who had praised him really knew him at all." In those days she felt such comfort and happiness that she was astonished at herself. Gracián had authority over her and her houses, and he was about to use it.75

Saint Teresa Obeys and Travels to Seville

Gracián told Saint Teresa to ask God whether her next house should be in Madrid or in Seville. She prayed and told him the answer was Madrid. He ordered Seville. Two or three days later he asked her why she had obeyed him and not her revelation. She said she could never be as sure of any revelation as she was of his order, and that she had to obey her superior. He told her to pray again, and this time the answer was Seville.76

She had serious reasons of her own against going south, and she set them all aside. "I gave in at once," she wrote. "Our Lord gives me this grace, that my superiors always seem to me to be right." On the road she made a vow to obey Gracián.77

They left Veas on 18 May 1575, six nuns with her, in covered carriages so hot that going into them was like going into purgatory. On the Guadalquivir the ferry lost its rope and drifted with the carriages on board. "I felt much more pity for the ferryman, he was so distressed, than fear of the danger," she wrote. At Córdoba the carriages would not fit through the bridge gate. "They had to saw them," she wrote, "or I do not know what."78

They reached Seville on 26 May. The archbishop did not allow houses of nuns without an income, and the nuns had one coin left. Saint Teresa was ready to take them back to Veas, and she would have gone with very little regret. The prior of the older branch's house in Seville came with other visitors and asked by what right the convent had been opened. She showed them the letter of the head of the Order but refused them a copy, because she knew it could be used against her. In the end the archbishop came to see her and told her to do as she liked.79

On 19 July 1575 she wrote to King Philip II. She told him she had lived forty years in the Order. She was sure that serious harm would come to the Discalced unless they were made a separate province without delay.80

At Seville she suffered more than at any other house except Ávila, and the trials were inside her. "I never saw myself so faint-hearted and cowardly in my life," she wrote. "Truly, I did not know myself." Her trust in God never left her. But she felt that the Lord was holding back His hand a little, so that she would see that any courage she had ever had was not her own.81

Two things lightened that time. In August 1575 Lorenzo came home from the Indies after more than thirty-four years away, and he brought his children. Gracián also ordered her to let Fray Juan de la Miseria paint her portrait, as one more act of self-denial. When she saw it she laughed, and she told the painter that God should forgive him. After all she had suffered at his hands, he had painted her blear-eyed and ugly.82

Before she left Seville, an order reached her from a general chapter of the Carmelites held in Italy. She was to found no more houses and never to leave the convent she chose to live in. She called it "a kind of prison." Worse, the head of the Order was displeased with her. It was Father Rubeo, who had once wanted her to found as many houses as she had hairs on her head. She wrote that he had no cause but the reports of people carried away by passion, and that this was what gave her pain. Two other charges came with the order, both very grave and both false.83

"I tell you, sisters," she wrote, "far from giving me pain, it gave me a joy so unexpected that I could not contain it." She thought of King David dancing before the ark of the Lord. "I would have wanted to do nothing else then, such was my joy." Except for the General's displeasure, to found no more houses would have been a great rest, a thing she had often wished for: to end her life in quiet. Gracián remembered how she rubbed her palms together for joy when he told her the evil things people said of her. To him it was torment.84

Gracián kept her in Seville until the house was settled. On 3 June 1576 the archbishop carried the Blessed Sacrament into her convent himself. At the end Saint Teresa knelt for his blessing. To her great confusion, he asked for hers.85

Her reform was still part of the one Carmelite Order, under the superiors of the older and easier branch. That branch had begun to move against it. In 1577 Felipe Sega came to Spain as the Pope's new envoy, and he took its side. To him she was "a restless, roving woman."86

Saint John of the Cross Is Seized

In the worst years of the struggle she wrote her calmest book. She began The Interior Castle in Toledo on Trinity Sunday 1577. God had shown her the soul as a castle of clear crystal with seven sets of rooms, and Himself in the innermost room. She finished it at the end of November, and it shows no trace of her troubles. Its advice is simple. To reach the inner rooms, it matters less to think much than to love much.87

That autumn the nuns of the Incarnation elected her their prioress. It was her old convent, the house that had once refused her. The Provincial of the older branch confirmed the nun with fewer votes instead. When more than fifty nuns refused to obey the new prioress, he declared them excommunicated. Saint Teresa had placed Saint John of the Cross and a companion in a small house near the Incarnation to hear the nuns' confessions. On the night of 3 December 1577 friars of the older branch broke into their cells and took their papers. They carried the two men off to their own priory in Ávila. The next day Saint Teresa wrote to the King about the prior of Ávila, Hernando Maldonado.88

"He holds the two fathers prisoner in his priory. He broke open their cells and took their papers. The neighbours are shocked at his boldness: he is not a superior and shows no authority for what he does. [...] These Calced friars seem to fear neither justice nor God. They have long wanted our confessors in their hands, and now they have them. It grieves me greatly. I would rather have seen our fathers held by the Moors, who might show more mercy. This friar, who serves God so fervently, is so weak from all he has suffered that I fear for his life. For the love of our Lord I beg your Majesty to order that the confessors be freed at once and that the Calced friars stop harming the poor Discalced."

— Saint Teresa of Jesus, letter to King Philip II, Ávila, 4 December 157789

She told the King that all Ávila thought Saint John a saint, "as in my opinion he is." He was taken to the older branch's priory in Toledo and shut in a tiny cell. Three days after her letter she wrote to a friend: "What a life to witness all this!"90

On Christmas Eve that year she broke her left arm. Her young companion was now Blessed Ana de San Bartolomé, who understood it as an attack of the devil and never heard her complain. In February Saint Teresa wrote, "My arm is in a bandage like a coat of mail, so that I can hardly use it." She could not use that arm for the rest of her life, and Blessed Ana helped her to dress.91

Saint John was held in his cell at Toledo until the feast of the Assumption in August 1578, when he escaped. Our Lady showed him the window, and he let himself down by a rope that was neither long enough nor strong enough for the drop. He came to Saint Teresa's nuns in Toledo to bring the sacraments to a sick sister. While the friars of the older branch searched for him, he hid in the nuns' infirmary.92

When Saint Teresa learned what he had suffered, she could not stop thinking of it. "I do not know how God can allow such things," she wrote to Gracián. For nine months Saint John had been shut in a cell hardly big enough for him, small as he was, and in all that time he never changed his tunic. "Fray John saw no one during his captivity," she wrote. "I envy him intensely. Thank God for giving him courage for such martyrdom!" Later she heard that he had been allowed to set out at once for Almodóvar, ill as he was. "God grant that death may not deprive us of him," she wrote.93

Under the Pope's envoy the other leaders of the reform were jailed or sent away. That winter Gracián was shut up in a priory of the older branch in Madrid, then sent to Alcalá. Saint Teresa wrote to his mother that for a long while he had begged God to send him crosses. "And what trials they have been! May God's name be praised!" She and her nuns used to read his letters aloud together like sermons. "But the devil is trying to deprive us even of that," she wrote. "However, God is over all!"94

Slowly the storm passed. In March 1580 the Grand Inquisitor of Spain told her that he had read her book and found nothing in it that was not very good. On 22 June 1580 Pope Gregory XIII ordered a separate province for the Discalced. The friars met at Alcalá on 3 March 1581, put the order into effect, and elected Gracián their first Provincial.95

In June 1580 she lost Lorenzo. He had written to her that he felt death was near, and on 19 June she scolded him gently. "I cannot tell how you know that you will die soon, or why you imagine such foolish things," she wrote. "Trust in God, Who is a faithful Friend and will never fail either your children or yourself." Two days after the feast of Saint John the Baptist, he died. A hemorrhage choked him, and he was gone in less than six hours.96

She wrote to the prioress of Seville that the Lord did not seem to want her to be long without suffering. "Life passes so quickly," she went on, "that it seems to me, my daughter, we ought to think more of how to die than how to live." And then, from the woman who had wanted heaven since she was seven: "I am four years older than my brother, yet I never manage to die." She told Lorenzo's son that the loss was felt most by herself and by his sister Teresita. The girl was a novice in her care, and she bore it "like an angel."97

In November 1581 Pedro de Castro read her Life. He was a canon of Ávila who had once said he had small faith in her revelations. Now he wrote to tell her what he thought of it. Before she thanked him she said a Te Deum, the Church's hymn of thanks. "My ill-doings have benefited your Reverence," she answered, "and well they may, since you see that I am not in hell as I have so often deserved to be. That is why I named the book Of the Mercies of God." She added, "I have never been so happy as I am tonight."98

That same month Saint John of the Cross came to Saint Joseph's in Ávila with mounts and all that was needed for the road. He had come to take her south to found a house in Granada. She could not go, because her next foundation at Burgos was already settled. She chose two nuns to go instead, and they set out on the eve of Saint Andrew. Saint John rode south without her.99

Saint Teresa's Last Journey to Alba

Burgos was her last foundation, and she left it in the summer of 1582. By then there were seventeen houses of her nuns. She meant to go home to Ávila, where she was prioress, and give the veil to her niece Teresita. Gracián had gone south, and she felt it. "I felt your absence at such a time so keenly that I lost all desire of writing to your Reverence," she told him on 1 September. Teresita had been bitterly disappointed that he did not come back. "In one way I am glad," Saint Teresa wrote, "as she will learn how little we can trust in any one but God—and it has done no harm even to me." In a second letter that day she sent her greetings to Saint John of the Cross.100

She said goodbye to the nuns of Valladolid on 15 September. They kept her words: "Desire great things; we gain much by this even if we cannot carry out our wishes." Blessed Ana remembered another farewell. The prioress of that house plucked Blessed Ana's sleeve and said, "Go away, both of you, and never come back." Saint Teresa wrote her last letter that day and the next, at Valladolid and then at Medina. "We are on the point of starting," she wrote, "with so much to do that I feel beside myself." She still expected to see Ávila, Salamanca, and Madrid.101

At Medina the vicar provincial was waiting for her. It was Antonio of Jesus, the friar she had once found sweeping the door at Duruelo. The Duchess of Alba had asked for her, and Antonio ordered her to Alba. Saint Teresa felt it as a great trial. She said that nothing in her whole life had been harder than being sent to Alba in the state she was in, when her road led home to Ávila. But she had to obey, as she always had. She obeyed to be like Christ, who became obedient even to death on a cross.102

She set out for Alba.

"They put her in a carriage, where she suffered and was unwell. Near Peñaranda she was in so much pain and weakness that she fainted, and everyone felt great pity to see her. In that state they had nothing to give her but some figs, and no egg could be found in the village. Sister Ana de San Bartolomé was distressed to see her in such need and to have nothing to ease it, but the Mother comforted her: 'Do not worry about me, child; these figs are very good. Many poor people will not have such a treat.'"

— Francisco de Ribera, Life of Saint Teresa of Jesus, 1590103

The next day all they could find in another village was boiled greens with onion. It was bad for her illness, but she ate it. She reached Alba de Tormes that night, on the eve of Saint Matthew. The nuns begged her to go to bed. "God help me, how tired I feel," she said. "It is more than twenty years since I went to bed early. Now I have."104

The Death of Saint Teresa at Alba

The next morning she got up and walked about looking at the house. She went to Mass and received Holy Communion. For some days she was up and down, but she received Communion every day. On 29 September, the feast of Saint Michael, she heard Mass and received Communion. Then she lay down on her bed with a flow of blood, and she did not get up again. Blessed Ana thought that what wore Saint Teresa down most was her burning love for God, and her longing to see Him and enjoy Him.105

Three days before she died she spent nearly a whole night in prayer. In the morning she asked Antonio of Jesus to hear her confession. Afterward some of the nuns heard him ask her to beg the Lord not to take her yet, not to leave them so soon. She answered that she was no longer needed in this world. Two days before the end she told Blessed Ana that she would die of this illness. She explained that she had not said so before because she did not want to give her pain.106

At five in the afternoon on 3 October she asked for the Blessed Sacrament, and she begged them not to delay it. It was the eve of the feast of Saint Francis. She was so weak that two people were needed to turn her in the bed. While they went to bring the Sacrament, she joined her hands and spoke to her nuns. "My daughters and my ladies, for the love of God I ask you to take great care to keep the Rule and the Constitutions. Do not look at the bad example this bad nun has given you, and forgive me for it."107

Then the priest came through the door of her cell with the Lord she loved so much. Saint Teresa sat up in bed lightly, with no help. It was as if she wanted to throw herself out of the bed to go to Him, and they had to hold her. Her face became very beautiful and glowing, and it looked much younger than her years. She joined her hands, full of joy, and spoke to Him.108

"Oh my Lord and my Spouse, the hour I have longed for has come. It is time now for us to be together, my God." She went on: "My Lord, it is time to set out. Let it be a happy hour, and let Your most holy will be done. Now the hour has come for me to leave this exile, and for my soul to enjoy with You what it has longed for so much."109

She thanked God many times that He had made her a daughter of the Church and that she was dying in it. Again and again she said, "In the end, Lord, I am a daughter of the Church." She asked God to forgive her sins, and she said she hoped to be saved through the merits of Jesus Christ. She asked the sisters to pray for this, and she asked their pardon. Through those hours she kept saying verses from a psalm, above all this one: "A broken and humbled heart, O God, You will not despise." At nine that evening she received the last anointing, and she helped to say the psalms.110

Then Antonio of Jesus asked her a practical question. If God took her, did she want her body carried to Ávila or buried here at Alba? The question pained her. "Am I to have anything of my own?" she said. "Will they not give me a little earth here?" Blessed Ana spoke up. "Come, Mother, let us go to Ávila. You are prioress of that house, and it is right that we go there." Saint Teresa lifted her arm with something like annoyance. "Enough of that." Then Juana del Espíritu Santo spoke. She was the prioress of Alba. "You do well, Mother. This house and all of them are yours, and Christ in this life had no house of His own." Saint Teresa answered, "Oh, how well you put it, daughter!"111

All that night she suffered much pain, and now and then she said those verses again. At seven in the morning she turned onto her side with a crucifix in her hand, the way painters show Saint Mary Magdalene. She held it until they took it from her to bury her. She stayed like that all day in prayer, very still. A nun who watched her said that her wrinkles had gone and her face glowed like a full moon. At times she moved like someone who was being spoken to, and who marvelled at what she saw.112

Blessed Ana held her in her arms. Just before the end Blessed Ana saw the Lord with the eyes of her soul. He stood at the foot of the bed in great light. Many saints and angels were with Him, waiting to take Saint Teresa's soul to glory. Blessed Ana asked Him to take Saint Teresa at once. From that hour she never again grieved over Saint Teresa's death.113

Saint Teresa breathed three soft sighs that could hardly be heard, and she gave her soul to God. It was about nine in the evening on Thursday 4 October 1582, the feast of Saint Francis. She was sixty-seven years old. Her death was so peaceful that the nuns who had often watched her pray thought she was praying still.114

Two Towns Fight Over Saint Teresa's Body

That October the calendar changed under Pope Gregory XIII. Ten days were dropped, and the day after 4 October was counted as 15 October. Saint Teresa died on the last night of the old calendar, and the Church keeps her feast on 15 October, the day that followed.115

The town council, the Franciscans, and crowds of people came to her body as to the body of a saint, and they kissed her body and her habit. She was buried between the two grilles of the lower choir. Teresa de Laíz, who had founded the convent, asked a mason and a carpenter to build a wall of stones and lime over the coffin. Now it would be hard for anyone to take Saint Teresa away. Before long the nuns noticed a sweet fragrance rising from the grave.116

Gracián came to Alba on 4 July 1583, and the nuns told him about the fragrance. He and his companion dug through the stones and lime with some of the nuns. The coffin was rotten and the clothes had gone to mould. Her body was whole and so fresh that the blood seemed still to run in it. Gracián cut off the left hand to take away as a relic. They wrapped the body in a clean sheet and buried it again.117

Ávila wanted her back. The bishop who had protected Saint Joseph's had built its chapel on one condition: his tomb and hers would lie on either side of it. In November 1585 the Provincial of the reform came to Alba with Gracián to hold an election. Afterward they went into the convent, bound the nuns to secrecy under heavy Church penalties, and took her body from the grave once more. Juana del Espíritu Santo held it in her arms for a long while. It was so soft and light that it seemed not to be flesh and bone.118

The Provincial sent the nuns to their night prayers at an unusual hour and kept only the prioress and two others with him. To them he read an order from his superiors that no one must stand in the way. The nuns in the choir above had reached the Gospel when the fragrance came to them so strongly that they knew what was happening. They could not hold themselves back. They left their prayers and hurried down, but her body was already outside the house. The friars had cut off the arm whose hand Gracián had taken, and left it at Alba. The rest of her they carried to Ávila.119

The Duke of Alba went to law and won letters from Pope Sixtus V. On 23 August 1586 her body came back to Alba, and there it has stayed.120

Among her things was her breviary, the book of daily prayers she had used until the end. She had kept a short poem of her own inside it.121

"Let nothing trouble you, let nothing frighten you. All things pass. God does not change. Patience gains everything. Whoever has God lacks nothing. God alone is enough."

— Saint Teresa of Jesus, verses kept in her breviary at Alba122

Her books went out into the world. The friar and poet Luis de León published the first edition of her works in 1588, and Ribera's Life appeared in 1590. Between 1591 and 1610 the Church took sworn statements from more than four hundred witnesses, and many of them had known her.123

Her friends carried on her work. Gracián was driven out of the Discalced in 1592 on false evidence. Pirates captured him, and he worked among Christian slaves in Tunis until he was freed. In Rome in 1597 he finished a book defending her teaching against those who wanted to call it heresy. He died at Brussels in 1614. In October 1604 six Spanish nuns of the reform reached Paris. Blessed Anne of Jesus was one of them. Blessed Ana de San Bartolomé was another, the nun in whose arms Saint Teresa had died.124

Many cures were worked through her relics, and one may stand for the rest. Isabel de Montoya was a nun at Salamanca who was blind from cataracts. On 10 February 1603 a relic of Saint Teresa's flesh was touched to her eyes. Within days she could see the Host at Communion. On 21 February she walked to Communion without a guide. Her sisters thanked God together and sang the Te Deum.125

Pope Paul V beatified her on 24 April 1614. On 12 March 1622 Pope Gregory XV canonized her in Saint Peter's with Saint Ignatius of Loyola, Saint Francis Xavier, Saint Isidore the Farmer, and Saint Philip Neri. Later popes kept returning to her books. In 1922 Pope Pius XI called her the highest teacher of contemplation. But the Church had never given the title of Doctor to a woman.126

The Church Names Saint Teresa a Doctor

On her feast day in 1967 Pope Saint Paul VI said in public that he meant to name her a Doctor of the Church. First the Church asked whether the title could go to a woman at all, and in 1968 the Pope answered that it could.127

On Sunday 27 September 1970 he said Mass in Saint Peter's before pilgrims from many nations, above all from Spain. During the Mass he declared Saint Teresa of Jesus, virgin of Ávila, a Doctor of the Universal Church. In his homily he put it more simply. "To Saint Teresa of Jesus we have conferred, or rather we have recognized, the title of Doctor of the Church." She was the first woman to receive it.128

His letter called her a woman of simple ways with no literary training. It said that Christ was the only source of her teaching and almost a living book to her. It pointed to what she taught in her Life: that prayer is a friendship made of long talks alone with the One who we know loves us. And it named what she had valued more than all her visions, revelations, and raptures: faithful and humble obedience to God's ministers.129

In the Spanish part of his homily, the Pope spoke of her last moments. He said that before her last breath she could rightly sum up her whole life in a few words, in her own language: "En fin, soy hija de la Iglesia." In the end, I am a daughter of the Church.130

A Note on the Sources

Saint Teresa's own books give few dates. She calls her brother Rodrigo "almost my age", while Ribera and Silverio say he was four years older, so this life gives no age. Ribera gives 2 November 1535 as the day she entered the Incarnation, from three documents. Older annals say 1533, and one editor says 1536. She gives no year for her coma, the wounded Christ, or the angel with the spear, so the years given here are her editors' and are marked "about." Silverio dates her father's death to 24 December 1543 from the papers of a lawsuit over his will. The editor of Ribera gives 1 February 1544. Teresa could not remember whether the first Mass at Duruelo was on the first or the second Sunday of Advent 1568. Her editor gives Sunday 28 November. This life tells the vision of hell after the angel, in the order of her own book, though her editors date it to 1558 or 1559. Her manuscript calls the angel one of the cherubim. Báñez wrote "seraph" in the margin, and later editors followed him. Báñez swore that he alone took her side before the city of Ávila. Her own account gives that part to the priest the bishop sent, and this life follows her. Ribera says she called herself Teresa of Jesus from the founding of Saint Joseph's, and a note in Lewis's edition says 1563. Her words when the Sacrament came are given in slightly different forms by two nuns and by Ribera. This life gives the first words as Mariana de Jesús swore to them and the rest as Ribera wrote them. The exchange with Blessed Ana and the prioress rests on the sworn word of one nun, Mariana de la Encarnación. The face like a full moon comes from María de San Francisco, as Ribera's editor quotes her. Ribera's editor says the Princess of Éboli herself denounced the book to the Inquisition, while Carmelite histories say her servants spread the story. Footnotes in the Stanbrook letters say that the devil threw Saint Teresa down a flight of stairs, and that she later saw Lorenzo in glory. They rest on later tradition and are left out here. The 1571 scene at the Incarnation and Saint John's escape come from her English editors, and this life tells them as they do. A letter that describes a meeting with Philip II at the Escorial has not been verified and is not used. No reply from the King to her letter of 4 December 1577 was found. Two sayings often printed under her name are not found in her writings. One is "Christ has no body now but yours." The other is "More tears are shed over answered prayers than unanswered ones." The Vatican prints the 1970 homily in Italian, and its last part also in Spanish.

How This Life Was Researched

This life draws on nineteen sources in Spanish, English, and Italian. The heart of it is Saint Teresa's own writing. Her Life, her Foundations, her rules, and her poems come from the 1915 Spanish edition of Silverio de Santa Teresa. Her letters come from the English of the Benedictines of Stanbrook. Next come the first biography, written by Francisco de Ribera in 1590, and the sworn testimony taken for her cause. Between 1591 and 1610 more than four hundred witnesses were heard. The Church documents are Saint Paul VI's letter and homily of 27 September 1970. We put her words into plain English from the Spanish where we had it. We did not reach the Spanish of her letters.
