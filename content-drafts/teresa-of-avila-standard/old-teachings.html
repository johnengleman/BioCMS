Teresa wrote for her nuns and her confessors, frankly and with humor, and she admitted that she could make herself understood only “at the cost of many words.” Her teaching is drawn from her Life (finished in a first draft in June 1562), the Constitutions she wrote for St. Joseph’s in Ávila, The Book of the Foundations (begun in 1573), The Interior Castle (1577), her poems, and her letters. The themes below are hers, with her own words; the quotations are from public-domain translations or are our own translations of her Spanish.

Prayer is friendship with God

For Teresa, mental prayer was no technique reserved for experts. It was time spent alone with someone who loves us, in the way friends spend time together.

“Mental prayer, to my mind, is nothing else than dealing as a friend, often alone, with the one who we know loves us.”

— Teresa, Libro de la Vida, ch. 8 (our translation from her Spanish)

She wrote this in the eighth chapter of her Life, soon after telling how she had given up mental prayer, for more than a year, as a young nun at the Incarnation. The heart of it is the phrase tratar de amistad, to deal as friends, and in the same sentence she adds that no one ever took God for a friend without being repaid. Her struggle to pray lasted nearly twenty years, until she threw herself down before an image of the wounded Christ. Paul VI cited this passage (Life 8:5) when he made her a Doctor of the Church in 1970.

Love counts more than thought

Teresa did not measure prayer by clever ideas or sweet feelings. What matters is love, and love is a settled will to please God.

“To make rapid progress and to reach the mansions we wish to enter, it is not so essential to think much as to love much.”

— Teresa, The Interior Castle, Fourth Mansions (tr. Benedictines of Stanbrook, 1921)

In the same part of The Interior Castle, written in 1577, she explained what she meant: “Love does not consist in great sweetness of devotion, but in a fervent determination to strive to please God in all things.” She wrote the book in a year when the nuns who had voted for her as prioress were declared excommunicated and her friars were in prison or in exile, and there is no trace of those troubles in it.

Perfection is in virtue, not in feelings

Dryness in prayer is no sign of failure. Teresa taught that what counts is the virtue a person practises, not the devotion they feel, and that dryness may even be a mark of God’s confidence.

“Do not be unhappy about it: perfection does not consist in feelings but in virtue. Your devotion will return when you least expect it.”

— Teresa to her niece Teresita, Medina del Campo, 7 August 1580 (tr. Benedictines of Stanbrook)

She wrote this to her niece, a young nun troubled by dryness in prayer, a few weeks after the girl’s father, Teresa’s brother Lorenzo, had died. In the same letter she told her that such dryness seemed to show “that our Lord considers you a strong soul.” Teresa had lived it: for years she found prayer hard, and for more than a year she gave it up. When she left Valladolid for the last time in September 1582, she told the nuns: “Desire great things; we gain much by this even if we cannot carry out our wishes.”

Seek God within

Teresa pictured the soul as a castle with many dwelling places, and the way to God as a way inward. God is not far away; the soul is his house.

“If you want to find me, you will look for me in yourself, for you are my room, my house and my dwelling.”

— Teresa, poem “Buscando a Dios” (Poesías VIII; our translation from her Spanish)

The Spanish reads si hallarme quisieres, a Mí buscarme has en ti. Porque tú eres mi aposento, eres mi casa y morada. According to Diego de Yepes, the image of the castle came to her in a vision on the eve of Trinity Sunday 1577: a castle of crystal with seven rooms. She began The Interior Castle the next day, Trinity Sunday, 2 June 1577.

God among the pots and pans

Prayer and work are not rivals. Martha, who serves, and Mary, who listens, both belong in every life, and God is present in ordinary chores.

“Even if you are in the kitchen our Lord moves amidst the pots and pans, helping us both within and without.”

— Teresa, The Book of the Foundations, ch. 5 (tr. David Lewis)

She wrote this in The Book of the Foundations, which she began at Salamanca on 24 August 1573 on the orders of the Jesuit Father Ripalda. In the last part of The Interior Castle she said the same: “Both Martha and Mary must entertain our Lord and keep Him as their Guest, nor must they be so inhospitable as to offer Him no food.” In her Constitutions no sister was ever to be given a set task; each was to work so that the others could eat. One nun testified at second hand that another had seen her rapt in the kitchen with a pan in her hand, and her letters mention quinces, dogfish and tunny.

Poverty that sets free

Teresa founded her houses in poverty, living by alms and work, and she believed that poverty gave her nuns their freedom. She cared more for a candidate’s love of God than for her dowry.

“Houses founded in poverty should not have an income, for I know, and have learnt by experience, that in all such cases, if the nuns are faithful to God, they will always possess the greatest liberty of spirit.”

— Teresa to Jerome Gracián, late 1579 (tr. Benedictines of Stanbrook)

Her Constitutions allowed the sisters no chest, box or drawer of their own, because, she wrote, the devil can loosen poverty in small things. When she founded Toledo in 1569 with three or four ducats, there was not even firewood to fry a sardine, and when gifts began to arrive she wrote, “I was in pain when my poverty was ended.” In the Foundations she told her prioresses never to turn away a good candidate for lack of money: those who came with nothing, she said, “cheered my spirit, and gave me so much pleasure as to make me weep for joy.”

Test every grace

Teresa received visions and raptures, and she feared being deceived by them. She preferred to be guided by learned confessors who were not great mystics, because spiritual men, she thought, are easier to deceive. She held that she might be mistaken about a vision, and never in obeying.

“For the love of God, Father, do not be so without fear, for you make me afraid; look again, I would not want to deceive you.”

— Teresa to her confessor Báñez, as he testified under oath in 1591 (our translation)

When confessors ordered her to make signs of scorn at a vision, she obeyed. In 1575 Gracián, her superior, told her to ask God whether to found next in Madrid or Seville; when the answer was Madrid and he ordered Seville, she went, and told him she could not be as sure of any revelation as of his order. When her Life was denounced to the Inquisition, Báñez himself took the manuscript to the Holy Office. On her deathbed at Alba in 1582 she said again and again, “En fin, Señor, soy hija de la Iglesia”: in the end, Lord, I am a daughter of the Church.

Looking at the wounded Christ

Teresa found the strength to accept suffering by looking at Christ’s own sufferings. Her conversion began before an image of Christ “most grievously wounded.”

“Who can look upon our Lord, covered with wounds, and bowed down under persecutions, without accepting, loving, and longing for them?”

— Teresa, The Life of St. Teresa of Jesus (tr. David Lewis)

She wrote this in her Life. She had lived it already: after a coma and eight months crippled, she wrote that when she began to crawl on all fours, she praised God. On her last journey, when there was nothing to eat but figs, she told a worried nun that many poor people would not have such a treat.

The sick come first

The Constitutions Teresa wrote for St. Joseph’s were strict: straw sacks instead of mattresses, no meat, long fasts. But she made one exception without hesitation.

“Let the healthy go without what they need, rather than the sick go without some kindnesses.”

— Teresa, Constitutions for St. Joseph’s, Ávila (our translation from her Spanish)

The Spanish reads antes falte lo necesario a las sanas, que algunas piedades a las enfermas. The sick, she wrote, were to be cared for with all love, comfort and kindness, as far as their poverty allowed, and to have linen and good beds, “digo colchones,” I mean mattresses. When the friars at Duruelo were too severe with themselves she asked them to be less so, and she later forbade a prioress to try her nuns with pinches and sudden blows.

No sullen saints

Teresa had no patience for gloomy piety or showy devotions. She wanted joy and plain good sense in her houses.

“From silly devotions, God deliver us!”

— Teresa, The Life of St. Teresa of Jesus (tr. David Lewis)

The Spanish is de devociones a bobas nos libre Dios. Her Constitutions banned games at recreation for a reason of her own: the Lord would give some sisters the grace to entertain the others, fundadas en esto, todo es tiempo bien gastado, and on that ground all the time is well spent. The nuns who travelled with her remembered the same spirit. Ana de San Bartolomé wrote that she was not fond of sad people and did not want her companions to be sad, and that she used to say, “Dios me libre de santos encapotados”: God deliver me from sullen saints. That saying comes from Ana’s own account; the English “sour-faced saints” is a translator’s footnote, and neither is in Teresa’s own writings.

God alone is enough

Everything passes, and God does not change. Whoever has God lacks nothing, and patience reaches everything in the end.

“Let nothing trouble you, let nothing frighten you. All things pass. God does not change. Patience gains everything. Whoever has God lacks nothing. God alone is enough.”

— Teresa, Nada te turbe (our translation from her Spanish)

These nine lines, beginning Nada te turbe, were in her breviary when she died at Alba in 1582, and Father Gracián wrote in the book that it had been hers. A longer poem beginning “Eleva el pensamiento” is a later gloss by another hand. She died far from home, after obeying an order to go to Alba instead of Ávila that, a companion testified, weighed on her more than any order of her life. When she was asked whether her body should be taken home, she answered, “Will they not give me a little earth here?”
