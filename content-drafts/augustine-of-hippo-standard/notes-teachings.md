## 1. What survives

Totals
- Possidius's list (the "Indiculus") counts "at least 1,030 writings numbered by their Author", with more "that cannot be numbered because he did not give them any number". [T-S10, 20 Feb 2008] Benedict XVI is describing it; the Indiculus itself I did not read.
- Today "more than 300 letters" and "almost 600 homilies" survive; there were once "perhaps even as many as between 3,000 and 4,000" homilies. [T-S10, 20 Feb 2008] (paraphrase of his numbers, with his wording kept in quotation marks)
- Letters: 270 in the Benedictine collection, 53 of them from other writers [T-S2]. Sermons: 363 "certainly authentic" in the Benedictine edition; shorthand writers took them down, and Augustine often revised them [T-S2]. The numbers differ from Benedict XVI's; the sources count different things and different editions.
- Best old edition: the Benedictine edition, 11 tomes, Paris 1679-1700; reprinted in Migne, Patrologia Latina 32-47 [T-S2].
- Authenticity: the main works are sure. See Q8 for what is doubtful.

Main works (dates as given by the source; "to check" where sources differ)
- Confessions, 13 books, Latin. 397-400 [T-S10]; "towards A.D. 400" [T-S2]. Free to read: Latin, T-S17 (thelatinlibrary.com/augustine/conf1.shtml to conf13); English: Pusey 1838 (Gutenberg 3296) and Dods/Pilkington on New Advent (newadvent.org/fathers/1101.htm, see OW-S2, OW-S3). Public-domain.
- Retractations, 426-428 [T-S2]. Augustine's own review of his works in order of writing; "a guide of inestimable price" to how his thought grew [T-S2].
- City of God, 22 books, begun 413; books 20-22 written 426 [T-S2]; written between 413 and 426 after the sack of Rome in 410 [T-S10]. English: Dods, newadvent.org/fathers/1201.htm.
- On the Trinity, 15 books, 400-416 per T-S2; T-S10 says the first 12 books were written 399-412, published without his knowledge, and the whole was finished and revised about 420. Sources differ: the writer should say "about twenty years of work" only if a source is added.
- Enchiridion (Handbook on Faith, Hope, and Love), 421, for a Roman named Laurentius [T-S2].
- On Christian Doctrine, begun 397, finished 426; "historically the first" treatise on how to read Scripture [T-S2].
- Sermons and Enarrations on the Psalms; Homilies on John (416, per T-S2); On the Sermon on the Mount [T-S2].
- Against Manichaeans, Donatists, Pelagians, Semipelagians, Arians: the lists with dates are in T-S2 (for example On the Spirit and the Letter 412; On Nature and Grace 415; On Rebuke and Grace 427; On the Predestination of the Saints 428; On the Gift of Perseverance 429; Conference with Maximinus 428). The last work against Julian of Eclanum was left unfinished at his death [T-S2].
- Early philosophical dialogues from Cassiciacum, 386-387: Against the Academics, On the Happy Life, On Order, Soliloquies, and later On the Teacher with his son Adeodatus [T-S2].
- Monastic texts: see Q6 (communities) and Q8 (dispute).
- Best free translations: Schaff's Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers series, series 1, volumes 1-8, on New Advent (public-domain). Modern translations (Hill, New City Press; Boulding) are facts-only: never copy.

## 2. The big ideas

Each idea: the words, the place, the occasion, and "new?" with who says so.

### 2.1 Grace comes first, and it heals a will that cannot heal itself
- Words (Latin, T-S17, Conf. X.29.40): "da quod iubes et iube quod vis". Our English: Give what you command, and command what you will.
- Words (T-S18, On the Gift of Perseverance, English): "But since even the will itself is prepared Proverbs 16:1 by the Lord, why do you rebuke me because you see me unwilling to do His precepts, and do not rather ask Him Himself to work in me the will also?" Locator: the chapter before the heading "Chapter 7"; chapter number to check. (The scraps "Proverbs 16:1" are link text in the web copy.)
- Occasion: the Pelagian controversy. Pelagius held that man can lead a good life "by the natural power of free will and without the necessary help of God's grace" [T-S8 CCC 406, paraphrase]. African councils condemned Pelagians at Carthage in 412 [T-S1]; Pope Innocent I confirmed in 417; Pope Zosimus condemned them in 418 [T-S1].
- Later turn: monks of Hadrumetum in Africa (about 426) and Cassian's circle at Marseilles denied that the first desire for faith is God's gift. Prosper of Aquitaine told Augustine. He answered in On the Predestination of the Saints, saying even the first wish for salvation is grace [T-S1].
- New? T-S3: "he was the first of all to synthesize the great theories of the Fall, grace, and free will" and the explanation that reconciles them "of which we can find no trace in his predecessors." Also T-S3: "Augustine was not their inventor, he was only the first to put them in a strong light." The same author says Augustine's system "was complete in his mind from the year 397", the answers to Simplician. Orthodox critics disagree (Q8).
- Augustine's own change: he "acknowledges that he had not yet understood how the first good inclination of the will is a gift of God (Retractions, I, xxiii, n. 3, as the CE text gives it)" [T-S1, paraphrase]. This is a good story for the writer: he changed his mind about his own early books.

### 2.2 Original sin
- Occasion: same controversy.
- Catechism: the teaching was "articulated more precisely in the fifth century, especially under the impulse of St. Augustine's reflections against Pelagianism" [T-S8 CCC 406].
- New? Florovsky, quoted by Sanidopoulos: "it was Saint Augustine who formulated the doctrine of Original Sin" [T-S23, quote of the blog text, not of Florovsky's own words]. T-S3 says he was "not the inventor". The two sources disagree on how new it was.
- Council use: Trent session V, canon 2 anathematizes anyone who says Adam's sin harmed only himself, and quotes Romans 5:12 as "in whom all have sinned" [T-S7]. Trent does not name Augustine (grep of V, VI, XXV found no "Augustin").

### 2.3 The Church holds sinners, and the sacraments do not depend on the minister
- Occasion: the Donatist schism in Africa. The question was whether a priest's sin spoils his sacraments, and whether a church with sinners can be holy [T-S1].
- At the Conference of Carthage, June 411, 286 Catholic and 279 Donatist bishops met; Augustine and Aurelius spoke for the Catholics; the proconsul Marcellinus ruled for the Catholics [T-S1].
- Words (CCC 1584, from In Jo. ev. 5,15, English): "As for the proud minister, he is to be ranked with the devil. Christ's gift is not thereby profaned: what flows through him keeps its purity". [T-S8] Facts-only; paraphrase in our own text is safer.
- New? T-S1: "He has corrected, perfected, and even excelled" Cyprian. Optatus of Mileve "had expressed the basis of the same doctrines"; Augustine "deepened, systematized, and completed" them. Specht, cited there: he "deserves to be named the Doctor of the Church as well as the Doctor of Grace" [T-S1].

### 2.4 Faith and reason
- Words (CCC 158, English): "I believe, in order to understand; and I understand, the better to believe." Source given: Sermon 43, 7, 9 [T-S8, facts-only]. Latin not fetched.
- T-S2: "He is the first of the Fathers", says Harnack, "who felt the need of forcing his faith to reason." Letter 120 to Consentius is the key text. (Harnack's claim; CE repeats it.)
- Words (CCC 230, English): "If you understood him, it would not be God" (Sermons 52, 6, 16 and 117, 3, 5) [T-S8].

### 2.5 The Trinity
- Words (CCC 264, English): "The Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father as the first principle and, by the eternal gift of this to the Son, from the communion of both the Father and the Son" (De Trin. 15, 26, 47) [T-S8].
- Pius XI quotes Augustine's rule: what the Persons are "to each other" (Father, Son, Gift) is plural, but what each is "to himself" (God, good, almighty) is one, "nec tamen tres dii" [T-S4, Latin, from De Trinitate; the full Latin is in paragraph 23].
- Method: the last books use analogies from the soul; he himself says they are "far-fetched and very obscure" [T-S2, paraphrase of CE].
- Disputed in the East (Q8).

### 2.6 Two cities and two loves
- Latin (OW-S4b XIV.28, already checked): "Fecerunt itaque civitates duas amores duo, terrenam scilicet amor sui usque ad contemptum Dei, caelestem vero amor Dei usque ad contemptum sui."
- Occasion: the sack of Rome in 410; pagans blamed the Christian God. Benedict XVI: the book answers "what we should and should not expect of God, and what the relationship is between the political sphere and the sphere of faith, of the Church" [T-S10, 20 Feb 2008].
- New? T-S2: "in a burst of genius he creates the philosophy of history". Claim by the CE author.

### 2.7 Peace as order
- English (OW-S4, Dods XIX.13, already checked): "The peace of all things is the tranquillity of order."
- Pius XI uses the book to describe a Christian ruler and gives the passage "We do not style certain Christian emperors happy [writes the Bishop of Hippo], because their reign was a long one" [T-S4, English, from City of God V; citation number in the notes of T-S4, not checked].

### 2.8 Evil is a lack, and freedom is the source of moral evil
- T-S1: against the Manichaeans he teaches "every work of God is good and that the only source of moral evil is the liberty of creatures" (City of God XIX.13.2, as CE cites it).
- Catechism 385: "I sought whence evil comes and there was no solution", said Augustine [T-S8, citing Conf. 7,7,11].

### 2.9 Two weights on his reading of Scripture
- English (OW-Q15, On Christian Doctrine I.36.40): a reading that does not build up love of God and neighbor "does not yet understand them as he ought."
- T-S2: he warns not to give interpretations "which are hazardous or opposed to science" so that Scripture is not mocked (De Genesi ad litteram I.19.39 as CE cites). Also: all the elements of the world were created "in a confused and nebulous mass" with hidden "rationes seminales" (CE's reading; CE says he held the fixity of species).

## 3. The everyday teaching

- Prayer: "learned ignorance." English (T-S19, Letter 130.15.28): "There is therefore in us a certain learned ignorance , so to speak — an ignorance which we learn from that Spirit of God who helps our infirmities." Occasion: advice to the widow Proba on how to pray. (Spaces before commas are web-copy artifacts.)
- Prayer in the Lord's Prayer. CCC 2762 (English): "Run through all the words of the holy prayers [in Scripture], and I do not think that you will find anything in them that is not contained and included in the Lord's Prayer." (Letter 130, 12, 22, per the CCC note) [T-S8].
- Prayer of Christ. CCC 2616 (English; En. in Ps. 85,1): "He prays for us as our priest, prays in us as our Head, and is prayed to by us as our God. Therefore let us acknowledge our voice in him and his in us." [T-S8]
- Singing. Latin (OW-Q12, Sermo 256.3): "canta et ambula." See OW for translation and the "sing and keep marching" correction.
- Love of neighbor as a rule of action. English (OW-Q13, Tractate 7 on 1 John, section 8): "Love, and do what thou wilt". Keep the context (OW section 8).
- Sin and mercy. CCC 1994 (English): "the justification of the wicked is a greater work than the creation of heaven and earth", because "heaven and earth will pass away but the salvation and justification of the elect . . . will not pass away." (In Jo. ev. 72,3) [T-S8].
- Envy. CCC 2539 area: Augustine saw envy as "the diabolical sin" (De catechizandis rudibus 4,8) [T-S8; paragraph number not read, the passage is in the section on the tenth commandment].
- Sin defined. CCC 1871 (English): "Sin is an utterance, a deed, or a desire contrary to the eternal law" (Contra Faustum 22) [T-S8].
- The Eucharist and unity. CCC 1398 (English): "O sacrament of devotion! O sign of unity! O bond of charity!" (In Jo. ev. 26,13). CCC 1372 quotes City of God X.6: "This wholly redeemed city, the assembly and society of the saints, is offered to God as a universal sacrifice" [T-S8]. Facts-only; do not copy long.
- Baptism. CCC 1228 (English): "The word is brought to the material element, and it becomes a sacrament." (In Jo. ev. 80,3) [T-S8].
- Work, money, family, death: money and poverty are in W (Possidius ch. 22-25). Death and grief: Conf. IX (OW-Q7). Everyday teaching on marriage is in T-S2's list of titles (five works on continence, marriage, widowhood; on lying 374 and 420; on patience; on prayer for the dead 421), but I read no text of them. "Not found" for exact words on work and family.
- Humility of office. LG 32, quoting Augustine (English): "What I am for you terrifies me; what I am with you consoles me. For you I am a bishop; but with you I am a Christian. The former is a duty; the latter a grace. The former is a danger; the latter, salvation." Source for the Council's quote: Sermon 340, 1 (the Council's note gives Sermon 340; I did not see the note text for this line). [T-S9] Latin not fetched.

## 4. A special perspective

- The inner self and God. Benedict XVI: the Confessions, "with their focus on interiority and psychology, ... constitute a unique model in Western (and not only Western) literature ... This attention to the spiritual life, to the mystery of the 'I', to the mystery of God who is concealed in the 'I', is something quite extraordinary, without precedent, and remains for ever, as it were, a spiritual 'peak'." [T-S10, 9 Jan 2008] He also says "confessiones" means both confession of faults and praise of God [T-S10, 20 Feb 2008]. T-S2: of all his works "none has been more universally read and admired".
- Words (OW-Q22, Latin): "intravi in intima mea duce te". Words (OW-Q3): "et ecce intus eras et ego foris".
- Total Christ and the Church as one body: CCC 2616 and 1372 above [T-S8]. LG 32 [T-S9].
- Ten Commandments as the frame of teaching. CCC 2065 (English): "Ever since St. Augustine, the Ten Commandments have occupied a predominant place in the catechesis of baptismal candidates and the faithful." CCC 2066: "The present catechism follows the division of the Commandments established by St. Augustine, which has become traditional in the Catholic Church. It is also that of the Lutheran confessions. The Greek Fathers worked out a slightly different division, which is found in the Orthodox Churches and Reformed communities." [T-S8] A clear case of a lasting practice that came from him: DOCUMENTED.
- Shared life with property in common. Pius XI quotes Possidius on the monastery at Hippo, "no one in that society kept anything of his own, but they held all things in common" and Augustine's own words on perfection [T-S4 paragraphs 46-48, English]. He says Augustine "established a community of clerics in the episcopal palace" [T-S4].
- Teaching to simple people. Benedict XVI: in a letter to Evodius he stopped dictating On the Trinity "because they are too demanding and I think that few can understand them; it is therefore urgent to have more texts which we hope will be useful to many" (Letter 169, 1, 1). [T-S10, 20 Feb 2008]. Also De catechizandis rudibus is "a theory and also a method of catechesis" [T-S10].

## 5. Influence during life

- Africa's councils: Augustine "impressed his spirit upon" the councils of Carthage in 398, 401, 407, and 419, and of Mileve in 416 and 418 [T-S1]. He was at the plenary council of Africa on 8 October 393, where he gave a discourse that became On Faith and the Creed [T-S1].
- Hippo as a school of bishops. His house was "a veritable nursery which supplied the founders of the monasteries that were soon spread all over Africa and the bishops who occupied the neighbouring sees". "Possidius enumerates ten of the saint's friends and disciples who were promoted to the episcopacy" [T-S1; the Possidius chapter number is given there as xxii; not re-checked]. Pius XI, from Possidius: Augustine "permitted many Religious men to sally forth in all directions, in order that they might found new monasteries—as one fire kindles another" [T-S4 para 47, English]. Names from W notes: Alypius (Tagaste, 394), Evodius (Uzalis), Possidius (Calama), Aurelius (Carthage).
- Manichaeans: 392 debate with Fortunatus (W); 404 conference with Felix, who "declared himself vanquished, embraced the Faith, and, together with Augustine, subscribed the acts" [T-S1].
- Donatists: 411 conference above; "Little by little Donatism died out, to disappear with the coming of the Vandals" [T-S1].
- Change of mind on force: T-S1 says he first sought "conferences and a friendly controversy", then after the Circumcellion violence "approved rigid laws" (Letter 93, 408), but "never wished heresy to be punishable by death" (Letter 100: "Vos rogamus ne occidatis"). Disputed; see Q8.
- Popes in his lifetime. Innocent I called a letter of Augustine and four bishops "plenas fidei, totoque religionis catholicae vigore firmatas" (a letter full of faith and strengthened with all the vigor of the Catholic religion) [T-S4 Latin, para 2; English: "A letter instinct with faith and staunch with all the vigor of the Catholic religion"]. He wrote to Augustine as "amicum sibi carissimum" (a friend very dear to him) [T-S4].
- Pelagian controversy: Innocent I confirmed the African condemnation in 417, Zosimus condemned in 418 "being enlightened by Augustine" [T-S1; the phrase is CE's]. A synod at Diospolis in Palestine had been deceived by the Pelagians [T-S1].
- Pastoral outreach: Enchiridion for Laurentius (421) [T-S2]; Letter 130 to Proba; Letter 120 to Consentius [T-S2, T-S19].
- Successor: in 426 he had the people and clergy accept the deacon Heraclius as helper and successor, "to spare his episcopal city the turmoil of an election" [T-S1].
- Debate with the Arian Maximinus, 428, "at a public conference" [T-S1].
- Honor from the Emperor: Theodosius II ordered him to be summoned to the Council of Ephesus (431) "with every mark of respect"; he died before it [T-S4 para 26; W / CM notes have the same fact from Butler].
- Siege length: CE says "eighteen months' siege"; Possidius says "almost fourteen months" (W). Do not use CE's number.

## 6. Influence after death (time order)

### 6.1 The fifth and sixth centuries: popes and councils defend his teaching
- 431, Pope Celestine I, letter to the bishops of Gaul (Venerius, Marinus, Leontius, Auxonius, Arcadius, Filtanius and the rest; epistle 21). Latin [T-S4, para 2]: "Augustinum sanctae recordationis virum pro vita sua atque meritis in nostra communione semper habuimus, nec umquam hunc sinistrae suspicionis saltem rumor aspersit; quem tantae scientiae olim fuisse meminimus, ut inter magistros optimos etiam ante a meis semper decessoribus haberetur." (The web copy prints "aspersiti quem": an OCR slip.) Our English: We have always counted Augustine, a man of holy memory, in our communion for his life and merits, and no rumor of bad suspicion ever touched him. We remember that his knowledge was so great that even my predecessors always ranked him among the best teachers. DOCUMENTED (Latin in an official modern text; I did not read the original letter). Date 431: T-S3. Occasion: Semipelagians in Gaul attacked his memory [T-S3].
- The Capitula that go with Celestine's letter: ten dogmatic points, of uncertain origin, "regarded, at least since Pope Hormisdas, as expressing the faith of the Church". They end: "As to the questions which are more profound and difficult, and which have given rise to these controversies, we do not think it necessary to impose the solution of them." [T-S3, English quote of the Capitula].
- 5th century, Gelasius I (1 Nov 493 per T-S3): called Jerome and Augustine "ecclesiasticorum lumina magistrorum" (lights of the teachers of the Church) [T-S4 para 3 Latin]. DOCUMENTED.
- 13 Aug 520, Hormisdas to Bishop Possessor. Latin [T-S4]: "De arbitrio tamen libero et gratia Dei, quod Romana, hoc est catholica, sequatur et asseveret Ecclesia, licet in variis libris beati Augustini, et maxime ad Hilarium et Prosperum, possit cognosci, tamen in scriniis ecclesiasticis expressa capitula continentur." Our English: What the Roman, that is, the Catholic Church follows and holds about free will and the grace of God can be learned from the books of blessed Augustine, most of all those to Hilary and Prosper, though the exact chapters are kept in the Church's archives. DOCUMENTED (date from T-S3).
- 529, Second Council of Orange (Caesarius of Arles presiding; 25 canons and a conclusion). T-S20: Caesarius told Pope Felix IV (III) of the Semipelagians; the pope sent "Capitula... drawn almost in their entirety from the works of St. Augustine and the 'Sententiae' of St. Prosper of Aquitaine"; these became the basis of the 25. T-S3: "the canons of the Council of Orange are borrowed from his works". Pius XI: the Fathers "made use of his very words" at Orange [T-S4 para 5]. Conclusion (T-S5, English): "The sin of the first man has so impaired and weakened free will that no one thereafter can either love God as he ought or believe in God or do good for God's sake, unless the grace of divine mercy has preceded him." Also: "We not only do not believe that any are foreordained to evil by the power of God, but even state with utter abhorrence that if there are those who want to believe so evil a thing, they are anathema." Note: the canons do not name Augustine; they say "the interpretations of the ancient Fathers" [T-S5]. They stop short of his teaching on predestination: this is the "moderate Augustinism" of T-S3. DOCUMENTED.
- 25 Jan 531, Boniface II confirms Orange in his letter to Caesarius. English (T-S6, Denzinger 399): "since many Fathers, and above all Bishop Augustine of blessed memory, but also our former high priests of the Apostolic See are proved to have discussed this with such detailed reasoning that there should be no further doubt in anyone that faith itself also comes to us from grace". DOCUMENTED. (T-S3 calls the process: "a whole century of fierce contests (430-529) ended in the triumph of moderate Augustinism".)
- 534, John II to the senators of Constantinople: "cuius doctrinam — aiebat — secundum praedecessorum meorum statuta, Romana sequitur et servat Ecclesia" (whose teaching, he said, according to my predecessors' rules, the Roman Church follows and keeps) [T-S4 Latin; date 534 from T-S3 "John II (534)"]. DOCUMENTED.
- Other defenders. T-S3: Prosper of Aquitaine "by his moderation" and the unknown author of "On the Calling of All Nations" opened the way to agreement; Fulgentius's strong formulas were used by opponents; Lucidus's predestinationism was condemned at the Council of Arles in 475. CLAIMED (CE only; I read none of them).
- Early cult. Feast on 28 August in the Martyrology of Jerome and in the Carthage martyrology, "as old as the sixth century"; kept with solemnity in the life of Caesarius [CM-S3 p.505].

### 6.2 The early Middle Ages: the "Great Master of the West"
- Leo the Great and Gregory the Great knew his teaching well [T-S4 para 4]. Gregory I, Registrum X.37, to Innocentius, prefect of Africa. Latin [T-S4]: "Si cupitis delicioso pabulo saginari, beati Augustini patriotae vestri opuscula legite et ad comparationem siliginis illius nostrum furfurem ne quaeratis." Our English: If you want rich food, read the little works of blessed Augustine, your countryman, and do not look for our bran next to his fine wheat. DOCUMENTED. T-S3 adds that Gregory "was formed in his school" and "popularized his theories" (CLAIMED, CE judgment).
- Hadrian I used to quote Augustine and called him "egregium doctorem" [T-S4 para 4, Latin]. DOCUMENTED (by Pius XI).
- Isidore, Bede, Alcuin "drew abundantly from the works of Augustine"; so did the sixth-century preachers, "notably St. Caesarius" [T-S3]. CLAIMED by CE; I read none of the works.
- Charlemagne. English (T-S16, Einhard): at table "he listened to reading or music. The subjects of the readings were the stories and deeds of olden time: he was fond, too, of St. Augustine's books, and especially of the one entitled 'The City of God.'" DOCUMENTED (Einhard knew the court). Latin not fetched. Link to the claim that City of God shaped his idea of rule: not found.
- Ninth century. T-S3: the predestinarian Gottschalk was met by the assemblies of Savonnieres and Toucy (859-860); "The doctrine of the Divine will to save all men and the universality of redemption was thus consecrated". CLAIMED (CE only).
- 9th-12th century. T-S3: in the quarrels on ordinations by simoniacal priests, "the text of Augustine plays the principal part". CLAIMED (CE; cites Mirbt 1888).

### 6.3 Scholasticism and mysticism
- T-S3: "Augustine's theological authority, indisputably the highest, dominates all thinkers" from Gregory to Trent; "appealed to alike by the Scholastics Anselm, Peter Lombard, and Thomas Aquinas, and by Bernard, Hugh of St. Victor, and Tauler". Hugh of St Victor was called "another Augustine". Peter Lombard's Sentences "are little else than an effort to synthesize the Augustinian theories." CLAIMED (CE). I did not read these works. Anselm's Proslogion (T-S: thelatinlibrary) has no "Augustin" in the text: grep gave no match, so a link through the Proslogion is not shown. The Monologion's preface was not reached.
- Peter the Venerable ranked him "immediately after the Apostles" [T-S3]. CLAIMED.
- 1277 (7 and 18 March): Paris and Canterbury condemned theses of Thomas Aquinas. T-S3 reads this as the "last victory of a too rigid Augustinism". The Franciscan school followed Bonaventure's Augustinian ways. T-S3: Albert and Thomas "far from being adversaries of St. Augustine... placed themselves in his school... Hence, there was no longer any school strictly Augustinian, because every school was such." Condemnations withdrawn 14 Feb 1325 after Thomas's canonization [T-S3]. CLAIMED (CE judgment), dates DOCUMENTED by CE only.
- 1298: Boniface VIII decree names Augustine one of the four Latin Doctors (with Gregory, Ambrose, Jerome); feasts kept as doubles [CM-S4, from the church-miracles notes]. DOCUMENTED.
- 14th century. Thomas Bradwardin (died 1349) and John Wyclif (1324-87) used Augustine for strict predestination; Wyclif's followers called him "John of Augustine" [T-S3, quoting Netter]. CE says this misreads Augustine. CLAIMED / DISPUTED.
- 15th century. Bessarion (1472) and Marsilio Ficino (1499) used his name for Plato against Aristotle [T-S3]. CLAIMED.

### 6.4 Reformation and the Counter-Reformation
- Luther entered the Augustinian monastery at Erfurt on 17 July 1505 [T-S13]. Schaff: the Augustinian hermits were the order to which "Staupitz and Luther belonged" [T-S15]. DOCUMENTED (order membership). Whether Augustine shaped Luther's teaching is a different claim: T-S3 says "Luther and Calvin were content to treat Augustine with a little less irreverence" than other Fathers, and that the Reformers took predestination "in the name of Augustinism". The CE author (and Cunningham, cited there) say Calvin's total depravity "is not the doctrine of Saint Austin" [T-S3]. DISPUTED; CE is a Catholic source.
- 1546, Council of Trent: the Catechism says the Church spoke on original sin "especially at the second Council of Orange (529) and at the Council of Trent (1546)" [T-S8 CCC 406]. Pius XI: Orange and Trent "made use of his very words" [T-S4 para 5]. T-S20: the Orange canons "were freely used by the Council of Trent in its condemnation of Luther". Trent's own text (T-S7) does not name him. DOCUMENTED for the line Orange to Trent; the "very words" claim rests on Pius XI and CE.
- 1588 onward: Molina and the quarrel De auxiliis (before Clement VIII and Paul V). T-S3: the three schools (Thomist, Augustinian (Noris), Molinist) may all teach; Benedict XIV, brief of 13 July 1748: "This Apostolic See favours the liberty of the schools". Clement VIII also used his authority "to throw light on the obscure features of abstruse debates" [T-S4 para 4]. DOCUMENTED (by CE and Pius XI).
- 1640: Cornelius Jansenius, bishop of Ypres, "Augustinus, seu doctrina S. Augustini de humanae naturae sanitate, aegritudine, medicina, adversus Pelagianos et Massilienses." He said he read Augustine "ten times" and the anti-Pelagian works "thirty times" [T-S12]. The Holy Office condemned it on 1 Aug 1641; Urban VIII renewed it in the bull "In eminenti" (1642); Innocent X condemned five propositions in "Cum occasione" on 31 May 1653 [T-S12]. On 7 Dec 1690 Alexander VIII condemned the claim that one may hold a teaching "clearly based on Augustine" "without referring to any pontifical Bull" [T-S3]. DOCUMENTED. Jansenists claimed Augustine; the Church held that they misread him.
- 1794: Pius VI, "Auctorem fidei" (28 Aug) against the synod of Pistoia; Augustine cited, and the Pope permitted a departure from Augustine's severity on unbaptized children [T-S3; T-S4 para 4].

### 6.6 Communities
- Pius XI on the Rule (English, T-S4 para 47): the rule Augustine gave to the women's community under his sister "is followed today by a goodly number of religious congregations of both sexes, not only those who are called 'Augustinians,' but others whose founders have added their individual constitutions to the original rule." DOCUMENTED (official statement).
- What "the Rule" is. T-S14: his writings, "especially in 'De opere monachorum'" (P.L. 40, 527), were "mentioned in the ancient codices regularum of the eighth or ninth century as 'The Rule of St. Augustine'"; Letter 211 "contains the early 'Augustinian Rule for Nuns'". T-S15: "so far as is known Augustine prescribed no definite code such as Benedict afterwards drew up". See Q8 for the dispute.
- Canons regular. Roman synods of 1059 and 1063 recommended priests to share their revenues; in the 12th century many groups of clerics "adopted what began to be known as the rule of St. Augustine" [T-S15]. T-S14: the instructions "formed the basis of the rule which, in accordance with the decree of the Lateran Synod, in 1059, was adopted by canons". Schaff's footnote: "tradition runs" that Innocent II prescribed it in 1139 for all canons regular (CLAIMED); a bull of 16 Dec 1243 speaks of "regula S. Augustini et ordo" (Innocent not numbered in the passage). In England: Austin canons, "Black Canons"; 170 houses at the suppression [T-S15].
- Premonstratensians "adopted the Augustinian rule" [T-S15]. The Gilbertines of England too [T-S15]. DOCUMENTED. Dominicans and Servites: CLAIMED by a search summary only (T-L3).
- Order of Hermits of St Augustine (the Augustinians): union of hermit groups called by Alexander IV; first meeting 1 March 1256 under Cardinal Richard of Santi Angeli; first prior-general Lanfranc Septala; bull "Licet ecclesiae catholicae", 4 May 1256, "the foundation-charter of the 'Ordo Eremitarum S. Augustini'" [T-S14]. Schaff: Alexander IV in 1256 "definitely recognized the rule" [T-S15; the pope's number in the text is cut: "IV., 1256"]. DOCUMENTED. They spread quickly; Germany soon had forty houses (T-S14, count cut off in my read). By 1908 the order had 19 provinces and 275 foundations, 2,050 members [T-S14]. The French Revolution destroyed most of 157 houses [T-S14].
- 1330s to 1483: Monica's remains were brought from Ostia to Rome in 1430 [T-S14]. (Not a teaching, but an Augustinian link.)
- Today: "Pope Leo XIV ... is the first pope from the Order of St. Augustine (OSA)", an order of "thousands of members worldwide" [T-S27, CNA]. DOCUMENTED news fact.

- Pius XI, 1930: "The praise of Augustine has never ceased to be proclaimed in the Church of God, even by the Roman Pontiffs." [T-S4 para 2, English] The long list of papal praise in Q6 comes from this encyclical.
- Catechism (T-S8): cites Augustine in many places. Paragraph numbers with his own words or his name in the text: CCC 158, 230, 264, 385, 406, 1228, 1372, 1398, 1584, 1871, 1994, 2065, 2066, 2616, 2762. - Benedict XVI, 9 Jan 2008: "the greatest Father of the Latin Church" [T-S10]. Four audiences read: 9 Jan, 16 Jan, 20 Feb, 27 Feb 2008.

## 8. Doubtful and disputed

Sayings often credited to him (see OW section 8 for the full list)
- "In essentials unity, in non-essentials liberty, in all things charity": T-S3 says his gift of short formulas "has so often caused the celebrated saying to be attributed to him". The CE author implies he did not write it. DOCUMENTED as a doubt; the real source is not in my notes.
- "Sing and keep marching," "Pray as though everything depended on God," "The world is a book": see OW. 

Works wrongly credited
- Soliloquies and Meditations. T-S2: there are two "Soliloquies" and "Meditations" "which are certainly not authentic" (apart from the genuine two books of Soliloquies). T-S24: the Meditations "are not the work of Saint Augustine of Hippo, but were merely inspired by his writings", and in the Middle Ages names were pinned to such copies. DOCUMENTED.
- The Athanasian Creed (pseudo-Athanasian): "At times he has even been credited with" it; T-S3 says it is "undoubtedly of later date" with inspiration "in the formulae in De Trinitate". DOCUMENTED (CE).

Authorship of the Rule
- T-S14: the "Rule" came from several writings (On the Work of Monks, Letter 211, and "epistolae 355 and 356", On the Conduct of Clerics, PL 39, 1570, as CE writes it). T-S15: Augustine "prescribed no definite code". Search summary (T-L2, not read): neither Augustine nor Possidius mentions a rule; Letter 211 "is today generally recognized as genuine" but was contested by one scholar on style; Verheijen (1967) and Lawless are the key studies. Pius XI says he "gave an admirable rule" [T-S4 para 47]. The writer should say "a rule that goes back to his house and letters" unless a source on Verheijen is read.

Teachings the Church set aside or softened
- Unbaptized children: T-S3 says the Church "has not adopted the severity of the great Doctor in condemning such children to bodily pains, however slight"; the milder view of Aquinas prevailed; "Auctorem fidei", 28 Aug 1794, shows popes permit departing from Augustine here. Original sin and concupiscence: expressions "which seemed to identify original sin with concupiscence have given way to clearer formulae" [T-S3]. Trent: concupiscence "is of sin, and inclines to sin" but is not "truly and properly sin in those born again" [T-S7 session V, English]. DOCUMENTED.
- "Strict predestination" and "irresistible grace" in later systems: Orange (529) rejected predestination to evil and was silent on the rest [T-S5]; T-S3 says God's will to save all, and Christ's death for all, "Augustine never denied, but... left too much in the background". The Jansenist propositions were condemned (1653). DOCUMENTED.
- Force against heretics: T-S1 says he changed his mind and approved "rigid laws", never death. Modern readers dispute; no second source read. "Not found" for a modern scholarly view.

Orthodox disagreement
- Romanides: Augustine's teaching on original sin (his reading of Romans 5:12) "was first condemned by the Council of Orange in 529" [T-S21]. Conflict: the Council of Orange and Boniface II approved his line on grace (T-S5, T-S6, T-S20). The two statements cannot both be right; Romanides's is an Orthodox opinion; the Orange canons as I read them (T-S5) do not say Romans 5:12. Writer: do not state Romanides's claim as fact.
- Romanides: the Councils of Constantinople 1341, 1347, 1351 condemned Barlaam, "the unique source" being Augustine [T-S21]. Opinion.
- Hierotheos: an argument whether Gregory Palamas used De Trinitate (via Planoudes' translation before 1281). Hierotheos finds it "difficult to accept" [T-S22]. Disputed.
- Photios: Photios's review of Theodore of Mopsuestia against "Defenders of Original Sin" shows Photios against the Western doctrine; "Saint Photios was clearly unaware" of Augustine's role [T-S23, Sanidopoulos's comment, not Photios's words]. Florovsky: "Theodore wrote against St. Augustine's doctrine of original sin" [T-S23, quoted]. Opinion.
- Gennadius Scholarius (after 1453) criticized his teaching on the procession of the Spirit: quote in T-S21 begins "To say that the Hypostasis of the Spirit comes or proceeds from the Son..." (cut; I did not copy the rest). Nikodemos says "Gennadios Scholarios testifies" that people used to pray "Deliver us O God from the dialectics of Augustine" [T-S25].

Slips in the Catholic Encyclopedia articles (do not repeat)
- T-S2: "De Moribus Ecclesiae Catholicae" dated "at Rome, 368": he was 14 in 368. Other sources: 387-388 (not read). "Galatians (324)" is a typo. T-S1: "eighteen months' siege" conflicts with Possidius. T-S1: "ordained in 391" is supported (OW Letter 21).
