## SC-06 — The garden, Milan, September 386
- Status: full
- When and where: Late summer 386. CE: "at the age of thirty-three, smote him to the ground in the garden at Milan (September, 386)". BU p. 433: "the year 386, the thirty-second of his age, in the month of August or September." The two sources differ on his age (32 or 33) and agree on 386. Use "late summer 386" or "September 386, by the Catholic Encyclopedia". The place is a small garden ("hortulus") belonging to the lodging where he lived. The owner did not live there; Augustine and his friends used it as part of the house [CL-8 8.8.19]. Fig tree: 8.12.28.
- Who was there: Alypius (his friend, close at his side throughout). Earlier that day Ponticianus, a fellow African who served at the imperial palace, a Christian, visited them in the house [CL-8 8.6.14]. Nebridius was away [8.6.14]. Monica was in the house [8.12.30].
- What happened, in order:
  1. Ponticianus comes in, sees a book of the apostle Paul on the game table, and is glad. He tells them of Antony the Egyptian monk, whom they did not know, and of monasteries, including one in Milan outside the walls under Ambrose [CL-8 8.6.14-15].
  2. He tells how in Trier, while the emperor watched the circus games, he and three companions walked in the gardens by the walls. Two went off and found a cottage with the Life of Antony. One began to read, was changed, and said to his friend: "I have broken with our hopes; I serve God from this hour." The other joined him [8.6.15].
  3. Ponticianus leaves. Augustine, ashamed, cries to Alypius: "The unlearned rise and take heaven, and we, with our learning and no heart, roll in flesh and blood." He rushes out into the garden; Alypius follows [8.8.19].
  4. In the garden he pulls his hair, strikes his forehead, clasps his knee [8.8.20]. Old habits whisper "Are you letting us go?"; Continence appears with many examples and says "Throw yourself on him, do not be afraid" [8.11.26-27].
  5. He leaves Alypius, lies down under a fig tree, and weeps: "How long, how long, tomorrow and tomorrow? Why not now?" [8.12.28].
  6. He hears a voice from a nearby house, boy or girl, he does not know, chanting "tolle lege". He recalls that Antony was turned by a gospel text heard by chance. He goes back to where Alypius sits, takes up Paul's book, and reads silently the first lines he sees [8.12.29].
  7. He marks the place with a finger or some sign, closes the book, and tells Alypius with a calm face. Alypius asks to see it and reads on to the next verse, Romans 14:1, and takes it as meant for him [8.12.30].
  8. They go in to Monica and tell her. She rejoices [8.12.30].
- Details: a game table with Paul's book (8.6.14). He left the book of the apostle by Alypius when he rose (8.12.29). A fig tree. A nearby house with a singing voice. He read "in silence" (in silentio).
- Words spoken (exact Latin, our English):
  - Augustine: "quamdiu, quamdiu, 'cras et cras'? quare non modo? quare non hac hora finis turpitudinis meae?" Plain: "How long, how long, 'tomorrow and tomorrow'? Why not now? Why not in this hour an end to my shame?" [CL-8 8.12.28]
  - The voice: "tolle lege, tolle lege." Plain: "Take up and read, take up and read." [8.12.29]
  - Alypius's verse: "infirmum autem in fide recipite." Plain: "Welcome the one who is weak in faith." [8.12.30]
- Inner life: on the cry "tomorrow": he had said he would delay to follow God alone because nothing certain was in sight; "now the day had come" and conscience asked "where is your tongue?" [8.7.18]. On the divided will: not a journey by ships or feet, but "to will it strongly and wholly" [8.8.19]. At the end: "as if a light of certainty had been poured into my heart, all the darkness of doubt fled" [8.12.29].
- Testimony (Augustine, Confessions VIII.12.29, Latin, public-domain; about 130 words):
  "dicebam haec et flebam amarissima contritione cordis mei. et ecce audio vocem de vicina domo cum cantu dicentis et crebro repetentis, quasi pueri an puellae, nescio: 'tolle lege, tolle lege.' ... arripui, aperui, et legi in silentio capitulum quo primum coniecti sunt oculi mei: 'non in comessationibus et ebrietatibus, non in cubilibus et impudicitiis, non in contentione et aemulatione, sed induite dominum Iesum Christum et carnis providentiam ne feceritis in concupiscentiis.' nec ultra volui legere nec opus erat. statim quippe cum fine huiusce sententiae quasi luce securitatis infusa cordi meo omnes dubitationis tenebrae diffugerunt."
  Plain: "I said this and wept with the bitterest sorrow of my heart. And suddenly I heard a voice from the house next door, a boy's or a girl's, I do not know, chanting again and again: 'Take up and read, take up and read.' ... I took the book, opened it, and read in silence the first passage my eyes fell on: 'Not in feasting and drunkenness, not in beds and lust, not in quarrel and jealousy, but put on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make no plan for the flesh and its desires.' I did not want to read more, and did not need to. At once, as the sentence ended, a light of certainty seemed to pour into my heart, and all the darkness of doubt fled."
  (The "..." skips the middle of the section: his thought that children do not sing such a game, and his memory of Antony.)
- What changed: He gave up "tomorrow". Alypius joined him, and Monica's long grief turned to joy: "you changed her mourning into joy, far more richly than she had wished" [8.12.30].
- Not in the sources: the weather; the hour of day; the time of year inside the text (the date comes only from CE and BU); the fig tree's size; any words from Alypius before the verse; what Monica said (the Latin says only "gaudet... exultat et triumphat et benedicebat tibi"; the Latin gives no "leaps for joy"; "exultat" is "she exults"). The child's sex and identity. Whether Augustine used the finger as a bookmark: he says "digit or some other sign" (aut digito aut nescio quo alio signo).
- Sources: CL-8, CE, BU, OW-S1, W-S2.

## SC-07 — Baptism, Milan, Easter eve 387
- Status: thin on the day itself; full on what Augustine says of it
- When and where: Easter 387, Milan. CE: "baptism at Easter, 387" [CE]. Butler (via notes W-S3 p.445-446): baptized "by St. Ambrose on Easter-eve in 387, together with Alipius and his son Adeodatus, who was about fifteen years of age". The Confessions passage gives no date, no church, and does not name Ambrose in it [CL-9 9.6.14]. Possidius ch.1 names Ambrose as baptizer and says it was in the Easter days (via notes W-S1 ch.1).
- Who was there: Augustine; Alypius; Adeodatus. Not stated: Monica, Ambrose in person, or others at the font.
- What happened, in order:
  1. When the time came to give his name (for the list of those to be baptized), he left the country house and returned to Milan [CL-9 9.6.14].
  2. Alypius chose to be reborn with him. Alypius had put on humility and the discipline to walk barefoot on the frozen soil of Italy [9.6.14].
  3. They took Adeodatus, born of his sin, about fifteen, who "outstripped many serious and learned men in talent" [9.6.14].
  4. "We were baptized, and the anxiety of past life fled from us" [9.6.14].
  5. In those days he could not get enough of the sweetness of thinking on God's plan for human salvation. He wept at the hymns and songs of the church [9.6.14].
- Details: Alypius barefoot on frozen ground (glaciale). Hymns sung by the church's voices. Adeodatus's book "De magistro" records a talk between them when the boy was sixteen (9.6.14).
- Words spoken: none in the sources for the day.
- Inner life: on the boy, "I had nothing of my own in that boy but the sin" (in illo puero praeter delictum non habebam). The boy was brought up in God's teaching, which God, "no one else", inspired. God "took his life from the earth quickly", and Augustine remembers him "more at ease, fearing nothing of his boyhood or youth" [9.6.14].
- Testimony (Augustine, CL-9 9.6.14, Latin, public-domain; about 120 words):
  "adiunximus etiam nobis puerum Adeodatum ex me natum carnaliter de peccato meo. tu bene feceras eum. annorum erat ferme quindecim et ingenio praeveniebat multos graves et doctos viros. ... et baptizati sumus et fugit a nobis sollicitudo vitae praeteritae. nec satiabar illis diebus dulcedine mirabili considerare altitudinem consilii tui super salutem generis humani. quantum flevi in hymnis et canticis tuis, suave sonantis ecclesiae tuae vocibus commotus acriter! voces illae influebant auribus meis, et eliquabatur veritas in cor meum, et exaestuabat inde affectus pietatis, et currebant lacrimae, et bene mihi erat cum eis."
  Plain: "We also took with us the boy Adeodatus, born to me in the flesh from my sin. You had made him well. He was about fifteen, and in mind he was ahead of many serious, learned men. ... And we were baptized, and the worry about our past life left us. In those days I could not get enough of the wonderful sweetness of thinking about the depth of your plan for human salvation. How I wept at your hymns and songs, deeply moved by the voices of your sweetly singing church! Those voices flowed into my ears, and the truth melted into my heart, and a feeling of devotion rose up from it, and tears ran, and it was good for me to be with them."
- What changed: The worry over his past left him. He gained a settled place in the church and began to weep at its song.
- Not in the sources: Ambrose's words at the font; the church or the night's setting; Monica's presence; clothes; candles; any date other than "Easter 387" and Butler's "Easter-eve". Adeodatus's age is "about fifteen" (ferme quindecim). Do not say the boy was sad or willing.
- Sources: CL-9, CE, W-S3 p.445-446 and W-S1 ch.1 via the notes (not re-read), W-S2 Bk IX.

## SC-08 — The window at Ostia, 387
- Status: full
- When and where: 387, at Ostia on the Tiber, in the house where they stayed, "removed from the crowds, resting after the labor of a long journey, to take ship" [CL-9 9.10.23]. The day is "approaching" the day she would leave this life. Her death came on the ninth day of her illness (9.11.28). The exact date is not in the Confessions; Butler (W-S3 p.446, via the notes): before 13 November 387.
- Who was there: Augustine and Monica alone ("soli staremus").
- What happened, in order:
  1. Mother and son lean on a window that looks out on a garden inside the house [9.10.23].
  2. They talk "very sweetly", forgetting the past and reaching ahead, and ask what the eternal life of the saints will be, "which eye has not seen, nor ear heard" [9.10.23].
  3. Their talk climbs: pleasure of the senses, however bright, is not worth mention beside that life. They pass through all bodies and the sky, then reach their own minds, and pass beyond them [9.10.24].
  4. For one instant they touch eternal wisdom "with the whole beat of the heart", sigh, and "come back to the noise of our mouth" [9.10.24].
  5. Monica says: she has nothing left to want in this life [9.10.26].
- Details: the window; the garden within the house; Ostia; the quiet of a house away from crowds. Later he heard that earlier in Ostia she told friends, without him present, that nothing is far from God and she need not fear being buried far from home (9.11.28).
- Words spoken:
  - Monica: "fili, quantum ad me attinet, nulla re iam delector in hac vita. quid hic faciam adhuc et cur hic sim, nescio, iam consumpta spe huius saeculi. unum erat propter quod in hac vita aliquantum immorari cupiebam, ut te christianum catholicum viderem priusquam morerer. cumulatius hoc mihi deus meus praestitit, ut te etiam contempta felicitate terrena servum eius videam. quid hic facio?" [CL-9 9.10.26]
  - Plain: "Son, for my part, nothing in this life delights me any more. I do not know what I am still doing here, or why I am here, now that my hope in this world is used up. There was one thing for which I wanted to stay a little in this life: to see you a Catholic Christian before I died. My God has given me this in fuller measure, since I see you despising earthly happiness and serving him. What am I doing here?"
  - Augustine says of his own reply: "ad haec ei quid responderim non satis recolo" ("what I answered I do not remember well") [9.11.27].
- Inner life: Augustine says they spoke "not in these words, though in this manner" (non isto modo et his verbis) [9.10.26]. The long speech they made together: "si cui sileat tumultus carnis ... intra in gaudium domini tui" [9.10.25]: if all noise were silent, and God alone spoke, "is this not 'enter into the joy of your Lord'?"
- Testimony (Augustine, CL-9 9.10.23-24, Latin, public-domain; about 75 words):
  "conloquebamur ergo soli valde dulciter et, praeterita obliviscentes in ea quae ante sunt extenti, quaerebamus inter nos apud praesentem veritatem, quod tu es, qualis futura esset vita aeterna sanctorum ... et dum loquimur et inhiamus illi, attingimus eam modice toto ictu cordis."
  Plain: "So we talked, the two of us alone, very sweetly. Forgetting the past and reaching toward what lies ahead, we asked each other, in the presence of the truth, which is you, what the eternal life of the saints would be like ... And as we talked and longed for it, we touched it a little, with the whole beat of our heart."
- What changed: Monica says she is ready to go. Then she falls ill (SC-09). The Latin gives no number of days between the talk and the fever.
- Not in the sources: the time of day, the season, the weather; what the garden looked like; whether others were in the house at that moment; Monica's expression. Augustine says he gives the sense, not the words (9.10.26). The outline's "talk of the life of the saints" is correct; do not add quotes beyond the Latin above.
- Sources: CL-9, W-S3 p.446 via the notes, OW-S3 IX.10 (not re-read).

## SC-09 — Monica's death, Ostia, 387
- Status: full
- When and where: Ostia, 387, at the house. Monica died "on the ninth day of her illness, in the fifty-sixth year of her age, the thirty-third of mine" [CL-9 9.11.28]. Month and day are not in the Confessions. Butler says before 13 November 387 (W-S3 p.446).
- Who was there: Augustine; his brother (the Latin says only "fratrem meum", no name); his son Adeodatus; Evodius; "all the house"; later "many brothers and religious women" [9.11.27, 9.12.29-31].
- What happened, in order:
  1. She lies in bed with fever, "scarcely five days or not much more" (vix intra quinque dies... decubuit febribus) [9.11.27].
  2. One day she faints and is briefly cut off from those present. They run to her. She wakes and asks "Where was I?" Then, to the two sons, who are stunned with grief: "You are putting your mother here" [9.11.27].
  3. Augustine stays silent and holds back his tears. His brother says something about hoping she would die at home rather than abroad. She frowns at him, looks at Augustine, and says "See what he says" [9.11.27].
  4. Then to both: "Lay this body anywhere. Do not let care for it trouble you. Only this I ask: that you remember me at the Lord's altar, wherever you are" [9.11.27].
  5. She falls silent, her illness growing worse [9.11.27].
  6. On the ninth day of her sickness she dies [9.11.28].
  7. Augustine closes her eyes. Grief floods his heart, and with a violent command of the mind his eyes dry up. When she breathes her last, the boy Adeodatus cries out in grief and is silenced by all of them. Something boyish in Augustine is silenced too [9.12.29].
  8. Evodius takes the psalter and begins to sing a psalm. "All the house" answers: "misericordiam et iudicium cantabo tibi, domine" [9.12.31].
  9. Many brothers and religious women come. Augustine speaks with those who thought he should not be left alone. They think him free of pain; he says he scolds his weak feeling "in your ears" [9.12.31].
  10. The body is carried out; they go and return without tears. At the offering of the sacrifice by the tomb he does not weep. That day he is heavily sad in secret [9.12.32].
  11. He goes to bathe and is no better. He sleeps, wakes, and finds the grief lighter. He remembers a verse of Ambrose's hymn, "deus, creator omnium" [9.12.32]. Then he weeps for her, in God's sight [9.12.33].
- Details: the Latin names the verse sung: "misericordiam et iudicium cantabo tibi, domine". She had long cared for a grave beside her husband's; she gave that up [9.11.28]. Augustine says she called him "pius" and said she never heard a harsh word from him [9.12.30].
- Words spoken: Monica, "ubi eram?" ("Where was I?"); "ponitis hic matrem vestram" ("You are putting your mother here"); "ponite hoc corpus ubicumque. nihil vos eius cura conturbet. tantum illud vos rogo, ut ad domini altare memineritis mei, ubiubi fueritis." Plain: "Lay this body anywhere. Do not let any worry about it trouble you. I ask only this: that wherever you are, you remember me at the Lord's altar." [9.11.27]. To friends at Ostia, reported later: "nihil longe est deo" ("nothing is far from God") [9.11.28].
- Inner life: "hoc modo etiam meum quiddam puerile... iuvenali voce cordis cohercebatur et tacebat." He did not think it right to mourn with tears and groans, because that treats death as misery or as total ending, "but she was not dying miserably, nor wholly dying" [9.12.29]. Yet "a fresh wound, from the sweetest and dearest habit of living together suddenly torn" [9.12.30].
- Testimony (Augustine, CL-9 9.12.29, 31, Latin, public-domain; about 90 words):
  "tum vero ubi efflavit extremum, puer Adeodatus exclamavit in planctu atque ab omnibus nobis cohercitus tacuit. ... cohibito ergo a fletu illo puero, psalterium arripuit Evodius et cantare coepit psalmum. cui respondebamus omnis domus: 'misericordiam et iudicium cantabo tibi, domine.'"
  Plain: "Then, when she breathed her last, the boy Adeodatus cried out in grief, and was quieted by all of us and fell silent. ... So, when the boy had been checked from weeping, Evodius took up the psalter and began to sing a psalm. And all the house answered him: 'I will sing to you of mercy and judgment, Lord.'"
- What changed: Augustine loses his mother and holds back his tears until the night after the burial; the family's trip home ends.
- Not in the sources: "nine days of fever" is wrong: she lay five days or a little more, and died on the ninth day of her illness. No name for the brother. The cause of death. The house's room. Adeodatus's words. Evodius's age or his role beyond the psalter. What the burial place was (the Latin says only "iuxta sepulchrum", by the tomb). Alypius is not mentioned in these sections.
- Sources: CL-9, W-S3 p.446 via the notes, W-S2 Bk IX.

## SC-10 — Seized in church, Hippo, 391
- Status: full (Latin from OCR of Weiskotten; Letter 21 in English only)
- When and where: 391, in the Catholic church at Hippo (Hippo Regius). Year: Letter 21 is headed A.D. 391 (L21); CE and the notes give ordination 391. Possidius does not give the year. The building and the day are not named.
- Who was there: Augustine, "standing among the people" as a layman; Valerius, bishop, who spoke to the people; the Catholic congregation [POS ch.4]. Possidius is not said to be present; he relays what Augustine told "us" (nobis).
- What happened, in order:
  1. Before this, Augustine had come to Hippo to win a man: an imperial "agent in affairs" who feared God and wanted to hear him. The man kept putting off his vow day after day [POS ch.3]. (Notes W-S5 ch.3 n. identify him as Evodius: not re-read.)
  2. Valerius, pressed by church needs, tells the people to provide and ordain a priest for the city [POS ch.4].
  3. The Catholics, who knew Augustine's purpose and teaching, laid hands on him. He stood among them "secure and unaware of what was about to happen". As a layman he avoided only churches without a bishop [POS ch.4].
  4. They held him and, as is usual, brought him to the bishop to be ordained. All asked for it with one consent, with great zeal and clamor, "while he wept freely" [POS ch.4].
  5. Some took his tears as hurt pride and consoled him: the priesthood is close to the bishopric. He wept because he saw the dangers to his life from governing the church [POS ch.4].
- Details: "ubertim eo flente". The crowd's "clamor". The bishop's request to the people. Augustine had lived "almost three years" in Africa with those who served God, after he returned [POS ch.3].
- Words spoken: none in the sources. In Letter 21 he asks Valerius for time "till Easter" for "praying, reading, and weeping" [L21 §4].
- Inner life (Letter 21, A.D. 391, English, public-domain): §2: "the tears which some of my brethren perceived me shedding in the city at the time of my ordination, and because of which they did their utmost with the best intentions to console me, but with words which, through their not knowing the causes of my sorrow, did not reach my case at all." Plain: some fellow Christians saw him weeping in the city when he was ordained and tried kindly to comfort him, but they did not know why he wept, so their words did not help. §3: he was ordained just when he planned time to study Scripture. §4: "by praying, reading, and weeping ... a short time, say till Easter". Plain: he asks for a short time, until Easter, to pray, read, and weep.
- Testimony (Possidius, Vita 4, Latin, public-domain; about 125 words; OCR "iii" corrected to "in"):
  "iam scientes Catholici sancti Augustini propositum et doctrinam, manu iniecta (quoniam et idem in populo securus et ignarus quid futurum esset astabat) ... eum ergo tenuerunt et, ut in talibus consuetum est, episcopo ordinandum intulerunt, omnibus id uno consensu et desiderio fieri perficique petentibus, magnoque studio et clamore flagitantibus, ubertim eo flente: nonnullis quidem lacrimas eius, ut nobis ipse retulit, tunc superbe interpretantibus ... cum ille homo Dei, ut nobis retulit, maiori consideratione intelligeret et gemeret, quam multa et magna suae vitae pericula de regimine et gubernatione ecclesiae impendere iam ac provenire speraret, atque ideo fleret."
  Plain: "The Catholics already knew the aim and teaching of the holy Augustine, and they laid hands on him (for he stood among the people, unconcerned and unaware of what would happen) ... So they held him and, as is usual in such cases, brought him to the bishop to be ordained. Everyone asked for this with one voice and one wish, and demanded it with great eagerness and shouting, while he wept freely. Some, as he himself told us, took his tears as pride and tried to console him. But the man of God, as he told us, understood with deeper thought and groaned at how many great dangers to his life he expected from governing and guiding the church; and so he wept."
- What changed: He is a priest. He asks Valerius for time to study Scripture, and starts a monastery in the church (POS ch.5, not part of this card).
- Not in the sources: the church's name or look; the day or hour; what the crowd shouted; who laid the first hand; the agent's name in Possidius's text; what Valerius said beyond the request; Augustine's words of protest. Possidius gives Augustine's reason for tears as foreseen dangers. Letter 21 gives no reason beyond "the causes of my sorrow", which it does not list in §2.
- Sources: POS ch.3-4 and note 4, L21 §2-4, CE, W-S1 ch.3-4, OW-S5.

## Report

Garden year found: 386 (CE: September 386, age 33; BU p.433: 386, August or September). The two sources differ on his age; the Confessions IX.11.28 says 33 at Monica's death in 387.
