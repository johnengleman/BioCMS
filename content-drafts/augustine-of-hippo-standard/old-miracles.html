This list gathers the wonders told about Augustine and the wonders he himself recorded, with their sources and their status. No miracle connected with Augustine has been formally approved by the Church, as far as the sources show. He was honored as a saint by popular acclaim long before the Church had formal procedures for canonization, and his feast was already kept by the sixth century. Each account below says who reported it. Most come from two witnesses: his friend Possidius, bishop of Calama, and Augustine himself, who collected the healings at the shrines of the martyrs in his own day. The list makes no claim of scientific proof, and it does not dismiss any account. Where a story is late, legendary, or contested, the entry says so.

During Augustine’s life

People tormented by demons freed at his prayer, Hippo

While Augustine was a priest and later a bishop at Hippo, he was asked more than once to pray for people tormented by demons. Possidius writes from his own knowledge that Augustine prayed for them “with many tears,” and the demons left them. Possidius gives no names or dates. All his life, when sick people asked him to come and pray over them and lay his hands on them, Augustine went.

Status: reported by Possidius, who knew Augustine for almost forty years. No formal Church approval was found.

Source: Possidius, Life of Augustine, chapters 27 and 29, translated by H. T. Weiskotten (1919), https://tertullian.org/fathers/possidius_life_of_augustine_02_text.htm

A sick man healed at his bedside, Hippo, 430

In the summer of 430, when Augustine himself lay in his last illness during the siege of Hippo, a man brought a sick relative and asked him to lay his hand on him so that he would be healed. Augustine answered that if he had any power in such things he would surely have used it on himself first. The man replied that he had heard in a dream: “Go to the bishop Augustine that he may lay his hand upon him, and he shall be whole.” Augustine then laid his hand on the sick man, and, Possidius writes, “immediately God caused the sick man to depart from him healed.” Butler repeats the story.

Status: reported by Possidius, who knew Augustine for almost forty years. No formal Church approval was found.

Source: Possidius, Life of Augustine, chapter 29, translated by H. T. Weiskotten (1919), https://tertullian.org/fathers/possidius_life_of_augustine_02_text.htm

Also in: Alban Butler, Lives of the Saints, vol. 8, “St. Augustine, Bishop, C. and Doctor,” 28 August (public domain).

An ambush escaped by a wrong turn

Armed Donatist bands called Circumcellions watched the roads for Augustine and once set an ambush on the road he was to take. His guide lost the way, and Augustine and his companions reached their destination by another road. Only later did he learn what the mistake had saved him from, and he gave thanks to God with the others. Possidius tells it as an act of God’s providence, not as a miracle.

Status: reported by Possidius as providence. No formal Church approval was found.

Source: Possidius, Life of Augustine, chapter 12, translated by H. T. Weiskotten (1919), https://tertullian.org/fathers/possidius_life_of_augustine_02_text.htm

A toothache stopped by prayer, Italy, about 386–387

In the months before his baptism, Augustine was struck by a toothache so severe that he could not speak. He wrote on a wax tablet asking the friends with him to pray for him. They knelt, and the pain stopped at once. Augustine tells the story in Book IX of the Confessions; Butler retells it.

Status: reported by Augustine about himself. No formal Church approval was found.

Source: Alban Butler, Lives of the Saints, vol. 8, “St. Augustine, Bishop, C. and Doctor,” 28 August (public domain), citing Confessions IX.4.

Monica’s dream of the wooden rule, Africa

While Augustine was still a Manichee, his mother Monica dreamed that she stood on a wooden rule and that a shining young man told her that where she was, there her son was also. Augustine suggested that the dream might mean she would come over to his side. She answered at once that she had not been told “where he, there thou also,” but “where thou, there he also.” Augustine records the dream and her answer in the Confessions; years later he joined her in the Catholic faith.

Status: reported by Augustine. A dream, not a miracle examined by the Church.

Source: Augustine, Confessions, Book III, translated by E. B. Pusey (1838), https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/3296/pg3296.txt

A child’s voice in the garden, Milan, 386 by the traditional date

Weeping under a fig tree in the garden of his house in Milan, Augustine heard from a neighboring house a voice, “as of boy or girl, I know not,” chanting again and again, “Take up and read; Take up and read.” He tried to remember whether children sang such words in any game and could not recall any. He opened the letters of Paul, read Romans 13:13–14, and “all the darkness of doubt vanished away.” Augustine does not call the voice a miracle; he tells what he heard and what followed.

Status: reported by Augustine. Not a miracle examined by the Church.

Source: Augustine, Confessions, Book VIII, chapter 12, translated by E. B. Pusey (1838), https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/3296/pg3296.txt

Hippo held out during the siege, 430–431 (contested)

Butler writes that Hippo was not taken by the Vandals during the siege and that this was “ascribed to his prayer.” The same sources also say that after the siege was lifted in July 431 the city was abandoned by its people and burned by the enemy, and the Vandals took it by treaty a few years later. Possidius, who was inside the walls, does not claim a miracle.

Status: contested. Reported by Butler only. No formal Church approval was found.

Source: Alban Butler, Lives of the Saints, vol. 8, “St. Augustine, Bishop, C. and Doctor,” 28 August (public domain); Possidius, Life of Augustine, chapter 28, translated by H. T. Weiskotten (1919), https://tertullian.org/fathers/possidius_life_of_augustine_02_text.htm

Wonders Augustine witnessed and recorded

In the last book of the City of God, Augustine set down the healings he knew of at the shrines of the martyrs, above all at the relics of St. Stephen, which had been in Hippo for less than two years when he wrote. At Hippo he had those who were healed write short accounts, called libelli, and had them read to the people. The accounts “which have been published amount to almost seventy at the hour at which I write,” he says, and at Calama there were “incomparably more.” These wonders are credited to the martyrs, not to Augustine, but he is their witness and their recorder, and they belong to his story.

A blind man sees at the relics of Gervasius and Protasius, Milan, 386

When Ambrose found the bodies of the martyrs Gervasius and Protasius at Milan, a blind man recovered his sight at their relics. Augustine was living in Milan at the time and writes that it happened while he was there, in the presence of the imperial court.

Status: reported by Augustine, who credits the healing to the relics of a martyr, not to himself. No formal Church approval was found.

Source: Augustine, City of God, Book XXII, chapter 8, translated by Marcus Dods, https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/120122.htm

Innocentius healed before his second operation, Carthage

Innocentius, a former advocate at Carthage, was being treated for fistulas, and his surgeons had already operated on him once. On the day before a second operation, visitors came to pray with him, among them the bishop Aurelius and Saturninus of Uzalis. Innocentius threw himself on the ground and prayed with sobs; Augustine writes that he himself “could not pray at all.” The next morning the surgeon came with his knife in his hand and found “a perfectly firm cicatrix.” Augustine says it happened “in my presence, and under my own eyes.”

Status: reported by Augustine as an eyewitness. No formal Church approval was found.

Source: Augustine, City of God, Book XXII, chapter 8, translated by Marcus Dods, https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/120122.htm

Innocentia cured of a cancer of the breast, Carthage

Innocentia, a noblewoman of Carthage, had a cancer of the breast for which her physician said there was no remedy. She was told in a dream to ask the first woman who came out of the baptistery after baptism to make the sign of the cross over the sore. She did so and was cured. When her doctor heard of it, she answered him: “What great thing was it for Christ to heal a cancer, who raised one who had been four days dead?”

Status: reported by Augustine. No formal Church approval was found.

Source: Augustine, City of God, Book XXII, chapter 8, translated by Marcus Dods, https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/120122.htm

A doctor freed from gout at his baptism, Carthage

A doctor at Carthage who suffered from gout was freed from it when he was baptized. Before his baptism, Augustine was told, devils had appeared to him in a dream in the form of black boys. Augustine had the story from others.

Status: reported to Augustine by others and recorded by him. No formal Church approval was found.

An old comedian cured at his baptism, Curubis

Hesperius, who owned a farm at Zubedi, found it troubled by evil spirits. A priest offered the Eucharist there, and the trouble ceased. Hesperius kept some holy earth from Jerusalem hanging in his bedroom.

Florentius the tailor and the ring in the fish, Hippo

Florentius, a poor old tailor of Hippo, lost his coat and had no money to buy another, so he prayed aloud to the Twenty Martyrs, whose shrine stands in Hippo, to be clothed. Soon afterward he found a large fish and sold it to a cook named Catosus. The cook found a gold ring inside the fish and gave it to the tailor, saying: “See how the Twenty Martyrs have clothed you.”

An old nobleman named Martial converted and baptized

Martial, an old nobleman, refused to become a Christian. His son-in-law prayed for him at the oratory of St. Stephen and took flowers from there, which were laid at the old man’s head. At dawn Martial asked for the bishop and was baptized. His last words were “Christ, receive my spirit.”

Petronia cured, Uzalis

Many miracles were reported at Uzalis, where Bishop Evodius had placed relics of St. Stephen earlier. A noblewoman named Petronia was cured there, and Augustine asked her to write her own account, a libellus, so that it could be read to the people.

Paulus and Palladia healed at Easter, Hippo, about 425

A brother and sister from Caesarea in Cappadocia, Paulus and Palladia, had been seized with trembling after their mother cursed them, and they came to pray daily at St. Stephen’s shrine in Hippo. On Easter Sunday morning Paulus was holding the bars of the shrine when he fell down “as if asleep,” and he rose with the trembling gone; the church shouted its thanks to God. Two days later, while the account of his cure was being read and Augustine was preaching, Palladia touched the bars, fell in the same way, “and rose up cured.” Augustine was present for both. He wrote that “such a shout of wonder rose from men and women together, that the exclamations and the tears seemed like never to come to an end.” The Oxford Cult of Saints database, which the researchers consulted only in summary, links Sermons 321–324 to these days.

Status: reported by Augustine as an eyewitness. No formal Church approval was found.

Source: Augustine, City of God, Book XXII, chapter 8, translated by Marcus Dods, https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/120122.htm

After Augustine’s death

Miracles attributed to the relics at Pavia, 1695–1698

On 1 October 1695, workmen at the crypt altar of San Pietro in Ciel d’Oro in Pavia found a marble box with letters read as the name of Augustine. During the inquiry that followed, Josef Sciberras, Postulator General of the Augustinian Order, writes that “a number of miracles, attributed to the relics” hastened events. No list of these miracles was found. In 1728 the bishop of Pavia declared the relics authentic, and Pope Benedict XIII confirmed his judgment; that judgment concerned the relics, not the miracles.

Status: recorded by the Augustinian Order; no details found. No formal Church approval of any miracle was found.

Source: Josef Sciberras, OSA, “The Troubled Events of the Relics of Augustine of Hippo,” https://agostinjani.org/reflections-and-articles/josef-sciberras/the-troubled-events-of-the-relics-of-augustine-of-hippo-between-transfers-findings-and-devotion/

The child at the seashore (late medieval legend)

In a well-known story about Augustine today, he walks on a beach thinking about the Trinity and meets a child pouring the sea into a hole with a spoon or a shell; the child tells him he will empty the sea sooner than Augustine will understand the Trinity. The story is not in Possidius, in the Confessions, or in the Golden Legend that Caxton translated. In 1483 Caxton wrote that he did not find it in his exemplar “neither in English, French, ne in Latin,” and had seen it painted on an altar of the Blackfriars at Antwerp. Benozzo Gozzoli painted it at San Gimignano in the fifteenth century.

Status: legendary; a late medieval tale, not history.

Source: Shari Boodts, “The Child by the Seaside: A Medieval Story about Saint Augustine,” Medievalists.net, 2019, https://www.medievalists.net/2019/02/the-child-by-the-seaside-a-medieval-story-about-saint-augustine/
