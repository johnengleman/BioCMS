Augustine left more writing than almost any other early Christian: the Confessions, the City of God, letters, sermons, and treatises. The themes below are only a doorway. Each is given in his own words, from public-domain translations or in our own English from the Latin, with where he wrote it and how he lived it.

The restless heart

Augustine taught that people are made for God, and that nothing less than God can give them rest. Every other good thing, friendship, love, success, learning, is real and good, but it cannot carry the weight of a whole heart.

“For Thou madest us for Thyself, and our heart is restless, until it repose in Thee.”

— Augustine, Confessions I.1.1 (tr. E. B. Pusey, 1838)

These are the first lines of the Confessions, which Pusey’s translation dates to about 401, when Augustine was already bishop of Hippo. The Latin says fecisti nos ad te: “you made us toward yourself,” as if the heart were always moving in one direction. He had learned it the hard way. As a young man he looked for rest in friends, in love, in rhetoric, and in the Manichees, and when a close friend died he called him “half of my soul” and found that his life had become “a horror” to him.

The divided will and the gift of grace

Augustine knew from his own life that a person can know the good and still not want it with the whole heart. He did not think that a man could heal that split by effort alone. In his own story it was healed by God, at a moment he did not choose.

“Give me chastity and continency, only not yet.”

— Augustine, Confessions VIII.7.17 (tr. E. B. Pusey, 1838)

He quoted this prayer of his youth against himself, and added: “For I feared lest Thou shouldest hear me soon.” In the garden at Milan, by the traditional date in 386, he heard a child’s voice chanting “Take up and read,” opened the letters of Paul, and found that “all the darkness of doubt vanished away.” Years later, as a bishop, he still treated his own will with suspicion: he wrote that his own consecration as bishop while his predecessor was living should not have happened, and worked to stop it from happening to others.

God within

Augustine taught that God is not found by running outward but by turning inward. He had looked for God outside himself for years, he wrote, while God was within him all along.

“Too late loved I Thee, O Thou Beauty of ancient days, yet ever new! too late I loved Thee! And behold, Thou wert within, and I abroad, and there I searched for Thee.”

— Augustine, Confessions X.27.38 (tr. E. B. Pusey, 1838)

He wrote this in the tenth book of the Confessions, looking back over his whole search. A few lines later he adds: “Thou wert with me, but I was not with Thee.” Of his years in Milan he wrote, in words we translate from his Latin, “I entered into my inmost self, with you as my guide.” In the same city he had watched Ambrose read in silence, “his voice and tongue were at rest,” and had sat with his friends in the bishop’s room for a long time, watching him, before going away.

Love is the root

For Augustine, love of God and of neighbor is the measure of everything else. Even hard acts, such as correcting someone, must grow from love. His famous short rule is not permission to do anything at all; it is a test of the heart behind an act.

“Once for all, then, a short precept is given you: Love, and do what you will: whether you hold your peace, through love hold your peace; whether you cry out, through love cry out; whether you correct, through love correct; whether you spare, through love do you spare: let the root of love be within, of this root can nothing spring but what is good.”

— Augustine, Homily 7 on the First Epistle of John, section 8 (tr. H. Browne)

He said it while preaching on the first letter of John. Just before, he had compared a father who beats a boy out of love with a kidnapper who strokes a boy to steal him: “If you mark the persons, it is charity that beats, iniquity that caresses.” He applied the same test to reading the Bible: whoever thinks he understands the Scriptures but finds in them a meaning that does not build up “this twofold love of God and our neighbor, does not yet understand them as he ought” (On Christian Doctrine I.36.40). He lived the rule as a judge: when the Donatist bishop Crispinus was fined, Augustine asked the judge not to collect the fine, and later he and the other Catholic bishops worked to have the emperor’s condemnation of the Donatists lifted.

Two loves, two cities

In the City of God Augustine taught that what a people loves makes it what it is. All of history is the story of two “cities” living side by side: one built on love of self, the other on love of God. Peace, too, is a matter of love rightly ordered.

“Two cities have been formed by two loves: the earthly by the love of self, even to the contempt of God; the heavenly by the love of God, even to the contempt of self.”

— Augustine, City of God XIV.28 (tr. M. Dods)

Butler says the sack of Rome by Alaric in 410 moved Augustine to write the City of God, which he began in 413 and finished in 426. In Book XIX he defined peace: “The peace of all things is the tranquillity of order.” He lived by the same order at his own table, where herbs and lentils were served on earthenware, wood, or marble by choice, and where two lines of verse inscribed on the table warned off anyone who loved to gossip about the absent.

Sing and walk

Augustine taught that the Christian life is a journey still under way. Christians may sing on the road, as travelers do, but they must keep going and make progress in good.

“As travelers usually sing, sing, but walk. Comfort your labor by singing. Do not love laziness. Sing and walk.”

— Augustine, Sermon 256, section 3 (our translation of “canta et ambula”)

He said it in a sermon on the Alleluia. “What does ‘walk’ mean?” he asked, and answered: make progress, make progress in good. Do not wander off the road, he told his people, do not turn back, and do not stay behind. The saying is often given as “sing and keep marching,” but the Latin ambula means “walk,” and his picture is of travelers singing to lighten the road. He had wept at the hymns of the Church in Milan after his baptism: “How did I weep, in Thy Hymns and Canticles, touched to the quick by the voices of Thy sweet-attuned Church!”

The weight of the pastor’s office

Augustine taught that being a priest or bishop is the easiest work in the world if done carelessly, and the hardest if done as God asks. He did not think anyone was simply ready for it.

“If the duties of the office of a bishop, or presbyter, or deacon, be discharged in a perfunctory and time-serving manner, no work can be in this life more easy, agreeable, and likely to secure the favour of men … but none at the same time more miserable, deplorable, and worthy of condemnation in the sight of God.”

— Augustine, Letter 21, section 1, to Bishop Valerius, 391 (NPNF tr.)

He wrote this letter in 391, soon after the people of Hippo had seized him in church and demanded his ordination while he wept. He asked Valerius for a short time, “say till Easter,” to prepare “by praying, reading, and weeping.” As bishop he heard lawsuits until mealtime and sometimes fasted all day to finish them, never held the key of the church treasury, and in a besieged city, dying, had the penitential psalms hung on his wall.

God still works wonders

Augustine taught that miracles did not end with the Apostles. God still heals, he wrote, through the prayers of his people and at the shrines of the martyrs, though such wonders are less widely known than those in the Scriptures.

“Even now, therefore, many miracles are wrought, the same God who wrought those we read of still performing them, by whom He will and as He will.”

— Augustine, City of God XXII.8 (tr. M. Dods)

He wrote this in the last book of the City of God, after collecting almost seventy written accounts of healings at the relics of St. Stephen in Hippo in less than two years. He did not claim such power for himself. When a man asked him, on his deathbed, to lay hands on a sick relative, he answered that if he had any power in such things he would have used it on himself first.
