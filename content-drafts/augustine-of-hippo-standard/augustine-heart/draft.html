Saint Augustine Steals Pears He Does Not Want

There was a pear tree near the family vineyard, loaded with fruit that was neither pretty nor good to eat. One night, in his sixteenth year, a boy and a gang of his friends shook it bare and carried the pears away, and he threw his share to waste. He had better pears at home. Decades later he was a bishop in Africa. He told that night to God in a book, and he was still asking what he had loved in it. Not the pears, he decided. Alone, he would not have done it at all.⟦Augustine, Confessions II.4.9, II.6.12, II.8.16 (our translation).⟧

The book was the Confessions, written between 397 and 400, a whole life told as a prayer. The boy was Saint Augustine. He would serve the African port of Hippo as its bishop for more than thirty years. When Rome fell to the Goths he wrote the City of God. In 1298 the Church named him one of its four great Latin teachers, the Doctors of the Church. Yet for years his own divided will stood between him and the one thing he wanted most.⟦Benedict XVI, General Audience, 20 February 2008; Possidius, Life of Augustine 8, 31; Butler, Lives, vol. 8, p. 478; Catholic Encyclopedia, “Doctors of the Church.”⟧

He was born on 13 November 354 in Thagaste, a small town in Numidia about fifty miles southeast of Hippo. His father Patricius was a citizen of the town with very little money. His mother Saint Monica was a believer, and she had her baby signed with the cross. He also had a brother and a sister. Patricius came to the faith only late in life.⟦Weiskotten, introduction to Possidius, Life, and note 1 to ch. 1; Augustine, Confessions I.11.17, II.3.6; Butler, Lives, vol. 8, p. 405.⟧

When he was still a small boy, a sudden pain in his stomach brought him close to death. He begged his mother for baptism, and she hurried to arrange it. Then he got well just as suddenly, and the baptism was put off. The thinking was that sins after baptism would carry a heavier guilt and a greater danger, so it seemed better to wait. By then the whole house believed except his father.⟦Augustine, Confessions I.11.17.⟧

Patricius was proud of his clever son. He spent more than the family could afford to send him to school in the nearby town of Madaura, and the neighbors praised him for it. Saint Augustine wrote that his father cared little how chaste he grew up, as long as he could speak well. In his sixteenth year the boy was brought home from Madaura while money was gathered to send him on to Carthage, and he had nothing to do. “The briars of lust grew up over my head,” he wrote, “and there was no hand to pull them out.”⟦Augustine, Confessions II.3.5–6 (our translation); Catholic Encyclopedia, “St. Augustine of Hippo.”⟧

That was the year of the pear tree. He and his gang had kept their games going in the open until the dead of night, as their bad habit was. They ate a few of the pears, and the rest were meant for the pigs. In his book he spent pages asking why. “It was ugly, and I loved it,” he wrote. In the end he decided that what he had loved was the company. “When someone says, ‘Come on, let’s do it,’ one is ashamed not to be shameless.”⟦Augustine, Confessions II.4.9, II.8.16–9.17 (our translation).⟧

The night did not change him. Soon afterward he went on to Carthage, the great city by the sea, about a hundred and fifty miles from home. He was a young student there in 371 when his father died, baptized just before the end.⟦Weiskotten, note 1 to Possidius, Life 1, and note to ch. 2 (“pater antea defunctus erat”); Catholic Encyclopedia, “St. Augustine of Hippo.”⟧

Saint Augustine Reads Cicero and Joins a Sect

“I came to Carthage,” he wrote, “and a cauldron of shameful loves sizzled all around me.” He said that inside he was hungry for God and did not feel the hunger. “I did not love yet, and I loved to love.” Before long a woman came to live with him. He never tells us her name. They did not marry, but he stayed true to her for years. She bore him a son named Adeodatus.⟦Augustine, Confessions III.1.1 (our translation); Butler, Lives, vol. 8, p. 412, note 8.⟧

He was training to be a public speaker, and he wanted to stand out. At nineteen he came in the usual course of study to a book by Cicero called the Hortensius. His father had been dead two years. It urged its readers to love wisdom. He did not read it to sharpen his tongue, though that was the skill his mother’s money was buying. What won him was what the book said.⟦Augustine, Confessions III.4.7.⟧

“That book changed my feelings. It turned my prayers toward you yourself, Lord, and it gave me new hopes and longings. Suddenly every empty hope lost its worth for me, and with an unbelievable fire in my heart I longed for the deathlessness of wisdom. I had begun to rise up and come back to you. … How I burned, my God, how I burned to fly back to you from earthly things, and I did not know what you were doing with me!”⟦Augustine, Confessions III.4.7–8 (our translation).⟧

— Saint Augustine, Confessions, Book III

This was the desire of his life: a wisdom that would never die. It would pull at him for years while he ran after other things. Long afterward he began the book of his life, and he set that longing in its first lines as a prayer: “You made us toward yourself, and our heart is restless until it rests in you.”⟦Augustine, Confessions I.1.1, VIII.7.17 (our translation).⟧

One thing cooled the fire. The name of Christ was not in Cicero’s book. He had taken in that name with his mother’s milk, and nothing that lacked it could carry him away completely, however learned and polished and true it was. So he opened the Scriptures to see what they were like. Beside the grand style of Cicero, they seemed to him not worth comparing. “My swollen pride shrank from their modest style,” he wrote, “and my sharp eye could not see into their depths.”⟦Augustine, Confessions III.4.8, III.5.9 (our translation).⟧

So he fell in with the Manichees. They talked about truth all the time. “They kept saying, ‘Truth, truth,’ and they said it to me often,” he wrote, “and it was nowhere in them.” The sect had two ranks. The Elect lived on what ordinary members brought them, and those ordinary members were called hearers. Saint Augustine became a hearer and stayed about nine years, from nineteen to twenty-eight. He read the sect’s books, defended its teaching, and drew his friends in after him.⟦Augustine, Confessions III.6.10, IV.1.1 (our translation); Weiskotten, introduction; Catholic Encyclopedia, “St. Augustine of Hippo.”⟧

His body pulled the other way. In the first years of his youth he had even prayed for purity, and later he set that prayer down against himself: “Give me chastity and self-control, but not yet.” He knew why he had added the last words. “I was afraid you would hear me too soon, and too soon heal me of the sickness of desire, which I wanted fed more than put out.”⟦Augustine, Confessions VIII.7.17 (our translation).⟧

Saint Monica heard her son speak against the faith, and she could not bear it. She would not let him live in her house or eat at her table.⟦Augustine, Confessions III.11.19.⟧

Saint Monica Weeps for Her Son

He wrote that she wept for him more than mothers weep over a body carried out to be buried. By the faith God had given her, she could see that her son was dead. Wherever she prayed, her tears ran down and watered the ground. God heard her and did not despise her tears. He comforted her with a dream.⟦Augustine, Confessions III.11.19.⟧

In the dream she was standing on a wooden rule, the kind used to draw straight lines, and she was worn out with grief. A young man came toward her, shining and cheerful, and he smiled at her. He asked her why she was sad and why she wept every day. She told him she was mourning her son’s ruin. He told her to stop worrying, and to look and see: where she was, her son was too. She looked, and there he stood beside her on the same rule.⟦Augustine, Confessions III.11.19.⟧

She told her son. He tried to turn the dream his way: perhaps it meant that she would one day stand where he stood. She answered at once. “It was not said to me, ‘Where he is, there you are,’ but ‘Where you are, there he is.’” He wrote that her quick answer shook him more than the dream itself. After it she let him live with her again and share her table.⟦Augustine, Confessions III.11.19–20.⟧

He did not move. Saint Monica went to a bishop who had grown up in the Church and knew the Scriptures well. She asked him to talk with her son and argue him out of his errors. The bishop refused. The young man was still unteachable, he said. He was puffed up with his new sect, and he had already upset many simple people with his small, clever questions. “Leave him where he is,” the bishop said. “Only ask the Lord for him. By reading he will find out what this error is and how great the wrong.”⟦Augustine, Confessions III.12.21 (our translation).⟧

The bishop knew the sect from inside. When he was a little boy, his own mother had handed him over to the Manichees. He had read and copied out nearly all their books, and then he had left them on his own, without anyone arguing him out of it. Still Saint Monica would not give up. She pressed him and wept, until he grew tired and a little annoyed. “Go away from me,” he said. “As you live: it cannot be that the son of these tears will perish.” She took those words as if they had sounded from heaven, and she often reminded her son of them.⟦Augustine, Confessions III.12.21 (our translation).⟧

Saint Augustine Loses His Closest Friend

In those years he went home to Thagaste to teach. There he grew close to a young man of his own age. They had grown up together, gone to school together, and played together, and now they shared the same studies. Saint Augustine does not give his name. He does tell what he did to him: he led his friend away from the faith and into the sect’s fables, and Saint Monica mourned over it. Saint Augustine could not live without him. The friendship was sweeter to him than anything else in his life then, and it lasted scarcely a year.⟦Augustine, Confessions IV.4.7; Catholic Encyclopedia, “St. Augustine of Hippo.”⟧

Then the friend fell ill with fever. He lay a long time unconscious in a deadly sweat, and they gave up hope for him. While he lay like that he was baptized without knowing it. Saint Augustine did not mind, since he was sure the friend would keep the views he had learned from him and not the rite done to his senseless body. But the friend recovered. As soon as he could talk, Saint Augustine joked with him about the baptism and expected him to laugh.⟦Augustine, Confessions IV.4.8.⟧

The friend drew back from him as if from an enemy. With a sudden, startling boldness he told Saint Augustine to stop talking like that if he wanted to stay his friend. Saint Augustine was stunned. He decided to wait until his friend was strong again and then deal with him as he liked. A few days later, while Saint Augustine was away, the fever came back and the friend died.⟦Augustine, Confessions IV.4.8.⟧

His heart went dark with grief, and everything he looked at was death. His home town became a torture to him and his father’s house a strange misery. Everything they had shared turned into pain. His eyes looked for his friend everywhere and did not find him. He asked his soul why it was so sad, and it had no answer. When he told it to hope in God, it would not obey. He wrote that it was right not to, because the friend it had lost was more real than the god of the sect, an empty phantom. Only weeping was sweet.⟦Augustine, Confessions IV.4.9, IV.7.12.⟧

“I was amazed that other people lived. He was dead, and I had loved him as if he would never die. I was more amazed that I lived, since I was his other self. Someone said well of his friend: ‘half of his soul.’ I felt that his soul and mine had been one soul in two bodies. That is why life was a horror to me: I did not want to live as half a man.”⟦Augustine, Confessions IV.6.11 (our translation).⟧

— Saint Augustine, Confessions, Book IV

He carried his soul around torn and bleeding, and he could find no place to set it down. It found no rest in pleasant groves or games or songs, at feasts or in bed, or even in books. Everything filled him with horror, even the daylight. “Where could my heart run from my heart? Where could I run from myself? Where would I not follow me? And yet I ran away from my home town.”⟦Augustine, Confessions IV.7.12 (our translation).⟧

Saint Augustine Tricks His Mother and Sails Away

He went back to Carthage, and from 374 he taught public speaking there for nine years. The students wore him down. They burst into classrooms with wild faces and wrecked the order a teacher had set up for his pupils. Friends told him that students in Rome were quieter and kept better discipline, and they promised him more money and a higher rank there. About 383 he decided to go.⟦Weiskotten, introduction; Augustine, Confessions V.8.14.⟧

His mother mourned bitterly at his going, and she followed him all the way to the sea. There she held on to him hard. He must stay, or he must take her with him. So he deceived her. He pretended he would not leave a friend until a wind came up and the friend could sail. “I lied to my mother,” he wrote, “and to such a mother.” She would not go home without him. He barely talked her into spending that night at the memorial of Saint Cyprian, the place nearest the ship. That night he sailed in secret. She stayed behind, praying and weeping.⟦Augustine, Confessions V.8.15 (our translation).⟧

What was she asking God for with all those tears, he wrote later, except that he would not let her son sail? God did not grant it. God looked deeper and answered the heart of her desire instead, to make her son what she had always prayed for. The wind blew and filled the sails, and the shore slipped out of sight. In the morning she was on that shore, wild with grief. She accused him of deceit and cruelty. Then she turned back to praying for him and went home to her usual life. “Through my own desires you were carrying me off, to put an end to those very desires.”⟦Augustine, Confessions V.8.15 (our translation).⟧

In Rome he fell ill with a fever that kept getting worse. He wrote that he was going down to the dead with every wrong he had ever done. This time he did not even want baptism. He admitted that he had been better as a sick little boy. His mother did not know he was ill, but far away she was praying for him. God heard her where she was, and had mercy on him where he was. He recovered.⟦Augustine, Confessions V.9.16.⟧

When he began to teach, he found that Roman students had a trick of their own. To avoid paying their teacher, many of them would plot together and suddenly move to another. Then the city of Milan asked the prefect of Rome to send it a teacher of public speaking. Saint Augustine gave a trial speech, and the prefect Symmachus sent him north. In Milan the bishop was Saint Ambrose. “I was led to him by you without knowing it,” Saint Augustine wrote to God, “so that through him I might be led to you knowing it.”⟦Augustine, Confessions V.12.22, V.13.23 (our translation).⟧

Saint Augustine Cannot Reach the Bishop of Milan

By then the sect had let him down. For almost nine years at Carthage he had waited for its famous teacher, Faustus. Other Manichees had promised him that Faustus would answer all his questions. When Faustus came at last, he was a pleasant man who said the usual things more charmingly. “But what good to my thirst,” Saint Augustine asked, “was a most elegant servant of costly cups?” His eagerness for the sect was broken. He stayed only because he had found nothing better.⟦Augustine, Confessions V.6.10, V.7.13 (our translation).⟧

Saint Ambrose welcomed the new teacher like a father, and Saint Augustine began to love him. At first he loved him only as a man who was kind to him, and he went to hear him preach so that he could judge his speaking. He hung on the bishop’s words and paid no attention to their meaning. Yet he was slowly coming closer without knowing it. Saint Ambrose explained the hard passages of the Old Testament as pictures of deeper things, and the Catholic faith began to look as if it could be defended.⟦Augustine, Confessions V.13.23, V.14.24.⟧

Saint Monica followed him by land and sea. When the voyage turned dangerous, she comforted the sailors themselves, though it is usually sailors who comfort frightened passengers. She promised them they would arrive safely, because God had promised it to her in a vision. In Milan her son told her that he was no longer a Manichee, though not yet a Catholic Christian. She was glad as if she had expected it, and her heart stayed calm. She told him very calmly that, trusting in Christ, she believed she would see him a faithful Catholic before she left this life. Then she prayed harder and wept more, and she hung on every word Saint Ambrose said. She loved him like an angel of God.⟦Augustine, Confessions VI.1.1.⟧

Her son wanted to put his own questions to the bishop, but he could not get near him. Crowds of busy people with their troubles kept him from the bishop’s ear and mouth, and Saint Ambrose served them all. In the few minutes he had to himself, he ate or he read. Anyone could walk in, and no visitor was announced, so Saint Augustine came in with the others and watched.⟦Augustine, Confessions VI.3.3.⟧

“When he read, his eyes moved over the pages and his heart searched for the meaning, while his voice and tongue were still. Often when we were there, we saw him reading like this, silently, and never otherwise. We sat a long time in silence, for who would dare to burden a man so intent? Then we went away.”⟦Augustine, Confessions VI.3.3 (our translation).⟧

— Saint Augustine, Confessions, Book VI

Saint Augustine guessed at the reasons. Perhaps the bishop did not want to be drawn into explaining hard lines to whoever was listening, or perhaps he was saving his voice, which tired very easily. Whatever the reason, the one man who might have helped him sat in the same room and could not be reached. “Ambrose did not know my tides,” he wrote, “nor the pit of my danger.”⟦Augustine, Confessions VI.3.3 (our translation).⟧

In Milan he also read books of the Platonist philosophers, and they turned him inward. “I entered into my inmost self, with you as my guide,” he told God. There he saw a light above his mind that had made him, and he trembled with love and awe. He also found that he was far from God, “in a land of unlikeness.”⟦Augustine, Confessions VII.10.16 (our translation); Catholic Encyclopedia, “St. Augustine of Hippo.”⟧

His mother worked hard to find him a wife. She hoped that baptism would wash him clean once he was married. A girl was asked for, but she was almost two years too young to marry, so they waited. The woman he had lived with for so many years stood in the way of the marriage, and about 385 she was sent back to Africa. “My heart, which clung to her, was torn and wounded and bleeding. She went back to Africa and vowed to you never to know another man. She left my son with me.”⟦Augustine, Confessions VI.13.23, VI.15.25 (our translation); Butler, Lives, vol. 8, p. 412, note 8.⟧

He could not bear the two years of waiting, so he took another woman who was not his wife. The old wound did not heal. He wrote that after the first fever and sharp pain it festered, and it hurt more coldly but with less hope. The prayer of his youth still held him: not yet.⟦Augustine, Confessions VI.15.25.⟧

Saint Augustine Weeps Under a Fig Tree

The house where he lodged in Milan had a small garden. Its owner did not live there, so Saint Augustine and his friends used it as part of the house. Late in the summer of 386 a visitor named Ponticianus came to see him and his close friend Saint Alypius. He was an African like them and a Christian, and he held a post at the emperor’s palace. He noticed a book lying on the game table and picked it up. It was the letters of the apostle Saint Paul, and he smiled at Saint Augustine in glad surprise.⟦Augustine, Confessions VIII.6.14, VIII.8.19; Butler, Lives, vol. 8, p. 433; Catholic Encyclopedia, “St. Augustine of Hippo.”⟧

So he began to talk about Saint Antony, the monk of Egypt, and he was amazed that they had never heard of him. He told them of monasteries full of men who served God, and of one just outside the walls of Milan, under the care of Saint Ambrose. Then he told a story of his own. Once, at the city of Trier, while the emperor watched the games, he and three friends had gone walking in the gardens by the walls. Two of them wandered into a poor cottage and found a book, the Life of Antony. One began to read, and as he read he caught fire. At court, he said to his friend, their highest hope was to become friends of the emperor, through danger after danger, and who knew when? “But if I want to be a friend of God, look, I can become one now.” He read on, and he was changed inside. “I have now broken away from those hopes of ours and decided to serve God,” he said, “and I begin from this hour, in this place.” The other joined him.⟦Augustine, Confessions VIII.6.14–15 (our translation).⟧

As Ponticianus talked, Saint Augustine saw himself. Perhaps twelve years had flowed away since he read the Hortensius at nineteen and was stirred to seek wisdom, and still he had put it off. He had always told himself he was waiting because nothing certain was in sight. Now his own conscience turned on him. “Where is your tongue? You used to say you would not throw off the load of vanity because the truth was uncertain. Look, now it is certain, and that load still presses on you.” He was eaten up inside with a terrible shame.⟦Augustine, Confessions VIII.7.17–18 (our translation).⟧

Ponticianus finished his story and left. Saint Augustine turned on Saint Alypius, his face as troubled as his mind. “What is wrong with us? What is this? What did you hear? The unlearned rise up and take heaven, and we, with our learning and no heart, look where we are rolling in flesh and blood!” Saint Alypius stared at him, stunned and silent. Saint Augustine rushed out into the garden. Saint Alypius followed close behind, because he would not leave him like this. They sat down as far from the house as they could.⟦Augustine, Confessions VIII.8.19 (our translation).⟧

Saint Augustine raged at himself. Every bone in him cried out that he must go to God, and he would not go. To get there took no ships and no carriages and no feet. It took only the will to go, if he willed it strongly and wholly. He tore at his hair and struck his forehead and locked his hands around his knee. His old loves, his “trifles of trifles,” tugged at the garment of his flesh and muttered behind his back. “Are you letting us go? From this moment we will not be with you ever again.” And habit asked him, “Do you think you can do without them?”⟦Augustine, Confessions VIII.8.19–20, VIII.11.26 (our translation).⟧

On the other side, where he was afraid to cross, he saw Self-Control, calm and cheerful, holding out her hands to take him in. Her hands were full of good examples: boys and girls, young people, grave widows, and women who had stayed virgins into old age. She smiled at him as if to tease him on. “Can you not do what these men and women have done? … Why do you stand on yourself, and so do not stand? Throw yourself on him. Do not be afraid. He will not pull away and let you fall. Throw yourself on him without worry. He will catch you and heal you.” Saint Augustine blushed because he could still hear the old whispers, and he hung there undecided. Beside him Saint Alypius waited in silence.⟦Augustine, Confessions VIII.11.27 (our translation).⟧

Then all his misery came together before his heart, and a great storm broke in him, bringing a great rain of tears. He got up and left Saint Alypius, so that he could weep alone. He threw himself down under a fig tree, he did not know how. Then he let the tears run. “How long, how long, ‘tomorrow and tomorrow’? Why not now? Why not in this hour an end to my shame?”⟦Augustine, Confessions VIII.12.28 (our translation).⟧

“I said this and wept with the bitterest sorrow of my heart. And suddenly I heard a voice from the house next door, a boy’s or a girl’s, I do not know, chanting again and again: ‘Take up and read, take up and read.’ … I took the book, opened it, and read in silence the first passage my eyes fell on: ‘Not in feasting and drunkenness, not in beds and lust, not in quarrel and jealousy, but put on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make no plan for the flesh and its desires.’ I did not want to read more, and did not need to. At once, as the sentence ended, a light of assurance seemed to pour into my heart, and all the darkness of doubt fled.”⟦Augustine, Confessions VIII.12.29 (our translation).⟧

— Saint Augustine, Confessions, Book VIII

When he heard the voice, his face changed, and he stopped crying to think. Did children sing words like those in any game? He could not remember ever hearing them. Then he remembered Saint Antony. He had come by chance upon a Gospel reading and taken it as spoken to him: “Go, sell all that you have, and give to the poor.” So Saint Augustine took the voice as a command from God. He hurried back to where Saint Alypius sat and picked up the book of the apostle that he had left there.⟦Augustine, Confessions VIII.12.29 (our translation).⟧

He marked the place with his finger or some other sign, closed the book, and with a calm face told Saint Alypius. His friend asked to see the page and read on to the next verse: “Welcome the one who is weak in faith.” Saint Alypius took it for himself, and he joined Saint Augustine without any troubled delay. Then the two of them went in to Saint Monica and told her. She rejoiced and triumphed and blessed God. Years before, God had shown her son to her standing beside her on a rule. Saint Augustine wrote that he now stood on that rule of faith. “You changed her mourning into joy, far more richly than she had wished.”⟦Augustine, Confessions VIII.12.30 (our translation).⟧

Saint Augustine and His Mother at the Window

Only a few days were left before the autumn holidays of the grape harvest, so he did not quit his post with a public scene. He waited, and at the end of the holidays he told Milan to find its students another seller of words. He had chosen to serve God, and his chest hurt so that he could hardly breathe. In a letter to Saint Ambrose he told of his past errors and his new purpose. The bishop told him which of the prophets to read first. Saint Augustine could not make sense of it, so he put it aside for later.⟦Augustine, Confessions IX.2.2, IX.5.13 (our translation).⟧

At Easter 387 Saint Ambrose baptized him. With him came Saint Alypius, who had taken up a hard discipline and walked barefoot on the frozen soil of Italy. Adeodatus came too. He was about fifteen, and his mind was already ahead of many serious and learned men. “You had made him well,” his father told God. “I had nothing of my own in that boy but the sin.” The boy’s mind filled his father with awe. “We were baptized, and the worry about our past life left us.” In the days that followed he wept at the hymns of the church. “Those voices flowed into my ears,” he wrote, “and the truth melted into my heart.”⟦Possidius, Life of Augustine 1; Augustine, Confessions IX.6.14 (our translation); Catholic Encyclopedia, “St. Augustine of Hippo.”⟧

Evodius, a young man from their home town, joined them. He had left the emperor’s service and was baptized before them. Together the little company made a plan: they would go home to Africa and serve God there. They set out for the coast. At Ostia, the port at the mouth of the Tiber, they stopped in a house away from the crowds. There they rested after the long trip and waited for a ship. The day Saint Monica would leave this life was near. God knew it, and they did not.⟦Augustine, Confessions IX.8.17, IX.10.23; Catholic Encyclopedia, “St. Augustine of Hippo.”⟧

One day mother and son stood alone, leaning on a window that looked out on the garden inside the house. They talked very sweetly, forgetting what lay behind them and reaching toward what lay ahead. Together they asked each other what the eternal life of the saints would be like, the life that no eye has seen and no ear has heard. He wrote that they opened the mouth of the heart wide toward the streams of God’s fountain, the fountain of life.⟦Augustine, Confessions IX.10.23 (our translation).⟧

Their talk climbed. They came to see that beside the joy of that life, the brightest pleasures of the senses were not even worth mentioning. Step by step they passed in thought through all bodily things and through the sky itself, where the sun and moon and stars shine. They went higher still, inward, and came to their own minds and went beyond them, to the wisdom that always was and always will be. “And as we talked and longed for it, we touched it a little, with the whole beat of the heart.” Then they sighed and came back down to the noise of their own voices, where every word has a beginning and an end.⟦Augustine, Confessions IX.10.24 (our translation).⟧

At nineteen he had longed for the deathlessness of wisdom. At a window in Ostia he touched it for one heartbeat, with his mother beside him. As they talked, the world and all its pleasures grew cheap to them, and she spoke.⟦Augustine, Confessions III.4.7, IX.10.24–26.⟧

“Son, for my part, nothing in this life delights me any more. I do not know what I am still doing here, or why I am here, now that my hope in this world is used up. There was one thing for which I wanted to stay a little in this life: to see you a Catholic Christian before I died. My God has given me this in fuller measure, since I see you despising earthly happiness and serving him. What am I doing here?”⟦Augustine, Confessions IX.10.26 (our translation).⟧

— Saint Monica to Saint Augustine at Ostia, Confessions, Book IX

He could not remember well what he answered.⟦Augustine, Confessions IX.11.27.⟧

Saint Monica Dies at Ostia

Within five days or a little more she took to her bed with fever. One day during the illness she fainted and for a moment was gone from them. They ran to her. She came back quickly and looked at her two sons standing over her. “Where was I?” she asked, as if she were searching. When she saw how stunned with grief they were, she spoke again. “You are putting your mother here,” she said.⟦Augustine, Confessions IX.11.27 (our translation).⟧

Saint Augustine said nothing and held back his tears. His brother said something about hoping she would die at home, as a happier thing than dying in a foreign land. She gave him an anxious look and scolded him with her eyes for thinking that way. She turned to Saint Augustine. “See what he says.” Then she spoke to them both. “Lay this body anywhere. Do not let any worry about it trouble you. I ask only this: that wherever you are, you remember me at the Lord’s altar.”⟦Augustine, Confessions IX.11.27 (our translation).⟧

Her son knew how much she had once cared about her grave. She and her husband had lived together in harmony, and she had prepared a grave for herself beside his body. Now that wish had left her, and her son was glad. He learned later that at Ostia she had talked one day with some of his friends about how little this life is worth and how good death is. They were amazed at her and asked whether she was not afraid to leave her body so far from home. “Nothing is far from God,” she said, “and there is no need to fear that he will not know where to raise me up at the end of the world.”⟦Augustine, Confessions IX.11.28 (our translation).⟧

The illness grew worse, and she said no more. On the ninth day of her sickness she died, in the fifty-sixth year of her age and the thirty-third of his. He closed her eyes. A great grief flowed into his heart and was ready to pour out in tears, and he forced his eyes to drink it back. When she breathed her last, the boy Adeodatus cried out in grief, and all of them hushed him. Something childish in Saint Augustine was hushed at the same time. Then Evodius took up the book of Psalms and began to sing, and the whole house answered him: “I will sing to you of mercy and judgment, Lord.”⟦Augustine, Confessions IX.11.28, IX.12.29, IX.12.31 (our translation).⟧

He did not think it right to mourn her with tears and groans, as people mourn a death that is a misery or a final end. She was not dying in misery, and she was not wholly dying. Still, the wound was fresh. Their life together had been the sweetest and dearest habit of his life, and it was torn away in a moment. He had cared for her in her last illness, and she had spoken tenderly to him and called him a good son. With great love she told him that she had never heard a harsh or hurtful word from his mouth. Many friends and women who served God came to the house. They thought he felt no pain. Inside, in God’s hearing, he was scolding his own weakness.⟦Augustine, Confessions IX.12.29–31 (our translation).⟧

The body was carried out, and he went and came back without tears. The Holy Sacrifice was offered for her beside the grave, and still he did not weep. All that day he was heavy with a sadness he kept to himself. He went to the baths, and they did nothing for him. Then he slept, and when he woke the grief was lighter. A line of Saint Ambrose’s hymn came back to him: “God, creator of all.” Little by little he remembered his mother as she had been, and he let himself weep in God’s sight, for her and for himself.⟦Augustine, Confessions IX.12.32–33 (our translation).⟧

“I let go the tears I had held back, to flow as much as they wanted, and I spread them like a bed beneath my heart. And my heart rested on them, because your ears were there, and not the ears of some man who would proudly misread my weeping.”⟦Augustine, Confessions IX.12.33 (our translation).⟧

— Saint Augustine, Confessions, Book IX

Hippo Makes Saint Augustine a Priest

The trip home was put off. The party went back to Rome for the winter, and they landed at Carthage about September 388. Saint Augustine went to Thagaste, to his own house and fields. For almost three years he lived there with friends who also served God, fasting and praying and thinking day and night about God’s law. Then he gave the property up.⟦Butler, Lives, vol. 8, pp. 446–447; Possidius, Life of Augustine 3.⟧

He lost his son in those years. Adeodatus died at about eighteen, already a young man of great gifts. Father and son had written a book together, The Teacher, a talk between them, and every thought in the boy’s part was the boy’s own, from when he was sixteen. “You took his life from the earth quickly,” Saint Augustine told God, “and I remember him with less worry. I fear nothing for his boyhood or his youth, nothing for him at all.”⟦Butler, Lives, vol. 8, p. 412, note 8; Weiskotten, note to Possidius, Life 2; Augustine, Confessions IX.6.14 (our translation).⟧

One day word came from Hippo Regius, a port about fifty miles away. A government agent there, a good Christian who feared God, wanted to hear Saint Augustine speak, and he said he was ready to give up the world. Saint Augustine went to Hippo at once to help him. The man promised day after day to keep his vow, and he did not do it while Saint Augustine was there. Saint Augustine was careful about one thing in those years. He feared being made a bishop, so he stayed away from any church that had no bishop. Hippo had an old bishop named Valerius, so Saint Augustine stood in the church among the people without a care.⟦Possidius, Life of Augustine 3–4; Weiskotten, note 1 to ch. 1; Catholic Encyclopedia, “St. Augustine of Hippo.”⟧

Saint Possidius would later live with Saint Augustine in his monastery and stay his friend for almost forty years. Saint Augustine told him what happened next. Valerius spoke to his people, told them the church needed another priest, and urged them to find and ordain one.⟦Possidius, Life of Augustine 4, 31; Weiskotten, introduction.⟧

“The Catholics already knew the aim and teaching of the holy Augustine, and they laid hands on him, for he stood among the people, unconcerned and unaware of what would happen. … So they held him and, as is usual in such cases, brought him to the bishop to be ordained. Everyone asked for this with one voice and one wish, and demanded it with great eagerness and shouting, while he wept freely. Some, as he himself told us, took his tears as pride and tried to console him, saying that the office of priest, though he deserved a greater one, stood close to a bishop’s. But the man of God, as he told us, understood with deeper thought and groaned at how many great dangers to his life he expected from governing and guiding the church; and so he wept.”⟦Possidius, Life of Augustine 4 (our translation).⟧

— Saint Possidius, Life of Augustine, chapter 4

Soon afterward he wrote to Valerius, and the letter survives. He said that God had put him in the second place at the helm when he did not yet know how to handle an oar. He took it as God’s rebuke. Once he had dared to find fault with many sailors before he knew their work. Now that he shared it, he felt how rash he had been. “The Lord laughed at me,” he wrote, “and chose to show me by experience what I am.” Then he pleaded with the old bishop. “Do you want me to perish, father Valerius? Where is your love?” He asked for a short time to make himself ready “by praying, reading, and weeping.” He hoped to have until Easter.⟦Augustine, Letter 21.1–4 (our translation from the public-domain English).⟧

Valerius gave him more than time. Saint Augustine set up a monastery within the church, and the men who joined him owned nothing of their own. Everything was held in common and given to each as he had need. Saint Alypius lived there with him for some years, until in 394 he was made bishop of their home town, Thagaste. And the old bishop did something against the custom of the African churches: he let his new priest preach in his presence. Valerius was Greek by birth and struggled with Latin. Some bishops complained, and he let Saint Augustine preach anyway.⟦Possidius, Life of Augustine 5; Weiskotten, introduction and note 1 to ch. 1.⟧

On 28 August 392 Saint Augustine met Fortunatus, a priest of the Manichees who had known him at Carthage, in a public debate at the Baths of Sossius. Fortunatus had been afraid to come, and his own people had shamed him into it. After two days he lost and left Hippo. His name spread, and Valerius began to fear that some other city would carry Saint Augustine off to be its bishop.⟦Possidius, Life of Augustine 6, 8; Weiskotten, note to ch. 6.⟧

Saint Augustine Becomes Bishop Against His Will

So Valerius hid him for a while where no one could find him. Then, old and very weak, he wrote in secret to the chief bishop at Carthage and asked that Saint Augustine be made bishop of Hippo alongside him. The answer was yes. Megalius, the senior bishop of Numidia, came to visit Hippo, and Valerius told everyone his wish. The clergy and the people rejoiced and shouted for it. For the second time a crowd was calling for him, and this time Saint Augustine refused. His own bishop was still alive, and that went against the custom of the Church.⟦Possidius, Life of Augustine 8.⟧

They told him it was often done and gave him examples from Africa and across the sea. At last, pressed and forced, he gave way, about 395 or 396. Afterward he learned of a council’s rule against it. He said and wrote that it should not have been done to him, and he did not want it done to anyone else. He worked to have the bishops’ councils rule that new bishops must be told the Church’s rules before they were ordained. Valerius died in 396, and Saint Augustine was bishop of Hippo alone.⟦Possidius, Life of Augustine 8; Weiskotten, note 1 to ch. 4.⟧

In those first years as bishop he wrote the book about his life. He told God about the pear tree, the lie on the shore, the woman sent away, and the voice in the garden, and he spoke to God as “you.” In it he named the beauty he had been running from and toward all along.⟦Benedict XVI, General Audience, 20 February 2008; Augustine, Confessions, Books II, V, VI, VIII.⟧

“Late have I loved you, beauty so ancient and so new, late have I loved you! You were within, and I was outside, and there I looked for you. … You were with me, and I was not with you. You called, you shouted, and you broke through my deafness. You flashed and shone, and you drove away my blindness. You touched me, and I burned for your peace.”⟦Augustine, Confessions X.27.38 (our translation).⟧

— Saint Augustine, Confessions, Book X

In Milan, Saint Ambrose died in 397. Saint Augustine heard how the bishop who baptized him had faced death, and he praised it warmly. The leading members of his church stood weeping around the sickbed and begged their bishop to ask the Lord for more time. “I have not lived so that I would be ashamed to live among you,” Saint Ambrose answered, “and I do not fear to die, because we have a good Lord.” As an old man, Saint Augustine still admired those words. He said that the first half was about what people could see. The second showed where Saint Ambrose put his trust: in the good Lord, to whom he prayed each day, “Forgive us our debts.”⟦Weiskotten, note to Possidius, Life 1 (Ambrose bishop 374–397); Possidius, Life of Augustine 27 (our translation).⟧

As bishop he went on living in common, now with his clergy. He never held the key to the church’s money or wore its seal ring, and the accounts were read to him once a year. A leading man of Hippo once asked for the deed to property he had given the church. He sent a hundred gold coins for the poor to soften the request, and Saint Augustine groaned. The gift had been the man’s own idea. Saint Augustine sent the deed back at once, refused the coins, and wrote to rebuke the man.⟦Possidius, Life of Augustine 24.⟧

His table was plain. There were herbs and lentils and always wine, and sometimes meat for guests or for the weaker brothers. The spoons were silver, but the dishes were clay or wood or marble, by his choice and not from need. No one was allowed to swear, even at table, and each man’s cups of wine were counted out ahead of time; whoever swore lost one. Saint Augustine liked reading and talk at meals more than eating and drinking. Two lines of verse were written on the table. “Whoever likes to gnaw at the life of an absent person with his words should know that this table is not meant for him.”⟦Possidius, Life of Augustine 22, 25 (our translation).⟧

One day some of his closest friends among the bishops sat at that table, forgot the lines, and began to talk about someone who was not there. Saint Augustine lost his temper. He told them sharply that either those verses must be wiped off the table, or he would get up in the middle of the meal and go to his room. “I and the others who were at that table experienced this,” Saint Possidius wrote.⟦Possidius, Life of Augustine 22 (our translation).⟧

Saint Augustine Defends the Church in Africa

Africa had a rival church with bishops of its own, the Donatists. Some of its people formed armed bands, the Circumcellions, and several times they lay in wait on the roads for Saint Augustine. He traveled often, whenever he was asked, to visit and teach and encourage Catholic congregations. Once the armed men were waiting for him in full force. His guide went wrong at a fork in the road, and the bishop and his companions reached their destination by a roundabout way. Only later did they learn what they had escaped.⟦Possidius, Life of Augustine 12; Augustine, Enchiridion 17, quoted in Weiskotten, note 3 to ch. 12 (our translation).⟧

Saint Augustine told the story himself as a lesson about going astray. “Going wrong has sometimes helped people,” he wrote, “but on a road for the feet, not on the road of life.” He and his companions were glad they had lost their way, and together they gave thanks to God, who had saved them. His friends were not always so fortunate. Saint Possidius was by then bishop of Calama, and he fell into their hands on another road. They seized him and his companions, took their animals and baggage, and beat him badly.⟦Augustine, Enchiridion 17, quoted in Weiskotten, note 3 to ch. 12 (our translation); Possidius, Life of Augustine 12; Weiskotten, note 5 to ch. 12.⟧

Crispinus, the Donatist bishop of Calama, was later judged a heretic in court and fined in gold. Saint Augustine then asked the judge not to collect the fine, and the judge agreed. Crispinus appealed to the emperor anyway, and the emperor’s answer put the full force of the laws against heretics on every Donatist. Saint Augustine and the other Catholic bishops worked to have the emperor let the penalty go, and with the Lord’s help it was done.⟦Possidius, Life of Augustine 12.⟧

He knew his old sect from the inside, and he kept preaching against it. One day he lost his thread in a sermon and ended by attacking the Manichees. At dinner he asked his brothers if they had noticed. “I think the Lord may have wanted to teach and heal someone who was lost among the people, through my forgetting and my mistake,” he said. A day or two later a merchant named Firmus came to the monastery, fell at his feet in tears, and asked them all to pray for his sins. He had been a Manichee for many years. That sermon had set him right and made him a Catholic. They all remembered how the sermon had gone, and together they thanked and blessed God.⟦Possidius, Life of Augustine 15 (our translation).⟧

Then came a new error, taught by the Pelagians. For almost ten years he worked against it, until popes and an emperor condemned it. To Saint Possidius he seemed in those years a limb of Christ, always anxious and watchful for the whole Church.⟦Possidius, Life of Augustine 18.⟧

In 410 the Goths under Alaric took Rome and plundered it. Pagans blamed the Christians for the disaster. In 413 Saint Augustine began his answer, the City of God, and he finished it in 426. In it he explained the story of the world by two loves. “So two loves made two cities: the earthly city by love of self, even to contempt of God; the heavenly city by love of God, even to contempt of self.”⟦Butler, Lives, vol. 8, p. 478; Augustine, City of God XIV.28 (our translation).⟧

Near the end of his life he went back through all his books, from the first ones he wrote after his conversion. Wherever he found something that did not agree with the Church’s teaching, he said so and corrected it, and he published the corrections. He also gathered the commands of Scripture into one book, so that anyone could read it and see how far he obeyed God. He called it The Mirror.⟦Possidius, Life of Augustine 28.⟧

Then the ships came. An army of Vandals and Alans, with some Goths among them, crossed from Spain and poured into Africa.⟦Possidius, Life of Augustine 28; Weiskotten, note to ch. 28 (428).⟧

Saint Augustine Stays With His People Under Siege

The invaders spared neither men nor women, young nor old. Saint Augustine watched cities fall and churches burn. Monks and nuns were scattered, and some people were tortured or killed while others were dragged off as slaves. The hymns of God fell silent in the churches. Bishops who escaped were stripped of everything and left to beg. Of countless churches, only three still stood: Carthage, Hippo, and Cirta. Saint Augustine did not see these things as other men did. He saw souls in danger. Saint Possidius wrote that more than ever, “tears were his bread day and night.” They were the bitterest days of his old age. He took comfort in the words of a wise man: “He will not be great who thinks it a great thing that wood and stones fall and mortals die.”⟦Possidius, Life of Augustine 28 (our translation).⟧

A bishop named Honoratus wrote to ask whether bishops and clergy should run from their churches before the enemy came. Saint Augustine’s answer was a long letter. He wrote that when the danger is the same for everyone, the people must not be left by those they need. Either all should go to safety together, or the clergy must stay with their people. Then they could “either live together or suffer together whatever the Father of the house wants them to bear.” He reminded Honoratus what happens in a church when there is no escape. Men and women of every age crowd in, some crying out for baptism, some for pardon, all for comfort and the sacraments. If no priest is there, how great a loss follows.⟦Possidius, Life of Augustine 30, quoting Augustine, Letter 228.2, 228.8 (our translation).⟧

Then the Vandals came to Hippo. Count Boniface held the city with an army of Goths, and the enemy shut it in for almost fourteen months, cutting it off even from the sea. Saint Possidius and other bishops from the country around had taken refuge inside the walls, and they stayed through the whole siege. They talked together often about the fearful judgments of God unfolding before their eyes. “You are just, Lord, and your judgment is right,” they said. With groans and tears they prayed together to the Father of mercies to hold them up in their trouble.⟦Possidius, Life of Augustine 28 (our translation).⟧

One day they were sitting at table with him, talking, and he told them what he was asking of God.⟦Possidius, Life of Augustine 29.⟧

“I want you to know what I ask of God in this time of our trouble. Either that he free this city that the enemy has surrounded; or, if he sees it differently, that he make his servants strong to bear his will; or at least that he take me from this world to himself.”⟦Possidius, Life of Augustine 29 (our translation).⟧

— Saint Augustine to the bishops at his table, Hippo, 430

He taught them that prayer, and they all prayed it with him, for themselves, for all their own, and for everyone in the city. In the third month of the siege he took to his bed with fever. It was his last illness.⟦Possidius, Life of Augustine 29 (our translation).⟧

He had often repeated the words of a fellow bishop, a close friend, near that man’s death. Saint Augustine had visited him more than once, and the friend showed with a movement of his hand that he was going. Saint Augustine told him he could still be of use to the Church if he lived. The friend did not want anyone to think he clung to this life. “If never, well and good,” he answered. “If some day, why not now?” Saint Augustine admired that saying from a man who feared God, born and raised in the country, with little learning. Under a fig tree in Milan he had once cried the same words.⟦Possidius, Life of Augustine 27 (our translation).⟧

Even as he lay sick, a man came to him. He brought a sick relative and asked him to lay his hand on him so that he would be healed. Saint Augustine answered that if he had any power in such things, he would surely have used it on himself first. The man said he had been told in a dream: “Go to the bishop Augustine, so that he may lay his hand on him, and he will be well.” When Saint Augustine heard this, he did it without delay. God healed the sick man at once, and he went away well.⟦Possidius, Life of Augustine 29 (our translation).⟧

Soon he would ask for one more thing: to be left alone with God.⟦Possidius, Life of Augustine 31.⟧

Saint Augustine Dies in Hippo

He had often told his friends that even good Christians and priests, after baptism, should not leave this life without fitting repentance. Now he did it himself. He had the shortest psalms of repentance copied out on sheets and set against the wall, where he could see them from his bed. Lying there through the days of his sickness, he looked at them and read them, and he wept freely and without stopping.⟦Possidius, Life of Augustine 31 (our translation).⟧

About ten days before the end he made one request, so that nothing would interrupt his prayer. No one was to come in to him except at the hours when the doctors came to examine him or when food was brought. His friends did as he asked, and all that time was his to pray. He had preached the word of God in the church right up to this last illness, strongly and gladly, with a clear mind and sound judgment.⟦Possidius, Life of Augustine 31 (our translation).⟧

Outside, the enemy still surrounded the city. Inside, the student who had once longed for a wisdom that would never die lay alone with the psalms on the wall and with God. At the end, Saint Possidius and the others stood by him. It was 28 August 430, and he was seventy-six years old.⟦Possidius, Life of Augustine 29, 31; Weiskotten, introduction, note 1, citing Prosper, Chronicle.⟧

“His body was whole, and his sight and hearing were clear. While we stood by and watched and prayed, he slept with his fathers, as the Scripture says, well nourished in a good old age.”⟦Possidius, Life of Augustine 31 (our translation).⟧

— Saint Possidius, Life of Augustine, chapter 31

How Saint Augustine Lives On in the Church

In front of his friends the Holy Sacrifice was offered to God for the peaceful rest of his body, and he was buried. He left no will. Saint Possidius wrote that as a poor man of God, he had nothing to make one from. He had often given orders that the church’s library and all its books be carefully kept for those who came after. He left the Church a full body of clergy and monasteries of men and of women. In July 431 the Vandals gave up the siege. Saint Possidius wrote that the Lord did not cheat his servant of the reward of his prayer. In due time he received what he had asked with tears for himself and for the city. Later the people left Hippo, and the enemy burned it.⟦Possidius, Life of Augustine 28, 29, 31 (our translation); Weiskotten, note to ch. 28.⟧

He had been laid to rest in the church of Peace at Hippo. About the year 720 the Lombard king Liutprand paid a great price for his relics. He brought them to the church of San Pietro in Ciel d’Oro in Pavia, in northern Italy. In 1362 a great marble shrine was begun for him there. In 1580 Pope Gregory XIII forbade anyone to search for the remains or move them.⟦Weiskotten, note to ch. 28; Butler, Lives, vol. 8, pp. 504–505; Sciberras, “The Troubled Events of the Relics.”⟧

On the morning of Tuesday, 1 October 1695, bricklayers were at work on the altar in the crypt of that church. Behind the altar they lifted the paving stones and found a box of white marble about four feet long. There were letters on it, and the men read them as the name of Saint Augustine. They sent for the friar who kept the church, because the prior was away. Inside was a second marble box, carved, and inside that a box of silver, decorated with crosses in the old Lombard style. When they opened the silver box, they saw human bones wrapped in cloth. A long inquiry followed, and miracles worked through the relics helped it along. In 1728 the bishop of Pavia declared the relics genuine, and on 22 September Pope Benedict XIII confirmed his ruling. They rest in Pavia today and are shown to the people twice a year, on 24 April for his conversion and on 28 August. In 1842 a piece of his right arm went back to Africa, to the basilica at Annaba in Algeria.⟦Sciberras, “The Troubled Events of the Relics”; Butler, Lives, vol. 8, p. 505.⟧

His way of life lived on too. In 423 he wrote to the nuns of a monastery in Hippo, which his own sister had once led, after a quarrel over who should lead it next. He praised their choice to live together under one roof “with one soul and one heart in God,” and he set down rules for them. Those rules became the Rule of Saint Augustine. The Hermits of Saint Augustine, united as one order in 1256, followed it, and so did the Canons Regular, priests who lived together under a rule.⟦Augustine, Letter 211.2 (our translation from the public-domain English); Butler, Lives, vol. 8, pp. 449–450; Catholic Encyclopedia, “Hermits of St. Augustine.”⟧

His teaching on grace shaped the Church long after him. In 529 the Second Council of Orange condemned a softer form of the error he had fought. Its chapters drew almost entirely on his works and those of his follower Saint Prosper. In 1298 Pope Boniface VIII ordered the whole Church to keep the feasts of four great Latin teachers with special honor. They were Saint Ambrose, Saint Jerome, Saint Gregory, and Saint Augustine. So the man who once sat silent in Saint Ambrose’s room came to share his title.⟦Butler, Lives, vol. 8, p. 497; Catholic Encyclopedia, “Councils of Orange”; Catholic Encyclopedia, “Doctors of the Church.”⟧

No formal canonization is recorded. The people honored him as a saint long before the Church had rules for it, and his feast on 28 August stands in calendars as old as the sixth century. Saint Caesarius of Arles kept it with great solemnity. The Orthodox Church keeps his memory on 15 June and sings of him as “an expounder of the City of God.”⟦Butler, Lives, vol. 8, p. 505; “Augustine of Hippo,” Wikipedia; Sanidopoulos, “Synaxarion of Saint Augustine of Hippo.”⟧

Saint Possidius ended his Life by asking his readers to pray for him. By God’s grace he had lived with this man as a close and loving friend for almost forty years, without one bitter quarrel. He hoped to imitate him in this world and to enjoy God’s promises with him in the next. He wrote that in the books Saint Augustine left behind, “the faithful may always find him living.” Anyone who opens the Confessions today finds him on the first page, still praying the prayer of his whole life: “You made us toward yourself, and our heart is restless until it rests in you.”⟦Possidius, Life of Augustine 31 (our translation); Augustine, Confessions I.1.1 (our translation).⟧

A Note on the Sources

Two witnesses carry this life, and they cover different halves. Saint Augustine’s Confessions tells the years up to his mother’s death. He says that he gives the sense of what he and his mother said at Ostia, and not their exact words. Saint Possidius lived with him for almost forty years, and he tells the bishop’s years. He does not name Saint Monica or tell the story of the garden. He says both parents were Christian, but this life follows Saint Augustine, who says his father was baptized only at the end. The Confessions gives no year for the garden; 386 comes from Butler and the Catholic Encyclopedia, which differ on his age. Saint Augustine’s own account of his baptism names neither Saint Ambrose nor Easter; those details come from Saint Possidius and later writers. The age of Adeodatus at his death comes from Butler; no source we read gives the place or year. Saint Possidius does not give the day of Saint Augustine’s death; 28 August comes from the chronicle of Saint Prosper. Saint Possidius does not name himself as the bishop beaten on the road; his editor Weiskotten identifies him. Butler says the marble box found at Pavia had the name engraved on it, while the Augustinian Order’s account says the letters were in charcoal. Josef Sciberras writes that no reliable document tells how the relics reached Pavia from Africa, and that scholars kept arguing after the ruling of 1728.

How This Life Was Researched

Three research passes covered about twenty sources in Latin and English, and the writer read the main ones again for this version. The quotations from the Confessions were translated fresh from the Latin text of James J. O’Donnell. Those from Saint Possidius were translated from the Latin of Weiskotten’s 1919 edition, checked against his English. Letters 21 and 211 were put into plain English from public-domain translations. Butler’s Lives of the Saints, the Catholic Encyclopedia, and the Augustinian Order’s history of the relics supplied dates and the story after his death.
