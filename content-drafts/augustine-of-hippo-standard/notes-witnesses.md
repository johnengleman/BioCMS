# Augustine of Hippo: notes (focus: witnesses)

Researcher: witnesses agent. Slug: augustine-of-hippo. Tier A. Written 2026-10-01.
Scope: the life as told by people who knew him (Possidius) and by Augustine himself in the Confessions. Letters, sermons, and later cult belong to the other two agents.

## 1. Identity (short; the first agent owns the full block)

- Aurelius Augustinus. Born 13 Nov 354 at Tagaste in Numidia; died 28 Aug 430 at Hippo Regius, age 76. [S5 intro + p.n.1; S3 p.405; S1 ch.31]
- Tradition: shared (Catholic Doctor of the Church; Orthodox also honor him). Not checked here.
- Feast days: Catholic 28 Aug (the page head in Butler is "Aug. 28") [S3]. Orthodox date: other agents, no source read by me.
- Name forms in my sources: "Austin" (Butler), "Alipius" (Butler), "Monnica" (Pusey), "Alypius" (Weiskotten).

## 3. Timeline

Dates marked (S5) are the editor's. Dates with no source key were not found and are not stated.

- 13 Nov 354: Augustine is born at Tagaste, a small Numidian town about fifty miles southeast of Hippo and about a hundred and fifty miles southwest of Carthage. His birthday is certain because he names it himself: "Idibus Novemhris mihi natalis dies erat" (OCR spelling) (De Beata Vita I.6, cited in S5 p.n.1). [S5 intro, ch.1 n.1; S3 p.405]
- Childhood: Possidius says his parents were "honorable Christian parents of curial rank" and paid for a liberal education. [S1 ch.1] Butler says the father Patricius was a pagan of "hasty choleric disposition" who was baptized "a little before his death", and gives him a brother Navigius and a sister who died an abbess. [S3 p.405] Conflict: Possidius calls both parents Christian; the Confessions and Butler say Patricius was baptized late. Weiskotten's note says Patricius was "received into the Church and baptized just before his death" in 371. [S5 ch.2 n.]
- Boyhood illness: seriously ill at school, he asks for baptism, his mother prepares it, he recovers, and baptism is put off. [S3 p.406-407] NEW for many English pages: Butler's remark that Augustine himself later condemned the habit of delay.
- Age 16: the stolen pears. Pusey: "that sixteenth year of my age". I read his reflection only, not the narrative of the night: he gathered pears he did not want and, "when gathered, I flung them away". [S2 Bk II]
- 371: His father dies. [S5 ch.2 n.] Augustine, aged 19, reads Cicero's Hortensius at Carthage, "my father being dead two years before" (Pusey words), and it turns his desire to "an immortality of wisdom". [S2 Bk III]
- Carthage, from 374: he teaches rhetoric nine years. [S5 intro] Theatres and love of love: "To Carthage I came, where there sang all around me in my ears a cauldron of unholy loves." [S2 Bk III] He takes a concubine; their son is Adeodatus. [S3 (Butler: "He took a concubine", page not noted); S2 Bk VI, Bk IX]
- About 19 to 28: Manichaean hearer (not one of the elect). He meets the famous teacher Faustus and is disappointed that gorgeous rhetoric hides weak answers. [S5 intro + ch.1 n.5]
- Monica's dream (while he is a Manichee): she stands on "a certain wooden rule"; a shining youth tells her "where she was, there was I also". When Augustine says she may be the one to become like him, she answers at once that it was not told her that, "where he, there thou also", but "where thou, there he also". [S2 Bk III] NEW detail for most English pages: the quick answer.
- Monica asks a bishop to talk with her son. The bishop refuses, says Augustine is "yet unteachable", and then, stung by her tears: "it is not possible that the son of these tears should perish." (Pusey words.) The bishop himself had been given to the Manichees by his mother as a child and had copied out their books. [S2 Bk III] The bishop is not named in the text I read.
- About 383, flight to Rome: he lies to Monica that he is waiting for a friend's ship; she follows him to the shore and prays all night in an oratory of St. Cyprian while he sails; "The wind blew and swelled our sails." [S2 Bk V] Reason he gave himself: better pay and better-behaved students at Rome than the eversores (wreckers) of Carthage. [S2 Bk V; S5 intro] At Rome he falls gravely ill, and then finds that Roman students avoid paying fees. [S2 Bk V; S5 intro]
- Milan, 384-386: he is named to teach rhetoric. Ambrose is bishop. Augustine watches him read: "his eye glided over the pages, and his heart searched out the sense, but his voice and tongue were at rest." [S2 Bk VI] Possidius says Augustine at first came to hear Ambrose preach and "was intent, hanging and fixed" on his sermons, alert in case anything was said for or against Manichaeism; Ambrose solved the Law's objections one by one. [S1 ch.1] The Latin says "intendebat suspensus atque affixus". [S1 ch.1 Latin]
- Concubine dismissed for a planned marriage: "my concubine being torn from my side as a hindrance to my marriage, my heart which clave unto her was torn and wounded and bleeding. And she returned to Afric, vowing unto Thee never to know any other man, leaving with me my son by her." He takes another woman in the two-year wait. [S2 Bk VI] NEW as an emotional detail.
- "Give me chastity and continency, only not yet." [S2 Bk VIII]
- 386, the garden at Milan (the year 386 is given in the task; Pusey does not date it, and Butler's page for it I did not read): Ponticianus' story of Antony; Augustine to Alypius, "What ails us?"; he runs into the garden. He cries under a fig tree, hears a child's voice chant "Take up and read; Take up and read", opens the Apostle to Romans 13:13-14 ("put ye on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make not provision for the flesh"), and "all the darkness of doubt vanished away". Alypius reads the next verse, Romans 14:1, and joins him. They tell Monica; "she leaps for joy". [S2 Bk VIII] The voice is a child's; Augustine does not know if boy or girl. Possidius does not tell this scene at all. [S1 ch.1-2]
- Lent to Easter, 387: he gives up his chair of rhetoric (he "renounced" it at the end of the vintage holidays; S5 ch.2 n. cross-references Conf. IX.5). Weiskotten (intro): "baptized by Ambrose at Easter 387". Butler: he came to Milan "in the beginning of Lent in 387" to enter the competentes, and was baptized "by St. Ambrose on Easter-eve in 387, together with Alipius and his son Adeodatus, who was about fifteen years of age." [S3 p.445-446] Pusey: "we were baptised, and anxiety for our past life vanished from us." [S2 Bk IX] In the days after: "How did I weep, in Thy Hymns and Canticles, touched to the quick by the voices of Thy sweet-attuned Church!" [S2 Bk IX] Possidius: he "received the water of baptism" in the holy days of Easter, through Ambrose. [S1 ch.1]
- Toothache (before baptism; Butler says "about this time"; he does not name the place): pain so bad he could not speak; he wrote on a wax tablet asking his friends to pray; they knelt, and the pain ceased. [S3 p.445] Source for that story in the Confessions: Conf. IX (Butler gives the reference "(9) Conf. 1 9. c 4"). Status: recorded by Augustine, mentioned by Butler, not read by me in Pusey.
- Milan, 386 or 387: relics of Gervasius and Protasius found and miracles there; Augustine was a witness. [S3 p.446] (Butler.) Others will cover this.
- 387 (April to September at Rome, then Ostia): Butler says he went from Milan to Rome with Monica and friends and stayed "from the month of April to the September following". [S3 p.446] Monica died at Ostia "before the thirteenth of November in 387". [S3 p.446]
- Ostia, the window: "she and I stood alone, leaning in a certain window, which looked into the garden of the house where we now lay, at Ostia"; they talk of "what sort the eternal life of the saints was to be". [S2 Bk IX] Monica: "One thing there was for which I desired to linger for a while in this life, that I might see thee a Catholic Christian before I died." [S2 Bk IX]
- Five days later she falls ill. She faints, wakes, and asks "Where was I?" and says "Here shall you bury your mother." Her other son (Pusey says only "my brother"; Butler names a brother Navigius, S3 p.405) wishes she might die in her own land. She then says: "Lay this body any where; let not the care for that any way disquiet you: this only I request, that you would remember me at the Lord's altar, wherever you be." [S2 Bk IX]
- Monica dies on "the ninth day then of her sickness, and the fifty-sixth year of her age, and the three-and-thirtieth of mine". Augustine closes her eyes. Adeodatus cries out; Euodius takes up the Psalter and the household answers with the psalm "I will sing of mercy and judgments to Thee, O Lord". [S2 Bk IX] She told him in her last illness that she had never heard a harsh word from him. [S2 Bk IX]
- Return to Africa: Butler says Augustine went back to Rome for the winter and "landed at Carthage about September in 388." [S3 p.446-447] Possidius: he went to his own home and lands and "was settled for almost three years" with friends, in fasting, prayer, and meditation, then gave up the possessions. [S1 ch.3] Possidius does not mention Monica's death at all (Weiskotten notes the omission). [S5 ch.2 n.]
- At Carthage, Butler says a lawyer named Innocent was cured of a fistula at prayer, with Augustine present. [S3 p.447] Not read in the original.
- 391: Hippo Regius. An agens in rebus (imperial agent, a good Christian) asks to see him. Augustine goes to Hippo to win him; the man delays. Possidius: the Catholics seized him in church "for he was standing there among the people secure and unaware". He "wept freely". Some thought the tears showed wounded pride and said the priesthood was only a little below a bishop's; "as he told us", he wept because he foresaw many dangers. [S1 ch.3-4; ordination year 391 per S5] NEW: the account of the tears and the misreading of them.
- Monastery: soon after ordination Augustine sets up a monastery "within the church", with no private property, "all things should be held in common and be distributed to each one as he had need". Bishop Valerius, a Greek by birth who had trouble with Latin, lets him preach in his presence, against African custom. Some bishops complain. [S1 ch.5]
- 28 Aug 392: Public debate with Fortunatus, the Manichaean presbyter, at the Baths of Sossius in Hippo, over two days. Possidius: Fortunatus, who had known Augustine at Carthage "when he was still involved with himself in this same error", was afraid to meet him and gave way only when shamed by his followers. He lost and left Hippo. [S1 ch.6; date from S5 note on ch.6]. NEW: the date matches the day of his death 38 years later, which no source I read remarks on. Treat as a coincidence of dates, not as a sign.
- Ordination as bishop, about 395-396: Valerius, afraid another church would carry off Augustine, hid him, then wrote secretly to the Primate of Carthage. Possidius: Augustine "refused to accept the episcopate contrary to the custom of the Church, since his bishop was still living" and gave way "under compulsion and constraint". Later he said it should not have been done and worked for a council rule. [S1 ch.8] Date conflict: Weiskotten says Valerius died in 396, Augustine succeeded within "the year (396-397)", yet another note says Megalius "in 391 consecrated him as coadjutor". The two statements cannot both be exact. Treat the year as 395 or 396, "about", and the "391" in that note as an error. [S5 ch.8 n.]
- Donatist ambush: Circumcellions, armed, watched the roads for him. His guide took a wrong road, and the party arrived another way; "he learned later that through this error he had escaped their impious hands". [S1 ch.12] NEW for English readers.
- Crispinus of Calama: a Donatist bishop of Calama, fined for heresy, denied being a heretic and was judged one; Augustine begged the judge not to enforce the fine. Crispinus ungratefully appealed to the emperor and the laws against Donatists were enforced. Augustine then worked to have the emperor's penalty eased. [S1 ch.12] Context: his own bishop-colleague was beaten and robbed by Donatists on the road. [S1 ch.12]
- Firmus the merchant: after a sermon Augustine had left unfinished by a digression against the Manichees, a merchant named Firmus, a Manichee of many years who had paid much money to the Elect, fell at his feet in the monastery and wept. Augustine and his clergy asked what had moved him; "Wondering and marvelling at the profound plan of God", they gave thanks. Firmus left trade and later became a presbyter in another land. [S1 ch.15] NEW in popular English pages.
- Pelagians: Possidius says he "labored for almost ten years" against them; African councils persuaded Pope Innocent and then Zosimus; the Emperor Honorius condemned the sect. [S1 ch.18] Years are not given by Possidius in what I read.
- 410 and after: Butler says the sack of Rome by Alaric led Augustine to begin the City of God in 413 and finish it in 426. [S3 p.478]
- 428: Debate at Hippo with Maximinus, an Arian bishop who came with the Gothic count Sigisvult; the record survives. [S3 p.478]
- 428 or after: Vandals, Alans, and some Goths come "by ship from the regions of Spain" and overrun Africa; Weiskotten dates the invasion to 428. [S1 ch.28; S5] Possidius says Augustine did not think about the invasion as others did and "more than ever tears were his meat day and night". [S1 ch.28] Possidius also says he replied by letter to Honoratus of Thiabe on whether bishops should flee; the letter is in Possidius' chapter 30 (not read by me). [S1 ch.30]
- Siege: Count Boniface defends Hippo with Gothic allies; the city is shut in for "almost fourteen months", the sea-coast cut off; Possidius and other bishops took refuge inside and stayed through the whole siege. [S1 ch.28]
- Summer 430: Augustine says at table that he prays God either to free the city, or to make His servants brave, "or at least that He may take me from this world unto Himself". All the bishops join the prayer. "In the third month of the siege he succumbed to fever". [S1 ch.29]
- Last illness: he has the shortest penitential psalms copied and hung on the wall by his bed; he reads them and "wept freely and constantly". About ten days before death he asks that no one come in except at the doctors' hours or with food. [S1 ch.31] He preached up to the very last illness, "with a clear mind and sound judgment" and sound sight and hearing. [S1 ch.31]
- 28 Aug 430: death, "while we stood by and watched and prayed". Possidius gives the siege month and the age (76; "almost forty" years as priest or bishop) but not the day. The day is from Prosper's Chronicon, quoted in Weiskotten's note: "Prosper, in his Chronicon, states that Augustine died August 28, 430". [S1 ch.31; S5 intro n.1; S5 ch.28 n. on Boniface]. After a service he is buried. [S1 ch.31]
- Estate: no will, "because as a poor man of God he had nothing from which to make it"; he left the church library and books to be kept for the future, the church's goods with his presbyter, and relatives had been helped "that they might not be in want", not made rich. [S1 ch.31]
- After his death: Hippo was abandoned by its people and burned by the enemy. [S1 ch.28] The Vandals raised the siege in July 431 per Weiskotten's note, and took the city by treaty in 435. [S5 notes on ch.3 and ch.28]

## 4. Character (one line each)

- Plain table: herbs and lentils, meat sometimes "for the sake of his guests or for some of the weaker brethren", always wine; modest clothes, "neither too fine nor yet too mean". Possidius' Latin says the dishes were earthenware, wood, or marble, "not from lack of need, but by purpose of will" (my paraphrase of "non tamen necessitatis inopia, sed proposito voluntatis"). [S1 ch.22 Latin]
- Table rule: reading and talk were better than eating and drinking. He had two lines cut on the table against gossip, and when fellow bishops broke it he got "so angry" he said he would take the lines off the table or leave the meal. Possidius: "Which I and the others who were at that table, experienced." [S1 ch.22] NEW and vivid.
- Oaths: he forbade swearing, even at table; a man who swore lost one of his set number of cups of wine. [S1 ch.25]
- Money: never held the key or wore the ring of the church treasury; accounts were read to him once a year; he would not buy a house or land; he refused gifts that would rob the heirs. [S1 ch.24]
- Anecdote: a Hippo notable at Carthage gave his property to the church, keeping the income, and some years later asked for the deeds back and sent 100 solidi for the poor. Augustine groaned, returned the deed unasked, refused the money, and rebuked him. [S1 ch.24] NEW.
- He told the people he would rather live on gifts than manage church estates, and offered to give the properties back; the laity would never take them. [S1 ch.23]
- Judge: he heard cases till mealtime and sometimes fasted all day. He kept a saying that he liked to hear cases between strangers rather than friends, because one party could become a friend and the loser among friends would be lost. [S1 ch.19]
- Letters of recommendation: he refused his closest friends' requests for letters to officials, on a sage's rule, but wrote when he saw real need; a vicar named Macedonius answered with praise for his tact. [S1 ch.20]
- No women in his house, "not even his own sister", a widow who had led a community of women, and not his brother's daughters; he never saw a woman without clerics as witnesses. [S1 ch.26]
- Ambrose's habits he cited: never find a wife for another, never urge a man to war, never go to a banquet at home. [S1 ch.27]
- He came to tears often: at ordination, at the end with the penitential psalms, over the sack of cities. [S1 ch.4, 28, 31]
- Frank about his own past: he writes of sin in the Confessions without disguise (pears, concubine, lies to his mother). [S2]
- Admirers: Possidius calls him a limb of Christ "ever anxious and watchful" for the universal Church. [S1 ch.18] Critics: some African bishops objected to a presbyter preaching in front of a bishop. [S1 ch.5] The Donatists attacked him on the road. [S1 ch.12]
- Humility about error: he later wrote and said his own consecration while Valerius lived was wrong, and worked to change the rule. [S1 ch.8]

## 6. Scenes (at most 10)

1. The pears: a boy of sixteen steals fruit he does not want and throws it away. [S2 Bk II]
2. Monica at the shore of Carthage, watching a ship she cannot stop; she sleeps by St. Cyprian's oratory; he sails. [S2 Bk V]
3. Augustine waiting at Ambrose's open door and watching him read without moving his lips. [S2 Bk VI]
4. The garden: fig tree, tears, a child's chant, Alypius at his side, the book on the seat. [S2 Bk VIII]
5. Easter eve at Milan: Ambrose baptizes Augustine, Alypius, and fifteen-year-old Adeodatus. [S3 p.445-446]
6. The Ostia window: mother and son, a garden below, a conversation that "passes through all things bodily". [S2 Bk IX]
7. Hippo, 391: the crowd lays hands on him while he stands unaware among them, and he weeps. [S1 ch.4]
8. Augustine's table at Hippo: the two lines against gossip, a bishop breaks the rule, Augustine threatens to leave the meal. [S1 ch.22]
9. The wrong road: Circumcellions waiting, the guide loses his way, they arrive safe. [S1 ch.12]
10. The besieged city, summer 430: a dying bishop reads penitential psalms on sheets pinned to the wall and asks to be left alone for ten days. [S1 ch.31]

## 7. Miracles (witness-focus only; the church-miracles agent will give the full list)

- Hippo, Augustine as presbyter and bishop: demoniacs freed after he prayed with many tears. Reporter: Possidius, "I know also". Status: eyewitness testimony recorded in a Life. [S1 ch.29]
- Hippo, last illness (summer 430): a man brought a sick relative and asked Augustine to lay a hand on him; Augustine said he would first have healed himself if he had power; the visitor said he had had a dream "Go to the bishop Augustine that he may lay his hand upon him, and he shall be whole"; Augustine did so and the sick man left healed. Reporter: Possidius. Status: eyewitness testimony in a Life. [S1 ch.29] NEW in most English pages.
- Before baptism, 386-387: toothache stopped at once when friends prayed. Reporter: Augustine (via Butler). Status: self-report. [S3 p.445]
- Carthage, 388: lawyer Innocent cured of a fistula before surgery. Reporter: Augustine (via Butler). Status: self-report. [S3 p.447]
- Milan: Gervasius and Protasius relics; miracles Augustine says he saw. [S3 p.446] Others will treat it.
- Late and legendary: the child at the shore with a spoon or shell, telling him he will empty the sea into a hole before Augustine grasps the Trinity. NOT in Possidius, the Confessions, or the Golden Legend text Caxton used. In Caxton's 1483 translation of the Golden Legend, Caxton says "I find it not in the legend, mine exemplar, neither in English, French, ne in Latin" (as quoted in S4) and saw it on an altar at the Blackfriars in Antwerp. Earliest art mentioned: Benozzo Gozzoli, San Gimignano (15th c.). Status: popular legend, late. [S4] Do not tell it as history; if used, say it is a medieval tale.
