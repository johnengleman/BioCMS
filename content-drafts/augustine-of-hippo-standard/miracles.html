The Church remembers Saint Augustine of Hippo as a bishop who saw God work wonders and wrote them down so that his people would not forget them. This list gathers those wonders as the oldest witnesses told them. Most come from two men. One is Saint Augustine himself. The other is his friend Saint Possidius. He lived with Saint Augustine for about forty years and became bishop of Calama. Each account names who told it and where.

Saint Augustine wrote about these wonders in the last book of the City of God. He said that miracles are still worked in the name of Christ, through his sacraments and through the prayers and relics of his saints. The same God who worked the miracles we read of in Scripture still works them today, “through whom he wills and as he wills.” When Saint Augustine saw signs of God’s power in his own day, he had the people who were healed write down what had happened. Their accounts were read aloud in church.

The people honored him as a saint long before the Church had rules for canonization. Canonization is the Church’s formal act that declares someone a saint. His feast was kept by the sixth century.

During Saint Augustine’s life

Demons flee when Saint Augustine prays, Hippo

Saint Possidius writes that he knew of these cases himself. People asked Saint Augustine to pray for men tormented by demons, both when he was a priest and when he was a bishop. He begged God for them with many tears, and the demons left the men. Saint Possidius gives no names or dates.

Status: told by Saint Possidius in his Life of Augustine, chapter 29, from his own knowledge. He lived with Saint Augustine for about forty years.

Source: Possidius, Life of Augustine, chapter 29, translated by H. T. Weiskotten (1919), https://tertullian.org/fathers/possidius_life_of_augustine_02_text.htm

A sick man is healed at Saint Augustine’s bedside

Saint Possidius tells this from the last illness. A man came to Saint Augustine’s bed with a sick relative and asked him to lay his hand on the sick man so that he would be healed. Saint Augustine answered that if he had any power in such things he would have used it on himself first. The man replied that he had seen a vision. In his dream he had been told: “Go to the bishop Augustine, so that he may lay his hand on him, and he will be well.” Saint Augustine did not delay. He laid his hand on the sick man. God healed him at once, and the man went away well.

Status: told by Saint Possidius in his Life of Augustine, chapter 29. He was with Saint Augustine through the last illness.

Source: Possidius, Life of Augustine, chapter 29, translated by H. T. Weiskotten (1919), https://tertullian.org/fathers/possidius_life_of_augustine_02_text.htm; the story is retold in Alban Butler, Lives of the Saints, vol. 8, “St. Augustine, Bishop, C. and Doctor,” 28 August (public domain).

God saves Saint Augustine from an ambush

Saint Possidius tells of armed men called Circumcellions who watched the roads for Saint Augustine. Once they were out in full force, and his guide took a wrong road. So Saint Augustine and his companions came to their destination by another way. Later he learned that the mistake had saved him from their hands, and together with all his companions he gave thanks to God, the Deliverer. Saint Possidius writes that the guide’s error was the providence of God.

Status: told by Saint Possidius in his Life of Augustine, chapter 12, as the providence of God.

Source: Possidius, Life of Augustine, chapter 12, translated by H. T. Weiskotten (1919), https://tertullian.org/fathers/possidius_life_of_augustine_02_text.htm

God converts a merchant through an unfinished sermon, Hippo

Saint Possidius was at the table when Saint Augustine spoke of a sermon he had not finished. He had lost his thread and ended by attacking the Manichees, a religious sect. He said that perhaps the Lord wanted to teach and heal some wanderer among the people through his forgetting and his mistake. We and all our words are in his hands, he said. A day or two later a merchant named Firmus came to the monastery. He fell on his knees at Saint Augustine’s feet in tears and asked the priest and his companions to pray to the Lord for his sins. He had followed the Manichees for many years and paid them much money. Now God in his mercy had made him a Catholic through Saint Augustine’s sermons. They asked what in the sermon had satisfied him, and they all remembered how the sermon had gone. Then they glorified and blessed God’s holy name. Firmus gave up his business and lived as a servant of God. Later he was called to be a priest in another region.

Status: told by Saint Possidius in his Life of Augustine, chapter 15. He was at the table, and he tells it as the plan of God for the salvation of souls.

Source: Possidius, Life of Augustine, chapter 15, translated by H. T. Weiskotten (1919), https://tertullian.org/fathers/possidius_life_of_augustine_02_text.htm

A toothache stops when friends pray, 386–387

Saint Augustine tells this himself in the Confessions, from the months before his baptism. He wrote that God tried him with a toothache so bad that he could not speak. It came into his heart to ask all the friends with him to pray for him to “the God of all health.” He wrote the request on a wax tablet and gave it to them to read. As soon as they knelt in humble prayer, the pain went away. He had never felt anything like it, and he was afraid. He wrote that God’s power was shown to him deep within, and that he rejoiced in faith and praised God’s name.

Status: told by Saint Augustine himself in the Confessions, Book IX, chapter 4. Alban Butler retells it.

Source: Augustine, Confessions, Book IX, chapter 4, translated by E. B. Pusey (1838), https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/3296/pg3296.txt; Alban Butler, Lives of the Saints, vol. 8, “St. Augustine, Bishop, C. and Doctor,” 28 August (public domain).

Saint Monica dreams her son stands beside her

Saint Augustine tells in the Confessions of a dream of his mother Saint Monica. He was then a Manichee, a member of a religious sect. She wept for him daily. She dreamed that she stood on a wooden rule and wept, and a bright young man asked her why she grieved. He told her to look: where she was, her son was also. She looked and saw him standing beside her on the rule. Saint Augustine tried to turn the dream around. He said it meant that she should not give up hope of becoming what he was. She answered at once: “It was not said to me, ‘Where he is, there you are,’ but ‘Where you are, there he is.’” He wrote later that God had not despised her tears, and that the dream was God’s comfort to her.

Status: told by Saint Augustine in the Confessions, Book III, chapters 11 and 12. He held the dream to be God’s answer to his mother’s tears.

Source: Augustine, Confessions, Book III, chapters 11 and 12, translated by E. B. Pusey (1838), https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/3296/pg3296.txt

A voice says “Take up and read,” Milan, 386

Saint Augustine tells how he was weeping under a fig tree in a garden in Milan in 386. He heard a voice from the next house chanting again and again: “Take up and read, take up and read.” He could not tell if it was a boy or a girl. Saint Augustine took the voice as a command from God to open the book and read the first passage he found. So he took up the letters of Saint Paul and read Romans 13:13–14. He wrote that a light of assurance seemed to pour into his heart, and all the darkness of doubt left.

Status: told by Saint Augustine in the Confessions, Book VIII, chapter 12. He took the voice as God’s command.

Source: Augustine, Confessions, Book VIII, chapter 12, translated by E. B. Pusey (1838), https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/3296/pg3296.txt

Hippo holds out in the Vandal siege, 430–431

Saint Possidius was inside Hippo through the whole siege, and he records what Saint Augustine asked of God at table. He asked God for one of three things: to free the city, to make his servants strong to bear his will, or to take him from this world to himself. In the third month of the siege Saint Augustine fell ill with fever, and he died on 28 August 430. Saint Possidius wrote that the Lord did not deny his servant the reward of his prayer. He received in due time what he asked with tears for himself and for the city. The siege lasted almost fourteen months and ended in July 431. Alban Butler wrote that the people credited this to Saint Augustine’s prayer. Afterward the people left Hippo, and the Vandals entered the empty city and burned part of it.

Status: told by Saint Possidius in his Life of Augustine, chapters 28 and 29, and by Alban Butler, who says the people ascribed it to Saint Augustine’s prayer.

Source: Alban Butler, Lives of the Saints, vol. 8, “St. Augustine, Bishop, C. and Doctor,” 28 August (public domain); Possidius, Life of Augustine, chapters 28 and 29, translated by H. T. Weiskotten (1919), https://tertullian.org/fathers/possidius_life_of_augustine_02_text.htm

Wonders Saint Augustine witnessed and recorded

In the last book of the City of God, Saint Augustine tells of wonders he knew of. Many were healings at the relics of Saint Stephen, the first martyr. Those relics had come to Hippo less than two years before he wrote. He had the people who were healed write accounts, and the accounts were read to the people in church so that all would know what God had done. He wrote that the published accounts already came to almost seventy, and that at Calama there were far more. Saint Augustine gives the glory to God, who works through the martyrs, the sacraments, and prayer. He was the witness and the recorder of these wonders.

A blind man sees at martyrs’ relics, Milan, 386

Saint Augustine was in Milan in 386 when a blind man was restored to sight at the relics of the martyrs Saint Gervasius and Saint Protasius. Their bodies had lain hidden and unknown for a long time. They were made known to the bishop Saint Ambrose in a dream, and he found them. The emperor was in the city, and a great crowd had gathered at the bodies of the martyrs. Saint Augustine wrote that by those holy relics the darkness of the blind man was scattered, and he saw the light of day.

Status: told by Saint Augustine in the City of God, Book XXII, chapter 8. He was in Milan at the time.

Source: Augustine, City of God, Book XXII, chapter 8, translated by Marcus Dods, https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/120122.htm

Innocentius is healed before surgery, Carthage, about 388

Saint Augustine says he saw this at Carthage with his own eyes. Innocentius was a former lawyer, and he and his whole household were devout. He had fistulas and had already had one painful operation, and the surgeons said a second was needed. The bishop Saint Aurelius, Saturninus of Uzalis, and other servants of God visited him every day. On the evening before the operation he begged them with tears to be there in the morning, for he was sure he would die under the knife. They comforted him and told him to trust in God. Then they knelt to pray. Innocentius threw himself to the ground as if someone had hurled him there, and he prayed with sobs and a flood of tears that shook his whole body. Saint Augustine wrote that he himself could not pray at all. He said only this in his heart: “Lord, what prayers of your people do you hear, if you do not hear these?” In the morning the surgeon came with his knife in his hand and searched for the place with his eyes and his fingers. He found a perfectly firm scar. Saint Augustine wrote that no words could describe the joy and praise and thanks to the merciful and almighty God that poured from every mouth, with tears of gladness.

Status: told by Saint Augustine in the City of God, Book XXII, chapter 8. He was in the room, and he says the bishop Saint Aurelius remembered it with him.

Source: Augustine, City of God, Book XXII, chapter 8, translated by Marcus Dods, https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/120122.htm

Innocentia is cured of breast cancer, Carthage

Saint Augustine tells of Innocentia, a very devout noblewoman of Carthage. She had cancer of the breast, and her doctor advised her to use no remedy. So she turned to God alone in prayer. As Easter came near, she was told in a dream what to do. She was to wait for the first woman to come out of the baptistery after her baptism and ask her to make the sign of Christ on the sore. Innocentia did this and was cured at once. Her doctor asked what remedy she had used, and when she told him he answered with contempt. She replied: “What is so great about Christ healing a cancer? He raised a man who had been dead four days.” Saint Augustine was troubled that so great a miracle was not known in the city. He had her tell the whole story from beginning to end, and the women who listened were amazed and glorified God.

Status: told by Saint Augustine in the City of God, Book XXII, chapter 8. He heard the story from Innocentia herself.

Source: Augustine, City of God, Book XXII, chapter 8, translated by Marcus Dods, https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/120122.htm

A doctor is freed from gout at baptism, Carthage

Saint Augustine records that a doctor of Carthage was freed from gout at his baptism. The man had given in his name for baptism. The night before his baptism, boys he understood to be devils appeared to him in a dream and forbade him to be baptized that year. They trod on his feet and gave him the worst pain he had ever felt. But he refused to obey them and would not put off the washing of new birth. In the baptism itself he was freed both of the pain and of the disease. He lived a long time afterward and never had gout again.

Status: told by Saint Augustine in the City of God, Book XXII, chapter 8. He says that he and a few brothers nearby know of it.

A comedian is cured at his baptism, Curubis

Saint Augustine writes that a comedian of Curubis was cured of paralysis and of a hernia when he was baptized. He came up from the font of new birth as if nothing had ever been wrong with his body. Saint Augustine had already heard of it from people whose word he could not doubt. Then the man was brought to Carthage at the order of the bishop Saint Aurelius.

Status: told by Saint Augustine in the City of God, Book XXII, chapter 8.

A farm at Zubedi is freed from evil spirits

Saint Augustine writes that evil spirits troubled the family, the cattle, and the servants of Hesperius on his farm at Zubedi. Hesperius asked that one of the priests of Hippo come and drive the spirits out by his prayers. A priest went and offered there the sacrifice of the body of Christ, the Holy Eucharist. He prayed with all his might that the trouble would stop, and it stopped at once, through God’s mercy. Hesperius also had some holy earth from Jerusalem, where Christ was buried and rose on the third day. He had hung it in his bedroom to keep himself from harm.

Status: told by Saint Augustine in the City of God, Book XXII, chapter 8. Hesperius was his neighbor.

A paralysed young man walks away cured, near Zubedi

When his house was freed, Hesperius did not want to keep the holy earth from Jerusalem in his bedroom any longer, out of reverence for it. Saint Augustine and Bishop Maximinus of Sinita were nearby, and Hesperius asked them to visit. He begged them to bury the earth and make the spot a place of prayer where Christians could gather to worship God. They did as he asked. A young countryman of that neighborhood was paralysed. When he heard of this, he begged his parents to carry him to that holy place at once. He prayed there and went away on his own feet, perfectly cured.

Status: told by Saint Augustine in the City of God, Book XXII, chapter 8. He was present when the earth was buried.

Source: Augustine, City of God, Book XXII, chapter 8, translated by Marcus Dods, https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/120122.htm

A young man and his eye are healed, Victoriana

Saint Augustine writes that a devil seized a young man while he watered his horse at noon on a summer day in a river pool. The young man was carried to the shrine of the martyrs Saint Protasius and Saint Gervasius at Victoriana, a country estate less than thirty miles from Hippo. He lay there near death. As was her custom, the lady of the house came in with her maids and some nuns for the evening hymns and prayers. They began to sing. At the sound the young man screamed and seized the altar and held on as if he were tied to it. The devil begged to be spared and said when and how he had taken the young man. Then he left him. Others had run in at the cries, and they had all thrown themselves down in prayer for him. They rejoiced that his mind was sound. But one of his eyes had fallen out on his cheek and hung by a thin vein. His sister’s husband had brought him there, and he said: “God, who drove out the devil, can restore his eye at the prayers of his saints.” He put the eye back and bound it with his handkerchief, and he told the young man not to loosen it for seven days. When the bandage came off, the eye was healthy.

Status: told by Saint Augustine in the City of God, Book XXII, chapter 8. He says that others were cured there too.

Source: Augustine, City of God, Book XXII, chapter 8, translated by Marcus Dods, https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/120122.htm

A woman is freed by oil and tears, Hippo

Saint Augustine writes that he knows of a young woman of Hippo who was tormented by a devil. A priest prayed for her, and his tears fell into oil. She anointed herself with the oil, and the devil left her at once.

Status: told by Saint Augustine from his own knowledge in the City of God, Book XXII, chapter 8.

A bishop prays for an unseen young man

Saint Augustine also tells of a bishop who once prayed for a young man troubled by a demon. The bishop had never seen him. The young man was cured on the spot. Saint Augustine does not name the bishop, the young man, the place, or the date.

Status: told by Saint Augustine from his own knowledge in the City of God, Book XXII, chapter 8.

A tailor finds a ring in a fish, Hippo

Saint Augustine tells of Florentius, an old man of Hippo. He was devout and poor, and he worked as a tailor. He lost his coat and had no money for another. So he went to the shrine of the Twenty Martyrs in Hippo and prayed aloud that he might be clothed. Some young men heard him and mocked him as he left, as if he had asked the martyrs for fifty pence for a coat. He walked on in silence and saw a large fish gasping on the shore. The young men helped him catch it. He sold it to a cook named Catosus, a good Christian, and told him how he had come by it. He meant to buy wool with the money so that his wife could make him a coat. When the cook cut the fish open, he found a gold ring inside. Moved by pity and by the fear of God, he gave it to the old man with the words: “See how the Twenty Martyrs have clothed you.”

Status: told by Saint Augustine as an answer to prayer to the Twenty Martyrs in the City of God, Book XXII, chapter 8.

A blind woman sees at Tibilis

Saint Augustine writes that Bishop Projectus was bringing relics of the martyr Saint Stephen to the waters of Tibilis. A great crowd came out to meet him at the shrine. A blind woman asked to be led to the bishop who carried the relics. He gave her the flowers he was carrying. She took them and touched them to her eyes, and she saw at once. Those around her were astonished. She went ahead of them full of joy, and she needed no one to guide her.

Bishop Lucillus is healed of a fistula, Sinita

Saint Augustine writes about Lucillus, the bishop of Sinita near Hippo. He carried relics of Saint Stephen in procession, with the people before him and behind him. He had suffered a long time from a fistula, and his own doctor was waiting for a chance to cut it. It was suddenly healed by the mere carrying of that sacred burden, and afterward there was no trace of it in his body.

A priest is healed and raised from death, Calama

Saint Augustine tells of Eucharius, a priest from Spain living at Calama. He had suffered a long time from the stone. Saint Possidius brought relics of Saint Stephen to him, and by them he was cured. Later he fell ill with another disease and died, and they were already binding his hands for burial. His cloak was brought from the shrine of the martyr and laid on his body, and by the help of the same martyr he was raised to life.

Status: told by Saint Augustine in the City of God, Book XXII, chapter 8. His friend Saint Possidius brought the relics.

An old nobleman named Martial is converted and baptized

Saint Augustine tells of Martial, an old nobleman who wanted nothing to do with the Christian faith. His daughter was a Christian, and her husband had been baptized that year. When Martial fell ill, they begged him with tears and prayers to become a Christian. He refused in anger and sent them away. The son-in-law went to the chapel of Saint Stephen and prayed for him there with all his heart. He asked God to give the old man a right mind, so that he would not delay believing in Christ. He prayed with groans and tears. As he left he took some flowers from the altar, and since it was night he laid them by the old man’s head. Before dawn Martial cried out for someone to run for the bishop. The bishop was away at Hippo with Saint Augustine, so priests came. He said that he believed, to the joy and amazement of all, and he was baptized. For the rest of his life he kept saying: “Christ, receive my spirit.” He did not know that these were the last words of the martyr Saint Stephen when he was stoned. They were his own last words too, for he died not long after.

Status: told by Saint Augustine in the City of God, Book XXII, chapter 8. He gives no place; the bishop of that place was with him at Hippo that night.

Two men are healed of gout

A little boy was playing in the courtyard when oxen pulling a wagon left the track. The wheel crushed him, and he seemed to be at his last gasp. His mother snatched him up and laid him at the shrine. He revived, and there was no mark on him.

A woman who has died revives, Caspalium

The dead infant son of Eleusinus is revived, Hippo

Saint Augustine writes about Eleusinus, a man of tribune rank. Eleusinus laid his dead infant son on the shrine of the martyrs in the suburb where he lived. He prayed there and poured out many tears. Then he took up his child alive.

A noblewoman named Petronia is cured, Uzalis

Saint Augustine writes that many wonders were worked at Uzalis through Saint Stephen. Bishop Evodius had placed relics of the martyr there long before Hippo had them. Petronia was a noblewoman of Carthage, and she was healed there of a long illness that no doctor could cure. Saint Augustine urged her to write an account for the people to hear, and she gladly obeyed. She wrote that she had worn a hair girdle under her clothes, with a ring on it that held a stone from an ox’s kidneys. On the way to the shrine of the martyr she stopped at her estate by the river Bagrada. As she rose to go on, she saw the ring lying at her feet. The girdle was still tied with its knots, and the ring itself was whole. She took this as a pledge of her cure, and she threw the girdle and the ring into the river. She was healed by the prayers of the martyr, Saint Augustine wrote. Saint Stephen believed in the Son of the Virgin, and such wonders were done through him because he had laid down his life for that faith.

Status: told by Saint Augustine in the City of God, Book XXII, chapter 8. He was at Uzalis soon after the cure, and he writes that Petronia lives in Carthage and that anyone who asks can confirm her account.

Paulus and Palladia healed at Easter, Hippo, about 425

Saint Augustine tells this as a wonder that no one in Hippo could forget. Paulus and Palladia were a brother and sister from Caesarea in Cappadocia. Their widowed mother had cursed them for a wrong they had done her. God punished them severely: they and their brothers and sisters shook in all their limbs. The two came to Hippo about fifteen days before Easter. Every day they came to church and prayed at the relics of Saint Stephen that God would be appeased and give them back their health. On Easter Sunday morning Paulus was holding the bars of the holy place and praying. Suddenly he fell down as if asleep, and he did not tremble. Then he rose up healed and stood quite well. The whole church was filled with voices praising God. People ran to Saint Augustine where he sat waiting to come in, and he rejoiced and gave God thanks in his heart. Paulus came in with them, fell at his knees, and was raised up to receive his kiss. They went in to the people, and the church rang with shouts: “Thanks to God! Praise to God!” Saint Augustine spoke only a few words, so that the people would think on what God had done and not on him.

Paulus dined with them and told the story of his family. On the third day after Easter both of them stood on the steps while his account was read to the people. Palladia still trembled, and the people saw in her what God’s mercy had taken from him. She went down to the holy relics and touched the bars, and she too fell as if asleep and rose up cured. They led her into the church in perfect health. Saint Augustine wrote that such a shout rose from men and women together that the cries and tears seemed never to end. They praised God without words, so loudly that his ears could hardly bear it. He asked what was in their hearts but the faith of Christ. For that faith Saint Stephen had shed his blood.

Status: told by Saint Augustine in the City of God, Book XXII, chapter 8. He was in the church both days, and his Sermons 321 to 324 belong to those days of Easter week. The date of about 425 comes from the Oxford Cult of Saints database.

Source: Augustine, City of God, Book XXII, chapter 8, translated by Marcus Dods, https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/120122.htm

A baby returns to life and is baptized, Uzalis

Saint Augustine told this in Sermon 324 on the day after Palladia’s cure. He gave it as one of many wonders God had worked at Uzalis through Saint Stephen. A woman there had a sick nursing son, and he died in her lap before he was baptized. Saint Augustine said that she wept for him more as a believer than as a mother, because she wanted him to have life in the world to come. Full of trust, she took up the dead child and ran to the shrine of the martyr Saint Stephen, and she demanded her son back. “Holy martyr, you see that I have no comfort left. I cannot say that my son has gone ahead of me, for you know that he is lost. You see why I weep. Give me back my son, so that I may have him before the face of the one who crowned you.” As she prayed like this, her son came back to life. She took him at once to the priests. He was baptized and anointed, and hands were laid on him. So he received all the sacraments. Then he was taken from this life. She led him to the grave with a face as if she were leading him to the arms of Saint Stephen. Saint Augustine said that God wanted to show her true heart, and that the woman’s faithful heart was proved.

Status: told by Saint Augustine in Sermon 324, preached at Hippo in Easter week. He calls it one miracle among many at Uzalis and gives no date for it.

Source: Augustine, Sermon 324, Latin text, https://www.augustinus.it/latino/discorsi/discorso_464_testo.htm

After Saint Augustine’s death

Miracles at the relics in Pavia, 1695–1698

On 1 October 1695 workmen at the crypt altar of San Pietro in Ciel d’Oro in Pavia found a marble box. It had letters that were read as the name of Saint Augustine. An inquiry followed. Josef Sciberras of the Augustinian Order writes that “a number of miracles, attributed to the relics” helped speed events on. He gives no list of them. In 1728 the bishop of Pavia declared the relics genuine, and Pope Benedict XIII confirmed this in writing. Both rulings concerned whether the relics were genuine.

Status: recorded by Josef Sciberras of the Augustinian Order in his history of the relics. He gives no details of the miracles.

Source: Josef Sciberras, OSA, “The Troubled Events of the Relics of Augustine of Hippo,” https://agostinjani.org/reflections-and-articles/josef-sciberras/the-troubled-events-of-the-relics-of-augustine-of-hippo-between-transfers-findings-and-devotion/

The child at the seashore

This story has been told since the later Middle Ages. Saint Augustine was working on his book On the Trinity, and he walked by the sea thinking about how God can be Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. He met a child who had dug a hole in the sand. With a spoon or a shell the child was pouring the sea into the hole. Saint Augustine asked what he was doing, and the child said he would pour the whole sea into the hole. That is impossible, Saint Augustine said. The child answered that he would do it sooner than Saint Augustine would understand the Trinity. Painters showed the scene more than once in the later Middle Ages. Benozzo Gozzoli painted it in the church of Saint Augustine at San Gimignano in the fifteenth century. By 1483 it was also painted on an altar of Saint Augustine in the church of the Black Friars at Antwerp. The Black Friars were the Dominicans.

Status: a story first written down in the later Middle Ages, retold by the medievalist Shari Boodts. The English Golden Legend that William Caxton published in 1483 adds it, and its translator says he saw it painted on the altar in Antwerp.

Source: Shari Boodts, “The Child by the Seaside: A Medieval Story about Saint Augustine,” Medievalists.net, 2019, https://www.medievalists.net/2019/02/the-child-by-the-seaside-a-medieval-story-about-saint-augustine/

A Note on the Sources

No formal Church ruling on any of these miracles was found. In Saint Augustine’s time the Church had no rules for canonization, and the people honored him as a saint. For Paulus and Palladia the year 425 comes from the Oxford Cult of Saints database, which the researchers saw only in summary. The Augustinian Order’s history of the relics mentions the Pavia miracles of 1695 to 1698 without details. The story of the child at the seashore is not in Saint Possidius or the Confessions. In the English Golden Legend that William Caxton published in 1483, the translator wrote that he had seen it painted on an altar in Antwerp. He did not find it in his copy of the Golden Legend in English, French, or Latin.
