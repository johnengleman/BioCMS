## SC-11 — The bishop's table, Hippo
- Status: full (the year is not given)
- When and where: Hippo, at the common table of Augustine's household, in his bishop years. Possidius gives no date and no room. [P-Lat ch.22, Weiskotten p.95]
- Who was there: Augustine; "some of his most intimate fellow-bishops" (the Latin is "familiarissimos coepiscopos"); Possidius, who writes "Both I and the others who were at the table experienced this" ("Quod ego et alii qui illi mensae interfuimus, experti sumus"). [P-Lat ch.22]
1. Augustine had an inscription written on his table against gossip. Possidius explains: he "warned every guest to refrain from unnecessary and harmful tales." [P-Lat ch.22]
2. Some of the closest bishops forgot the verses and spoke against them. [P-Lat ch.22]
3. Augustine "became exasperated" and rebuked them so sharply that he said either the verses must be erased from the table or he would get up in the middle of the meal and go to his room. [P-Lat ch.22]
- Details: spoons of silver, but dishes of earthenware, wood, or marble, "from the choice of his own will," not need. He loved reading and discussion at table more than eating and drinking. Frugal table: herbs and lentils, sometimes meat for guests or weaker brothers, always wine. [P-Lat ch.22] Household rule: no swearing even at table; the one who swore lost one cup of wine. The cups for each person were set beforehand. [P-Lat ch.25, p.103]
- Words spoken: the inscription (speaker: Augustine's table, a "secular" couplet, no author named by Possidius).
  Latin: "Quisquis amat dictis absentum rodere vitam, / Hac mensa indignam noverit esse suam."
  Our English: "Whoever likes to gnaw at the life of an absent person with his words should know that this table is not meant for him."
  Weiskotten (1919): "Who injures the name of an absent friend / May not at this table as guest attend."
  Locator: P-Lat ch.22, Weiskotten p.95, Latin line 5-8.
  Augustine's threat is told only in indirect speech: "aut delendos illos de mensa versus, aut se de media refectione ad suum cubiculum surrecturum" = "either those verses must be wiped off the table, or he would rise from the middle of the meal and go to his room."
- Testimony: Possidius, ch.22 (P-Eng, public-domain): "And when some of his most intimate fellow-bishops forgot that inscription and spoke without heeding it, Augustine on one occasion became exasperated and so sternly rebuked them as to declare that either those verses would have to be removed from the table or he would leave in the midst of the meal and retire to his chamber. Both I and the others who were at the table experienced this."
  plain: Some of his closest fellow-bishops once forgot the sign and spoke against it. Augustine lost his temper and told them: take the verses off this table, or I will leave the meal and go to my room. I was there, and so were the others.
- What changed: nothing is told; the rule of the table stood, and Possidius uses the scene to show Augustine's strictness.
- Not in the sources: the year, the names of the bishops, what gossip was said, whether he left, how the bishops answered, the room, or the look on his face. The text does not say Augustine wrote the couplet.
- Sources: P-Lat ch.22, ch.25; P-Eng.

## SC-12 — Firmus at his feet, Hippo
- Status: full on the event; year not in the sources
- When and where: no year. Possidius says "on the next day or the day after" ("alia die vel post biduum") after a sermon, and he adds "unless I am mistaken" ("nisi fallor"). Place: Hippo; the sermon was in the church, and Firmus came to Augustine "seated within the monastery." [P-Lat ch.15, Weiskotten pp.74-77]
- Who was there: Augustine; Possidius and "my brothers and fellow-servants who were then living with the holy man in the church at Hippo"; the merchant Firmus. [P-Lat ch.15]
1. At table Augustine asks his companions if they noticed that his sermon that day began and ended against his custom. He had left his subject "hanging" and ended by attacking the Manichees. [P-Lat ch.15]
2. They say they noticed and wondered at the time.
3. Augustine says: perhaps the Lord wanted some wanderer among the people to be taught and healed by his forgetting and his error. [P-Lat ch.15]
4. A day or two later Firmus, a merchant, throws himself on his knees at Augustine's feet, weeping, and asks the priest and his companions to pray for his sins. [P-Lat ch.15]
5. He confesses that he followed the Manichees for many years and spent much money on them, "or rather on those they call the Elect." He says Augustine's sermons had recently corrected him and made him Catholic. [P-Lat ch.15]
6. Augustine and the others ask what in the sermon satisfied him. He tells them, and they all recall the sermon's course. They thank and bless God. [P-Lat ch.15]
- Details: Firmus gives up trade, lives as a servant of God, and in another region is "called and forced" to take the office of presbyter and stays in holy life. Possidius adds: "perhaps he is still alive across the sea." [P-Lat ch.15]
- Words spoken: Augustine, Latin: "Credo, ait, forte aliquem errantem in populo Dominus per nostram oblivionem et errorem doceri et curari voluerit: in cuius manu sumus et nos et sermones nostri."
  Our English: "I think the Lord may have wanted to teach and heal someone who was lost among the people, through my forgetting and my mistake. We and our sermons are in his hand."
  Locator: P-Lat ch.15, Weiskotten p.74-75.
- Inner life: Possidius gives the view, not Augustine's own prayer: "wondering at the deep plan of God for the salvation of souls," they bless him, because he works salvation "through those who know and those who do not know." [P-Lat ch.15]
- Testimony: Possidius, ch.15 (P-Eng, public-domain): "there came a certain merchant, Firmus by name, to the holy Augustine, who was seated in the monastery, and in our presence fell down on his knees and prostrated himself at his feet, shedding tears and asking that the priest and his holy companions intercede with the Lord for his sins."
  plain: A merchant named Firmus came to Augustine as he sat in the monastery. In front of us he dropped to his knees at Augustine's feet. He was weeping and begged the priest and his companions to pray to the Lord for his sins.
  Latin: "intra monasterium sedenti sancto Augustino nobis coram, ad pedes genibus provolutus sese iactavit lacrimas fundens."
- What changed: a long-time Manichee became Catholic, left trade, and later became a presbyter elsewhere.
- Not in the sources: the year; Augustine's rank at the time (Possidius calls him "priest" in Firmus's request; the sermon's subject); what the sermon said that moved Firmus (Possidius says Firmus told them but does not record it); the town where Firmus became presbyter; any "light" or sound. Weiskotten's note: this Firmus is probably unknown and is not the Firmus of Augustine's Letters 184, 191, 194, 248.
- Sources: P-Lat ch.15 and Weiskotten note on ch.15; P-Eng.

## SC-13 — The prayer at table, Hippo, summer 430
- Status: full. The start of the siege is not dated; only "third month" and "almost fourteen months."
- When and where: Hippo, during the Vandal siege of 430. The table is the common table. Possidius says the invaders came "by ship from the regions of Spain" and that the siege lasted "almost fourteen months." [P-Lat ch.28] Death in the third month: Weiskotten gives 28 Aug 430, from Prosper's Chronicon [P-Lat intro n.1].
- Who was there: Augustine; Possidius and other bishops from the nearby regions who had taken refuge in the city and stayed the whole siege; "our people" and others in the city. Count Boniface held the city with allied Goths. [P-Lat ch.28-29]
1. Possidius and the bishops often talked of God's judgments and prayed together with groans and tears. [P-Lat ch.28]
2. "At one time," at table, Augustine told them what he asked of God. [P-Lat ch.29]
3. All joined the same prayer for themselves, the other bishops, and the city. [P-Lat ch.29]
4. "In the third month of the siege he took to his bed with fever." [P-Lat ch.29]
- Details: Possidius says that before this, Augustine's "tears were his food by day and night," and that he saw cities fall, churches burn, bishops stripped and begging; of countless churches only Carthage, Hippo, and Cirta stood. [P-Lat ch.28]
- Words spoken: Augustine, Latin: "Noveritis me hoc tempore nostrae calamitatis id Deum rogare, ut aut hanc civitatem ab hostibus circumdatam liberare dignetur, aut si aliud ei videtur, suos servos ad perferendam suam voluntatem fortes faciat, aut certe ut sese de hoc saeculo ad se accipiat."
  Our English: "I want you to know what I ask of God in this time of our trouble. Either that he free this city that the enemy has surrounded; or, if he sees it differently, that he make his servants strong to bear his will; or at least that he take me from this world to himself."
  Locator: P-Lat ch.29, Weiskotten pp.116-117. (Weiskotten's apparatus shows manuscript variants in this line; the Latin here follows his text.)
- Inner life: Possidius: "the Lord did not cheat his servant of the reward of his prayer. What he asked with tears and prayers for himself and the city he obtained in due time." [P-Lat ch.29]
- Testimony: Possidius ch.29 (P-Eng, public-domain), the prayer as above in Weiskotten: "either that He may be pleased to free this city which is surrounded by the foe, or if something else seems good in His sight, that He make His servants brave for enduring His will, or at least that He may take me from this world unto Himself." plain: as our English above.
- What changed: the bishops join his prayer; he soon falls ill and does not leave the sickbed.
- Not in the sources: the exact day of the table, who sat where, the food, how the siege sounded or looked, whether the city was relieved before he died (Butler p.504-505 says the siege was raised later and credits his prayer; Weiskotten says July 431, which is after his death). No quotation of what any bishop said.
- Sources: P-Lat ch.28-29; P-Eng; Weiskotten note (July 431, Prosper); BUT8 pp.503-505.

## SC-14 — Death, Hippo, 28 August 430
- Status: full; no last words are in Possidius ch.31.
- When and where: Hippo, 28 August 430, third month of the siege. Possidius gives the siege month and the age (76) but not the day; the day is from Prosper's Chronicon, quoted in Weiskotten's note. [P-Lat ch.29, ch.31; Weiskotten intro n.1] Place: his bed, in Hippo. No room is described.
- Who was there: Augustine; Possidius and other bishops ("we who were present," "while we stood by"); doctors; the man with the sick relative. [P-Lat ch.29, 31]
1. In his illness a man comes with a sick relative and asks him to lay a hand on him. [P-Lat ch.29]
2. Augustine answers that if he had any power in such things he would have used it on himself first. [P-Lat ch.29]
3. The man says a dream told him: "Go to the bishop Augustine, that he lay his hand on him, and he will be well." Augustine "did not delay to do it." Possidius: the sick man was healed at once. [P-Lat ch.29]
4. Augustine has the shortest penitential Psalms of David copied, and as he lies in bed he looks at the sheets set against the wall, reads them, and weeps "freely and constantly." [P-Lat ch.31]
5. About ten days before the end he asks that no one come in except at the doctors' hours or when food is brought. All that time he is free for prayer. [P-Lat ch.31]
6. He preached until the last illness. At the end all his limbs were whole, sight and hearing sound; while they stood by, watched, and prayed, "he slept with his fathers." [P-Lat ch.31]
7. After a service for the repose of his body, he was buried. No will: "as a poor man of God he had nothing to make one from." He ordered the church library and all the books kept for later generations. [P-Lat ch.31]
- Details: The healing is Possidius's own report of a miracle ("Novi quoque," "I know also"); mark it "reported." [P-Lat ch.29] Augustine's own teaching before death that even good Christians and priests should not leave life "without fitting repentance." [P-Lat ch.31]
- Words spoken: man's dream-message, Latin: "Vade ad Augustinum episcopum, ut eidem manum imponat, et salvus erit." Our English: "Go to bishop Augustine, so that he may lay his hand on him, and he will be well." [P-Lat ch.29, Weiskotten p.118] Augustine's answer is indirect only ("si aliquid in his posset, sibi hoc utique primitus praestitisset"): "if he had any power in these things, he would surely have used it on himself first." (paraphrase, as in the Latin)
- Testimony: Possidius ch.31 (P-Eng, public-domain), about 105 words: "he commanded that the shortest penitential Psalms of David should be copied for him, and during the days of his sickness as he lay in bed he would look at these sheets as they hung upon the wall and read them; and he wept freely and constantly. ... With all the members of his body intact, with sight and hearing unimpaired, while we stood by and watched and prayed, 'he slept with his fathers,' as it is written, 'well-nourished in a good old age.'"
  plain: He had the shortest psalms of repentance copied out. As he lay sick he looked at the pages on the wall and read them, and he wept without stopping. His body was whole and his sight and hearing were clear. While we stood by, watched, and prayed, he died, as the Bible says, in a good old age.
  Latin: "sibi iusserat Psalmos Davidicos, qui sunt paucissimi de poenitentia scribi ... et ubertim ac iugiter flebat ... nobis astantibus et videntibus et orantibus, dormivit cum patribus suis."
- What changed: he dies with nothing to leave but the library, the clergy, and the monasteries; the pear-thief ends poor.
- Not in the sources: any last words; the hour; the weather; the names of the bishops at the bed; the name of the sick man or his illness; the month of Possidius's account (the day, 28 August, is Prosper's, via Weiskotten). Do not state the siege was lifted before he died. Hanging psalms: Possidius says "sheets" ("quaterniones," quires), not framed pictures.
- Sources: P-Lat ch.29, 31; P-Eng; Weiskotten intro n.1 and note on Prosper, Chronicon PL 51, 597; CM-S1.

## SC-15 — Pavia, 1 October 1695
- Status: full on the facts; the words of the letters are thin (two sources disagree on their form).
- When and where: Tuesday morning, 1 October 1695, in the church of San Pietro in Ciel d'Oro, Pavia, at the altar in the crypt. [SCI]
- Who was there: a group of bricklayers; the sacristan friar of the Hermits, called at once because the prior was not in the city. [SCI]
1. The bricklayers work on the crypt altar. Lifting paving stones behind it, they find a buried box of white Carrara marble, about 120 x 30 x 30 cm. [SCI]
2. It has letters in charcoal that those present read as the name of Augustine. [SCI]
3. Inside is another marble box, carved; inside that, a silver box decorated with Lombard-style crosses. [SCI]
4. Opening the silver box, they see human remains wrapped in cloth. [SCI]
5. Later: exhumation before the authorities of the two communities (Hermits and Canons) and others, with anatomists listing the remains; inquiry Nov 1695 to Feb 1696 and in 1698; "a number of miracles" attributed to the relics sped events. [SCI]
6. 1728: Pope Benedict XIII asked for conclusions; the bones were reopened in May and work ended 20 June; the Bishop of Pavia, Francesco Pertusati, confirmed authenticity in July (public proclamation set for 16 July); on 22 September 1728 the Pope confirmed it in writing. [SCI]
- Words of the letters: SCI says "charcoal" and gives no wording beyond "the name of Augustine." Butler says the marble coffin had "in many places" the name "Augustinus" "engraved." [BUT8 p.505] The sources conflict on charcoal versus engraved; the writer should follow SCI (the Order's own account, nearer the find) and say "letters" without a form, or keep both unmentioned.
- Testimony: Butler (public-domain), p.505: "In this condition the sacred bones were discovered in 1695. They were incontestably proved authentic by the bishop of Pavia in 1728, whose sentence was confirmed by pope Benedict XIII. in the same year."
  plain: The bones were found in this state in 1695. In 1728 the bishop of Pavia judged them to be genuine beyond doubt, and Pope Benedict XIII confirmed his ruling that same year.
  Butler's wider chain: Bede's Martyrology says the body went to Sardinia and was ransomed from Saracens and placed in St Peter's at Pavia about 720; Oldrad of Milan, by Charlemagne's order, wrote a history of this from the Pavia archives; King Luitprand paid a great sum and had it hidden under a brick wall, in a lead coffin inside silver inside marble. [BUT8 p.504-505] SCI says the Sardinia move has "no reliable documents"; do not tell it as fact.
- What changed: the relics, hidden for centuries, were found and then judged genuine by bishop and pope.
- Not in the sources: the crypt's look, the hour, who spoke, any sound, the friar's name, what anyone felt. Butler's "incontestably" is Butler's word (1779), not an official finding. SCI notes scholars of the time still disputed it ("controversy among the scholars"); the 1728 judgment came with a threat of excommunication on those who disagreed.
- Sources: SCI (facts-only); BUT8 pp.504-505 (the Butler public-domain account). Not read: Fontanini (1728) and Touron, which Butler cites; Oldrad's letter.

## FN-1 — The Rule (Letter 211) and orders that follow it
Letter 211 is dated A.D. 423 by New Advent. Augustine wrote it to the nuns of a Hippo monastery (his sister had been its prioress) after a dispute over her successor. In it he praises the nuns' choice "to dwell with one accord in fellowship under the same roof, that you may have one soul and one heart in God" (sec. 2) and sends general rules. Butler says the rule is in the second letter to the nuns, that the Hermits of St Austin (united as one order by Pope Alexander IV in 1254) and the Regular Canons both follow it, and that it was copied into the rule of Caesarius [BUT8 pp.449-450 and note on p.~447]. Sources: newadvent.org/fathers/1102211.htm (public-domain, Letter 211 secs. 1-4 read; the rule text itself, sec. 5 on, not read in this pass); BUT8 (public-domain). Orders not named by Butler as following it are not claimed.

## FN-2 — A later council shaped by his writings
Council: the Second Council of Orange, 529, under Caesarius of Arles. Butler p.497 says the Semipelagian error, which Augustine had opposed in 429 in two books answering Prosper and Hilary, "was condemned in the second council of Orange, under Saint Caesarius, in 529," confirmed by Pope Boniface II [BUT8 p.497]. Butler p.505-506 adds that councils "have frequently borrowed the words" of Augustine, and names Peter Lombard and Thomas Aquinas as following his principles (no date given). Caution: no source I read says the council text cites Augustine; the link is from Butler. The papalencyclicals.net page on Orange had no match for "Augustine" in the short text I got. Rights: public-domain. The Rule of Caesarius also includes Augustine's rule [BUT8 p.450], a second link, undated.

## FN-3 — Orthodox veneration and the 15 June date
The Orthodox Church keeps the feast of Augustine of Hippo on 15 June. Source: Sanidopoulos, "Synaxarion of Saint Augustine of Hippo" (johnsanidopoulos.com/2017/06/synaxarion-of-saint-augustine-of-hippo.html), Synaxarion text credited to St Nikodemos the Hagiorite; header "Feast Day - June 15." It has an Apolytikion in the Third Tone calling him "an expounder of the City of God" and "a wise and God-inspired Hierarch." It says Greek readers knew him mainly through a few translated works, and cites Dositheos that Orthodox accept his writings only where they agree with the Church's common view. A note gives his real dates, 13 Nov 354 and 28 Aug 430. This is paraphrase; rights not checked, so use facts only.
