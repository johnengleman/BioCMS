## SC-01 — The pear tree, about 370
- Status: full
- When and where: At night ("nocte intempesta", the dead of night), in Augustine's "sixteenth year" [OW-S1 II.4.9, II.6.12]. Near the family vineyard, at home in Thagaste. The year is not given. Born Nov 354 [W-S5], so about 370 (the notes' count).
- Who was there: Augustine and a group of other boys ("nequissimi adulescentuli", very worthless youths). No names, no number. Context: his father Patricius was a poor town councillor of Thagaste; he was home from Madaura for a year while money for Carthage was saved; his mother Monica was a believer, his father still a catechumen [OW-S1 II.3.5-6].
- What happened, in order:
  1. A year of idleness at home, studies paused; "the thorns of lust" grew over his head [OW-S1 II.3.5-6].
  2. Near the vineyard stood a pear tree, heavy with fruit, neither beautiful nor good to taste [II.4.9].
  3. The boys had stayed out late, playing in the open ground as their bad habit was; at dead of night they went to shake the tree and carry the pears off [II.4.9].
  4. They took huge loads, not to eat but to throw to the pigs; they ate a little, because the point was to do what was forbidden [II.4.9].
  5. He had better pears at home. He picked these only to steal them, and threw them away [II.6.12].
- Details: The pears: "nec forma nec sapore inlecebrosis". He had "meliorum copia" (plenty of better ones) [II.6.12]. The fruit went "proicienda porcis".
- Words spoken: One phrase of the boys: "eamus, faciamus" ("let's go, let's do it") [II.9.17]. Augustine does not say this was said that night; he says it is how such a thing is started.
- Inner life: He asks what he loved in the theft. Answer: not the pears; the company. Alone he would not have done it. He calls it "nimis inimica amicitia", an over-hostile friendship. See quotations.
- Testimony (Augustine, Confessions II.4.9, II.6.12, II.8.16, II.9.17; Latin; public-domain; our English):
  - "arbor erat pirus in vicinia nostrae vineae pomis onusta nec forma nec sapore inlecebrosis. ad hanc excutiendam atque asportandam nequissimi adulescentuli perreximus nocte intempesta (quousque ludum de pestilentiae more in areis produxeramus) et abstulimus inde onera ingentia, non ad nostras epulas sed vel proicienda porcis, etiamsi aliquid inde comedimus" [II.4.9]
    English: "There was a pear tree near our vineyard, loaded with fruit that was neither pretty nor tasty. A gang of worthless young fellows, we went to shake it and carry it off in the dead of night, after we had kept our game going in the open, as our bad habit was. We took away huge loads, not for our own meals but to throw to the pigs, though we ate a few."
  - "nam decerpta proieci, epulatus inde solam iniquitatem qua laetabar fruens." [II.6.12] English: "For I threw away what I picked, feasting there only on the wrong, which I enjoyed."
  - "foeda erat, et amavi eam." [II.4.9] English: "It was ugly, and I loved it."
  - "et tamen solus id non fecissem (sic recordor animum tunc meum), solus omnino id non fecissem." [II.8.16] English: "And yet I would not have done it alone (so I remember my mind then); alone I would not have done it at all."
  - "sed cum dicitur, 'eamus, faciamus,' et pudet non esse impudentem." [II.9.17] English: "But when someone says 'Come on, let's do it,' one is ashamed not to be shameless."
- What changed: Nothing in the story itself. Years later he writes it as the plainest case of the divided will: wrong done for no gain, because of company.
- Not in the sources: How many boys; names; a wall, ladder, dog, owner, moon, weather, season; that anyone caught them; that he was the leader; what he felt that night. The pigs are only an intention ("vel proicienda porcis").
- Sources: OW-S1 II.3.5-II.9.17; OW-S3 checked; W-S5 (birth year).

## SC-02 — Monica's dream and the bishop, during his Manichee years (about 373-383)
- Status: full (place and year thin)
- When and where: While Augustine was a Manichee. Augustine says "nine years or so" followed the dream, in which he rolled in the mud [OW-S1 III.11.20]. The text names no town and no year; he was a Manichee from about 19 to 28 (Dods IV.1.1 [OW-S3]). The bishop is not named and no place is given for his talk with Monica.
- Who was there: Monica; Augustine (told of the dream afterward); an unnamed bishop, raised in the Church and trained in the Scriptures.
- What happened, in order:
  1. Monica refused at first to eat or live in the same house with her son, because she hated his blasphemies [III.11.19].
  2. She dreamed she stood on a wooden rule ("regula lignea"), weeping. A bright, cheerful young man asked why she grieved. She said she wept for her son's ruin. He told her to look: where she was, there was her son also. She looked and saw him standing beside her on the same rule [III.11.19].
  3. She told Augustine. He tried to read it as: she should not despair of becoming what he was. She answered at once that the words were "where you are, he is also", not the reverse [III.11.20].
  4. Augustine says that quick answer moved him more than the dream itself [III.11.20].
  5. Later she asked a bishop to talk with her son and refute his errors. He refused: Augustine was "indocilem", puffed up with his new sect, and had troubled many untrained people with small questions. He told her to leave him alone and only pray for him: he would find the error by reading [III.12.21].
  6. The bishop said that as a child his own mother had given him to the Manichees; he had copied their books and left on his own, with no one arguing with him [III.12.21].
  7. She pressed him, with many tears. Annoyed, he said: "vade a me. ita vivas: fieri non potest, ut filius istarum lacrimarum pereat." She took it as a voice from heaven [III.12.21].
- Details: Her tears "watered the ground" wherever she prayed [III.11.19]. The young man was "splendidum hilarem atque arridentem". Augustine: "She was a chaste, devout, sober widow."
- Words spoken (Latin; our English):
  - Monica: "non enim mihi dictum est, 'ubi ille, ibi et tu,' sed, 'ubi tu, ibi et ille.'" English: "It was not said to me 'Where he is, there you are,' but 'Where you are, there he is.'" [III.11.20]
  - Bishop: "sine illum ibi. tantum roga pro eo dominum. ipse legendo reperiet quis ille sit error et quanta impietas." English: "Leave him where he is. Only ask the Lord for him. By reading he will find out what this error is and how great the wrong." [III.12.21]
  - Bishop: "vade a me. ita vivas: fieri non potest, ut filius istarum lacrimarum pereat." English: "Go away from me. As you live: it cannot be that the son of these tears will perish." [III.12.21]
- Inner life: Augustine says God "did not despise her tears" and that the dream was how God comforted her [III.11.19].
- Testimony (Augustine, Conf. III.12.21; Latin; public-domain; our English):
  "ille iam substomachans taedio, 'vade' inquit 'a me. ita vivas: fieri non potest, ut filius istarum lacrimarum pereat.' quod illa ita se accepisse inter conloquia sua mecum saepe recordabatur, ac si de caelo sonuisset."
  English: "He, by now a little annoyed and tired, said, 'Go away from me. As you live: it cannot be that the son of these tears will perish.' She often recalled, in her talks with me, that she took this as if it had sounded from heaven."
- What changed: She keeps praying. Augustine's years in the sect continue for about nine more years [III.11.20].
- Not in the sources: The bishop's name or city; where the dream or the talk took place; the date; that Monica sat at the same table afterward (the text says God consoled her "that she agreed to live with me and have the same table", III.11.19); any later meeting of the bishop and Augustine.
- Sources: OW-S1 III.11.19-III.12.21; OW-S3 (meaning check); W-S5 (dates from the notes).

## SC-03 — The friend dies, Thagaste, about 372-375 (year not in sources)
- Status: full (the year is thin)
- When and where: "In those years, when I first began to teach in the town where I was born" [OW-S1 IV.4.7]. The home town is Thagaste ("Thagastensi oppido", IV.7.12). The year is not given. Augustine says the nine years from his nineteenth to his twenty-eighth were his Manichee years (Dods IV.1.1 [OW-S3]); notes put him at Carthage from 374 [W-S5]. Say "in his early twenties".
- Who was there: Augustine; a friend of his own age, grown up with him, "classmates and playmates". The friend is not named. Augustine had led him from the faith into Manichee "superstitious fables"; Monica mourned that [IV.4.7].
- What happened, in order:
  1. The friendship lasted "scarcely a year" [IV.4.7].
  2. The friend was ill with fever; he lay a long time unconscious in a deadly sweat. As he was given up, he was baptized without knowing it, and Augustine did not mind [IV.4.8].
  3. He recovered. Augustine joked with him about the baptism, expecting him to laugh. The friend drew back from him as from an enemy and told him, with sudden boldness, to stop if he wished to stay a friend [IV.4.8].
  4. Augustine, shocked, put the matter off until the friend was strong. Within a few days, while Augustine was away, the fever returned and the friend died [IV.4.8].
  5. Grief: everything he saw was death; his homeland was torture and his father's house a misery; only weeping was sweet [IV.4.9]. He marvelled that others lived; he could not bear to live "half" [IV.6.11]. No place gave rest [IV.7.12].
  6. He left: "et tamen fugi de patria ... a Thagastensi oppido veni Carthaginem" [IV.7.12].
- Details: Nothing of the friend's looks, name, family, or the baptizer. Ordinary things hurt: groves, songs, banquets, bed, books [IV.7.12]. Even "lux" (daylight) was a horror.
- Words spoken: The friend's only reported act is his sharp rebuke, given without words quoted. No quoted speech.
- Inner life: He asked his soul why it was sad and it could not answer; told to hope in God, it "rightly did not obey", because the lost friend was truer than the "phantasm" he was told to hope in [IV.4.9]. He says his god then was an empty phantasm [IV.7.12].
- Testimony (Augustine, Conf. IV.6.11 and IV.4.8, 4.9, 7.12; Latin; public-domain; our English):
  - "mirabar enim ceteros mortales vivere, quia ille, quem quasi non moriturum dilexeram, mortuus erat, et me magis, quia ille alter eram, vivere illo mortuo mirabar. bene quidam dixit de amico suo: 'dimidium animae' suae. nam ego sensi animam meam et animam illius unam fuisse animam in duobus corporibus, et ideo mihi horrori erat vita, quia nolebam dimidius vivere" [IV.6.11]
    English: "I was amazed that other people lived, because he, whom I had loved as if he would never die, was dead; and I was more amazed that I lived, since I was his other self. Someone said well of his friend: 'half of his soul.' I felt that his soul and mine had been one soul in two bodies. That is why life was a horror to me: I did not want to live as half a man."
  - "et erat mihi patria supplicium et paterna domus mira infelicitas" [IV.4.9] English: "My own country was a torture to me and my father's house a strange misery."
  - "et tamen fugi de patria." [IV.7.12] English: "And yet I ran away from my home town."
- What changed: He left Thagaste for Carthage.
- Not in the sources: The friend's name, age, or looks; where Augustine was away; who baptized him; the funeral; the date. The poet who said "half of my soul" is not named (do not name Horace).
- Sources: OW-S1 IV.4.7-IV.7.12; OW-S3; W-S5 (Carthage from 374).

## SC-04 — The shore at Carthage, about 383
- Status: full
- When and where: Carthage harbor, at night, about 383. The date is not in the passage. Notes give nine years of teaching from 374 [W-S5 intro].
- Who was there: Augustine; Monica, his mother, who followed him to the sea. A "friend" whose ship he pretended to wait for.
- What happened, in order:
  1. He had decided to go to Rome. His main reason: students there were said to be quieter and better disciplined; at Carthage they burst into classes and made trouble. Better pay and rank were also promised [OW-S1 V.8.14].
  2. He told neither God's plan nor his mother. She mourned bitterly at his leaving and followed him to the sea [V.8.15].
  3. She clung to him to make him stay or take her with him. He lied: he pretended he would not leave a friend until a wind rose for him to sail [V.8.15].
  4. She refused to go home without him. He barely persuaded her to spend the night in a place next to their ship: the "memoria" of St. Cyprian [V.8.15].
  5. That night he left in secret. She stayed, praying and weeping [V.8.15].
  6. The wind blew, filled the sails, and took the shore out of sight. In the morning she was there, beside herself with grief, filling God's ears with complaints [V.8.15].
  7. After accusing him of deceit and cruelty, she turned back to praying for him and went home to her usual ways; he went to Rome [V.8.15].
- Details: "memoria beati Cypriani": Dods renders "oratory". The word is "memoria"; do not call it a church with a crypt. "Vix persuasi", he persuaded her only with difficulty.
- Words spoken: None quoted. His lie, in his own telling, is the reported statement in step 3.
- Inner life: He calls the lie a sin God "mercifully forgave"; "et mentitus sum matri, et illi matri" ("I lied to my mother, and to such a mother"). He says her tears asked that he not sail, and God answered what she wanted more deeply: that he become what she always prayed for.
- Testimony (Augustine, Conf. V.8.15; Latin; public-domain; our English):
  "sed fefelli eam, violenter me tenentem ut aut revocaret aut mecum pergeret. et finxi me amicum nolle deserere donec vento facto navigaret, et mentitus sum matri, et illi matri. et evasi ... et tamen recusanti sine me redire vix persuasi ut in loco qui proximus nostrae navi erat, memoria beati Cypriani, maneret ea nocte. sed ea nocte clanculo ego profectus sum, illa autem non; mansit orando et flendo. ... flavit ventus et implevit vela nostra et litus subtraxit aspectibus nostris, in quo mane illa insaniebat dolore, et querellis et gemitu implebat aures tuas"
  English: "But I deceived her, as she held on to me hard, to call me back or to come with me. I pretended I would not leave a friend until a wind came up and he sailed, and I lied to my mother, and to such a mother. And I got away ... But when she would not go home without me, I barely talked her into spending that night in the place nearest our ship, the memorial of blessed Cyprian. That night I sailed in secret, and she did not; she stayed, praying and weeping ... The wind blew and filled our sails and took the shore out of our sight. On that shore, in the morning, she was wild with grief and filled your ears with her complaints and groaning."
  (The "..." marks my cuts: the clause on God's mercy, and the lines asking what she begged for.)
- What changed: He is in Rome; Monica later follows him by sea and land (VI.1.1).
- Not in the sources: A "friend" who was real (it was a pretense); the ship's name; the hour; Monica's words to him; whether she saw him go; weather beyond the wind. The Latin does not say whose ship it was.
- Sources: OW-S1 V.8.14-15; OW-S3; W-S5 intro (nine years from 374).

## SC-05 — Ambrose reading in silence, Milan, 384-386
- Status: full (years from the notes' range; not in the passage)
- When and where: Milan, when he first taught rhetoric there. The passage gives no year. Notes give 384-386 [notes-witnesses.md, Milan 384-386]. A room where anyone could walk in and visitors were not announced.
- Who was there: Augustine, other visitors, and Ambrose the bishop. Ambrose is surrounded by "catervae negotiosorum hominum" (crowds of busy people) whose troubles he served.
- What happened, in order:
  1. Milan asked Rome for a teacher of rhetoric; Augustine got the post through Symmachus, then prefect, and came to Milan to Bishop Ambrose, who received him "paterne" like a father [OW-S1 V.13.23].
  2. He began to love Ambrose first as a kind man, not a teacher of truth; he went to hear him preach to judge his speech, not his message [V.13.23].
  3. Every Sunday he heard Ambrose teach the people [VI.3.4].
  4. He could not put his own questions to Ambrose, because crowds shut him off from the bishop's ear and mouth. Ambrose gave the few free minutes to eating or to reading [VI.3.3].
  5. Visitors saw him read with his eyes moving, voice and tongue still, "never otherwise". Augustine and others sat in a long silence, then left [VI.3.3].
  6. Augustine guessed three reasons: not to be pulled into explaining hard lines, not to lose his time with books, and to save his voice, which tired easily [VI.3.3].
- Details: "Non enim vetabatur quisquam ingredi", no one was kept out and no one announced. Ambrose was unmarried; Augustine called him "happy" by worldly measure, and thought his celibacy hard [VI.3.3].
- Words spoken: None in this scene.
- Inner life: He says he then no longer groaned in prayer; his mind was set on questions and arguing. "Ambrose did not know my tides nor the pit of my danger." In V.13.23 he says God led him there "nesciens" so as to lead him knowingly to God.
- Testimony (Augustine, Conf. VI.3.3; Latin; public-domain; our English):
  "non enim quaerere ab eo poteram quod volebam, sicut volebam, secludentibus me ab eius aure atque ore catervis negotiosorum hominum, quorum infirmitatibus serviebat. ... sed cum legebat, oculi ducebantur per paginas et cor intellectum rimabatur, vox autem et lingua quiescebant. saepe cum adessemus (non enim vetabatur quisquam ingredi aut ei venientem nuntiari mos erat), sic eum legentem vidimus tacite et aliter numquam, sedentesque in diuturno silentio (quis enim tam intento esse oneri auderet?) discedebamus"
  English: "I could not ask him what I wanted, as I wanted, because crowds of busy people kept me from his ear and mouth, and he served their weaknesses. ... But when he read, his eyes moved over the pages and his heart searched for the meaning, while his voice and tongue were still. Often when we were there (no one was barred from coming in, and it was not his custom to have visitors announced), we saw him reading like this, silently, and never otherwise. We sat a long time in silence (who would dare to burden a man so intent?) and went away."
- Also (Latin, one line each): "quamquam et causa servandae vocis, quae illi facillime obtundebatur, poterat esse iustior tacite legendi" [VI.3.3]; English: "although saving his voice, which tired very easily, could be a better reason for reading silently." And "ad eum autem ducebar abs te nesciens, ut per eum ad te sciens ducerer" [V.13.23]; English: "I was led to him by you without knowing it, so that through him I might be led to you knowing it." Possidius, as in the notes: Augustine heard Ambrose's sermons "intendebat suspensus atque affixus" ("hanging on and fixed") [W-S1 ch.1, notes].
- What changed: He heard Ambrose explain Old Testament passages as allegory, and the Catholic faith began to look defensible, though not yet won [V.14.24].
- Not in the sources: The room's furniture; the book Ambrose read; that Ambrose noticed Augustine; any talk between them in this scene; Augustine's age at the time.
- Sources: OW-S1 V.13.23-V.14.24, VI.3.3-4; OW-S3; W-S1 ch.1 (notes).
