# Nicholas of Myra — scene cards, group 3 (SC-09 to SC-12, FN-1 to FN-3)

Researched 2026-10-04. Source keys follow notes.md (S1 to S20). New sources are listed in full on the cards. I read only English texts. I did not reach the Greek or Latin originals, so each quotation below is English only, with a `plain:` line.

## SC-09 — A sermon at the tomb (Myra, early 8th century)
- Status: thin. The sermon is real and quotable, but it says nothing about pilgrims or oil at the tomb.
- When and where: early 8th century (the page dates it so). Place: Myra, "this temple" (the text's word). The claim "at his grave site" comes only from S5, who cites Migne PG 97, 1191-1206.
- Who was there: Andrew of Crete (S5: d. 740) and a "faithful audience". The sermon names Myra's current bishop only as "successor of your sacred dwelling". No names of pilgrims.
- What happened:
  1. Andrew opens by thanking Nicholas for "miraculous and ardent help" [S13].
  2. He lists what to call Nicholas: farmer, architect, soldier, angel, steersman [S13].
  3. As steersman, Nicholas turns "a hurricane into a breeze". The preacher then retells the famine at Andriake: Nicholas "seen standing by seamen in the manner of a sailor", and "three gold coins" left behind [S13].
  4. He calls the crowd to come together "today" for the feast and to honor Nicholas with spiritual flowers and hymns, not with pagan games [S13].
- Details: the only myrrh is a figure of speech: "anoint him with the fragrant myrrh—our own deeds". The sermon contains no oil, tomb, relic, or pilgrim.
- Words spoken (Andrew, Greek, English by Jaakko Olkinuora, 2015, public domain):
  - "Receive the sermon we deliver to you as a gift, and consider it our gratitude in return for grace for the miraculous and ardent help you have shown toward us." plain: Accept this sermon as a gift. It is our thanks for the grace and the wonderful, warm help you have given us.
  - "Having come into this temple, let us celebrate the sacred and high feast, and let us keep the memory, most worthy of praise, of our God-bearing father." plain: We have come into this church. Let us keep this great feast and honor the memory of our father, who carried God within him.
- Testimony (Andrew of Crete, Oration 18 / BHG 1362, early 8th c.; locator: the paragraph "Should we call you a steersman?"; rights: public domain, stated on the page; Olkinuora's English): "Should we call you a steersman? We would be speaking the truth: for you steer most orderly those, who sail through a big and wide sea and those who are at sea near the land with the tiller of intercessions towards God and the steering-paddle of faith, turning a hurricane into a breeze and a storm into a calm, by the vigils of prayers, and, O most sacred one, you lead those, who come in faith, to the harbour of detachment."
  - plain: Shall we call you a ship's pilot? That would be true. With the tiller of your prayers to God and the steering oar of faith, you guide people on the wide sea and near the coast. Your night prayers turn a hurricane into a breeze and a storm into calm. You lead those who come in faith into the harbor of inner peace.
- What changed: nothing changes in a sermon. It shows Nicholas already honored at a yearly feast, with the sea and the famine ships at the center of his fame.
- Not in the sources: the pilgrims, the crowd's size, the oil, the tomb, the weather, the hour. Do not describe Andrew standing at the tomb as the text's own claim.
- Cautions: Anrich doubts that Andrew wrote it. The St Nicholas Center footnote says the miracle part mixes in Nicholas of Sion. The text says Nicholas pushed a bishop, Theognes, to reconcile with "Come to reconciliation, O brother, before the sun sets on our anger." That is not needed here.
- Sources: S13 (St Nicholas Center page, public domain per the page, fetched in full via --max-words); S5 for "grave site" and the PG locator.

## SC-10 — Breaking the tomb (Myra, April 1087)
- Status: full. Two independent accounts. Rights: both translations are facts-only. Short quotes only.
- When and where: April 1087 (see the date conflict below). Myra, the shrine church, "set apart from the habitations of men" [S9].
- Who was there: 47 armed men from Bari; four custodian monks; two priests among the Bari men, Grimoald and (in S8) Lupus; a young man, Matthew; two Jerusalem pilgrims, one Greek and one French, who had scouted ahead; other armed Bari men at the ships [S9; S8].
- What happened, in order:
  1. Two pilgrims scout Myra and find no Turks. Forty-seven armed men go to the shrine; the rest guard the ships [S9].
  2. They ask the monks to show the body. The monks think they mean the place of offering and show where they take the oil with a sponge [S9].
  3. The monks grow afraid and say they would rather die than let him be taken. The Bari men say they came for that purpose and claim the pope sent them. They offer gold. The monks refuse; the Bari men bind them and guard the gates [S9].
  4. A priest sets a glass vial of the oil on a column. It falls on the marble and does not break. The Bari men take this as the saint's consent [S9]. S8 names the priest Lupus.
  5. Matthew seizes a monk with his sword drawn and threatens him. A more gentle monk tells of a vision a year before [S9; S8, where Matthew's sword comes after the vial].
  6. Matthew puts the sword away, takes an iron mallet, breaks the pavement, and finds a white marble tomb. He breaks the lid [S9].
  7. The coffin is "full of holy liquid up to the umbilicus". A scent rises. It carries to the ships, nearly three miles away [S9].
  8. Matthew steps into the tomb in his shoes and washes his hands in the liquid. The monks weep and lament. Grimoald wraps the bones in silk. The men carry the body to the ships singing [S9].
  9. The Bari men refuse the monks' request to keep the old icon; they leave it in Myra [S9].
- Details: iron mallet, marble pavement, the white marble tomb, glass phial, silk covering [S9]. S8: the bones "glowing like coals of fire", Matthew kisses them [S8 sec.6].
- Words spoken:
  - Monks' vision (Nicephorus, Latin; Jones's English; facts-only): a year earlier Nicholas appeared to three residents and told them to call the citizens, who had fled "to a mountain twelve stades away", back to Myra, or "he would migrate elsewhere". Paraphrase.
  - Monks' lament (Nicephorus; Jones; short quote): "Our lord, dearest father, why dost thou desert us when we are afflicted by so many miseries?" plain: Our lord and dear father, why do you leave us when we already suffer so much?
  - S8 sec.7 lament (paraphrase): they call him their only father, who never allowed this, and ask who will now be their patron and give them justice against their enemies.
- Inner life: none from Nicholas. The Bari men read the vial's fall as his voice: "Regard that as a miracle; but now leave, taking my body with you." That is Nicephorus's own comment on what the Bari men believed, not a recorded saying. Paraphrase.
- Testimony: facts-only sources, so no long passage. Nicephorus is the writer, within about a week of 9 May per S9's intro. He did not go to Myra himself in the text. He says he records what "those who saw it narrated" for the bird episode; the Myra scenes carry no eyewitness claim.
- What changed: the body leaves Myra; the monks and later the townsfolk lament on the shore [S9].
- Not in the sources: the monks' names, Matthew's age, the day of the week, the hour, weather. The outline says "a vial of oil falls", with Lupus; only S8 names Lupus. Nicephorus has the vial before Matthew's sword; S8 agrees. The outline's order of sword, then vial, is wrong.
- Date conflict: Nicephorus's own text says the body was "borne away on the eleventh day of April". S8 sec.19 says "eleven days before the Kalends of May" (21 April). The "20 April" in notes comes from a St Nicholas Center caption. Write "April 1087".
- Threat: S9 says Turks; S8 says Persians (twelve stades in both).
- Sources: S9 (Nicephorus, tr. Jones 1978, St Nicholas Center, https://www.stnicholascenter.org/pages/translation-1/); S8 (Greek Anonymous, https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/basis/nicholas-bari.asp).

## SC-11 — Becalmed at Perdikca (the voyage, April to May 1087)
- Status: full.
- When and where: on the way home from Myra, after passing Patara. Place: Perdikca, then Markiano and the sea past Culfus Trachiae [S9].
- Who was there: the Bari sailors; five sailors who had taken fragments; Disigius, a sailor who dreamed [S9].
- What happened, in order:
  1. The first night they rest at Caccavus. Then a north wind (Aquilo) and tides push them back as far as Patara, where Nicholas was born. They think the saint does not want to go unless he is returned [S9].
  2. After spending all their strength they reach about 24 miles and turn to Perdikca, exhausted. On landing they see the sea is calm [S9].
  3. They cannot start. They accuse each other of taking a piece of the relics. They bring out the Gospels so each sailor can swear he took nothing [S9].
  4. Five sailors say they have pieces. They return them. All then swear no one holds any more [S9].
  5. Nicephorus reads this as the saint's will that his relics never be divided. He notes that God spoke "through the inanimate elements", not by an angel or a dream [S9].
  6. After Mass, with the wind quiet, they sail on, to Markiano [S9].
  7. Past Culfus Trachiae, Disigius falls asleep and sees Nicholas. The sailors rejoice [S9].
- Details: the Gospels brought out; calm sea at Perdikca; north wind Aquilo; Mass before leaving [S9]. The dream words in S9's English: "Don't fear, for I am with you. At the end of twenty days we will all arrive together at the city of Bari." That is 18 words, within the 40-word limit. plain: Do not be afraid. I am with you. In twenty days we will all reach Bari together.
- Words spoken: Nicholas to Disigius, as above (Latin, Jones's English). The sailors' oath has no recorded words.
- Inner life: the sailors "began to think that the saint of God had never wished to go with them" [S9, paraphrase].
- What changed: the relics are whole again; the ship sails, and the crew has a promise of twenty days.
- Not in the sources: the names of the five sailors; what they took; how large the pieces were; whether they were punished. Also not given: the exact day of the dream. After Disigius, S9 tells of a lark that kissed the casket (a different sign; not in this card).
- Sources: S9. S8 does not tell Perdikca in the part I read (I read only to sec.22).

## SC-12 — Bari, 9 May 1087 (the harbor and the city)
- Status: full for the arrival and the quarrel. The young man's healing is thin (one sentence in S9, one in S8).
- When and where: 9 May 1087, the first evening watch [S8 sec.19]. The port of Saint George (four miles from the city), then the Bari harbor, the monastery of St Benedict, and the praetorium [S9; S8].
- Who was there: sailors; relatives in small boats; Bari clergy in vestments; Abbot Elias of St Benedict's; Archbishop Ursus (called Urso in S9); a crowd; the sick [S9; S8].
- What happened, in order:
  1. At St George's port they build a casket for the body [S9; S8 sec.14].
  2. Relatives meet the ships in small boats and run back to shout the news. The whole city rushes to the harbor; clergy in vestments come singing [S9; S8 sec.15].
  3. The sailors announce a pledge: to build a church worthy of him in the Catapan's court. Some townspeople agree. Others want him in the cathedral [S9; S8 sec.15-16].
  4. Abbot Elias comes aboard and asks to keep the body until the city agrees. The body goes to the altar of St Benedict. The sailors guard the gates [S9; S8 sec.16].
  5. Ursus comes back from Canosa. He wants the body taken to his palace. The sailors and townspeople resist. Both sides arm. In the fight, S9 says two youths die; S8 says two of the archbishop's men and one townsman [S9; S8 sec.17].
  6. The crowd carries the body singing "Kyrie eleison" to the praetorium and the church of St Eustratius. Its altar is torn down to build Nicholas's [S9; S8 sec.18].
  7. The sick come that night. S9 counts 47 "that night and on Monday"; S8 counts 47 "that night and the following morning" [S9; S8 sec.20].
- Details: the 47 included a "very noble Barian", a man withered on his left side, lunatics, a deaf and dumb man, cripples, blind, a Pisan with a withered arm and clubbed feet [S9]. The sailors dropped their arms before the body was carried in [S9].
- The young man: S9 says on Thursday Nicholas appeared in a vision to a venerable monk and told him to speak to anyone who despaired of healing. He said he would go away to Greece, but first they would see a great miracle. That same day a young man was healed who "had been possessed by a demon for five years and was deaf, dumb, and blind". S8 sec.21 puts the monk's vision on the fifth day, names the monk Mark of Celius, and gives the saint's words: "For by the will of God I am leaving the Roman world; whither I go, I go on a visit, but here I shall dwell forever." (28 words, translated by McGinley and Musurillo, 1980, facts-only.) plain: By God's will I am leaving the Roman world. Where I go, I go only as a visitor. Here I will stay forever. S8 says that day before sunrise a man tormented by an "evil and deaf and dumb spirit" was cured. S8 gives no five years or blindness.
- Words spoken, the sailors' pledge (S9, Jones): "we pledged ourselves that, together with you, we would erect a church worthy of him in the domanial court". plain: We promised that, together with you, we would build a church worthy of him in the Catapan's court.
- What changed: the body rests in Bari at St Eustratius's altar. Cures begin that night. The pledge becomes the basilica.
- Not in the sources: the weather, the crowd's number, the hour of landing beyond "first evening watch" [S8], any speech by Ursus, the young man's name, how he was brought, what he said, or what happened to the demon. Do not say the relics went "to the governor's hall"; the sources say first St Benedict's altar, then the praetorium and the church of St Eustratius.
- Conflicts: 47 cured (S8, S9) against Butler's 30 [S1 p.94]. The dead: two (S9) against three (S8). The five-year, deaf, dumb, blind young man appears only in S9.
- Sources: S9; S8 sec.14-21 (Fordham page).

## FN-1 — Roman Martyrology wording and the Roman calendar rank for 6 December
- Fact: The Roman Martyrology entry for 6 December, in the traditional English text, reads: "AT Myra, the metropolis of Lycia, the birthday of St. Nicholas, bishop and confessor, of whom it is related, among other miracles, that, while at a great distance from the emperor Constantine, he appeared to him in a vision and moved him to mercy so as to deter him from putting to death some persons who had implored his assistance." That is the officers' story only. Edition and year of this text are not stated on the site.
- Rank: in the General Roman Calendar (1969), 6 December "St Nicholas, Bishop" is an optional memorial. The Liturgy Office says: "If no rank is given the celebration is an Optional Memorial."
- Sources: https://roman-martyrology.brandt.id.au/martyrs/12-06.html (a hobby site, not official); https://liturgyoffice.org/Calendar/Universal/DecUC.shtml (Catholic Bishops' Conference of England and Wales). I did not reach the Vatican's 2004 Martyrologium Romanum text; the Latin and any 2004 wording are unchecked. The divinumofficium.com page blocked my fetch.

## FN-2 — When Pope Urban II placed the relics in the basilica
- Fact: Urban II laid the relics under the altar of the crypt in October 1089. The crypt was "completed by October 1089" and Urban "laid the relics of St. Nicholas beneath the crypt's altar" (St Nicholas Center, "Saint in Bari"). The University of Kent pilgrimage site says the crypt "was consecrated by Pope Urban II in 1089". Wikipedia's Synod of Melfi page says Elias invited Urban to Bari to consecrate the crypt.
- Not confirmed: the exact day. Web searches gave "1 October 1089" from tourist sites. I did not find a primary or scholarly source for that day. Write "October 1089" and not 1 October.
- S5 gives no date. The S5 phrase "magnificent basilica" is later than 1089: the crypt came first; the whole basilica was consecrated in 1197 (Wikipedia). Write "the crypt of the new basilica".
- Sources: https://www.stnicholascenter.org/who-is-st-nicholas/saint-in-bari ; https://pilgrimagestudies.ac.uk/node/416 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synod_of_Melfi_(1089). All secondary. No primary text (John the Archdeacon, Latin chronicles) read.

## FN-3 — When the Russian Church took up the 9 May feast
- Fact: The Orthodox Church in America says the Russian celebration of 9 May "was established soon after the year 1087". Patriarch Kirill (2017, Bari) said the Kiev Metropolitan Ephraim II set the date after Urban II sent him a fragment of the relics, and "We think it happened in 1091 or 1932" [sic; the text most likely means 1092, because Ephraim died in 1092]. So: about 1091 to 1092, not firmly dated.
- Conflict with S5: S5 names Theodore, envoy of Metropolitan John II of Kiev (1080-1089), as the source of the Kiev feast. Kirill names Ephraim II. Use "within a few years of 1087".
- Old calendar: Kirill says 9 May on the Julian calendar falls on 22 May in the Gregorian.
- Sources: https://www.oca.org/saints/lives/1988/05/09/101336-translation-of-the-relics-of-saint-nicholas-the-wonderworker-fro ; https://www.pravmir.com/patriarch-veneration-st-nicholas-unites-eastern-orthodox-roman-catholic-christians/ ; S5.

## Found but not assigned
- Nicephorus (S9) tells of a bird like a lark that landed on the casket at sea and "gently touched" it, then circled the ship. He says the sailors told him this themselves. It is a short, vivid sea-scene for the voyage.
