Here is the Church’s memory of the works God did through Saint Nicholas of Myra, the Wonderworker, in his life and at his tomb. The accounts come from the oldest tellers, and each one names its teller. Some stand in the Church’s books and prayers. The shrine at Bari keeps others, and later writers added more. A few belong to the Life of Saint Nicholas of Sion, a monk from the same coast. The list says so where it applies.

During Saint Nicholas’s life

Saint Nicholas saves three condemned men at Myra

The Praxis de stratelatis says that the governor Eustathius sentenced three innocent men of Myra to be beheaded. Saint Nicholas ran to the place of execution at Byrra and took the sword from the executioner. The writer of the old account adds a line from Proverbs: the righteous are bold as a lion. Then the saint freed the men and went to the governor. Eustathius blamed two other men. Saint Nicholas answered, “Eudoxius and Simonides did not do this. Silver and gold did.” The Praxis says the governor was to be paid more than 200 pounds of silver. Saint Nicholas forgave him when three officers of the emperor asked. Michael says that in this Saint Nicholas laid down his life for the men, as his Master did.

Status: the oldest story the Church tells of Saint Nicholas, in the Praxis de stratelatis, a Greek text written between about 400 and the sixth century. Michael the Archimandrite tells the rescue again in his ninth-century Life.

Source: Praxis de stratelatis, tr. Charles W. Jones; Michael the Archimandrite, Life, sec. 31.

Saint Nicholas saves three generals in dreams, Constantinople

The Praxis de stratelatis tells how the generals Nepotianus, Ursus, and Eupoleonis were falsely accused in Constantinople. They had been at Myra when Saint Nicholas saved the three men, and now the emperor Constantine ordered them beheaded. In the prison Nepotianus prayed with tears to the God of Saint Nicholas, and he asked the saint to pray for them from far away. That night Saint Nicholas appeared in dreams to Constantine and to the prefect Ablabius and told them to free the generals. The next day Constantine asked the generals if they knew anyone named Nicholas, and they cried out, “Lord God of Saint Nicholas, hear us.” Constantine freed them and said they owed their lives to Saint Nicholas, whom they had called on. He sent them to Myra to thank the saint. There they gave alms to the poor with tears for many years, praising the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit.

Status: tradition kept in the Roman Martyrology, the Church’s official list of saints and feast days. Its older English text says: “Among other miracles it is told of him that while he was far from the emperor Constantine, he appeared to him in a vision and moved him to mercy, so that Constantine did not put to death some people who had begged for his help.”

The Praxis says the prefect Ablabius was promised 1,700 pounds of gold to destroy the generals. Eustratios of Constantinople quotes the story after 583, and a sermon under the name of Saint Proclus retells it.

Source: Praxis de stratelatis, tr. Jones; Encomium on Saint Nicholas, under the name of Saint Proclus; Roger Pearse, “Literary Development of the Life of St Nicholas”; Roman Martyrology, 6 December; Cioffari, “Nicholas of Patara, Bishop of Myra”.

Saint Nicholas secretly gives gold for three sisters’ weddings

Michael the Archimandrite tells of a poor man who lived next door to Saint Nicholas. The man was ready to put his three daughters in a brothel, because no one would marry them. Michael says the Lord who loves mankind sent Saint Nicholas to him like an angel. At night Saint Nicholas threw a bag of gold through the man’s window. In the morning the father wept and thanked God, because he believed the gift had come from God. Saint Nicholas did it again for the second daughter, and the father asked God to show him the giver. When Saint Nicholas came with the third bag, the father ran after him and recognized him. The father fell at his feet and said that the Lord had saved him and his daughters through this man. Saint Nicholas lifted him up and made him swear to tell no one for the rest of his life.

Status: told in the ninth-century Life by Michael the Archimandrite. One Orthodox history lists it among the principal miracles that tradition has always told of him. This story also lies behind Saint Nicholas’s place as the giver of gifts.

Source: Michael the Archimandrite, Life, secs. 9–18; Cioffari, “Nicholas of Patara”.

At Myra, a voice names Saint Nicholas as bishop

Michael tells how the bishops of nearby cities prayed for a new bishop of Myra. The Lord told one of them, “Stand at the church door at night. Take the man who comes in first and quietly, and make him bishop. His name is Nicholas.” At dawn Saint Nicholas came alone to enter the church, and Michael says God sent him. The bishop asked his name. Saint Nicholas answered, “Nicholas, a sinner, a servant of your Holiness, master.” The bishops gave glory to God and showed him to the crowd as the shepherd God had sent them, and the people seated him on the bishop’s throne.

Status: told in the ninth-century Life by Michael the Archimandrite. Gratian’s Decretum is an old book of Church law, and it records a case like this one.

Source: Michael the Archimandrite, Life, secs. 21–24; Cioffari, “Nicholas of Patara”.

Saint Nicholas feeds Myra in a famine, Andriake

Michael tells how grain ran short in Lycia and ships from Alexandria came into the harbor at Andriake. Saint Nicholas went down from Myra and asked each captain to unload a hundred measures of grain. The captains said the grain belonged to the state. Saint Nicholas promised to answer to the treasury official in Constantinople for their loss. Michael says the captains found their cargo unchanged at Constantinople, and everyone there praised God, who always gives grace to those who love him. At Myra the people praised God as they received the grain, and it fed the city for two years. Michael says they enjoyed God’s kindness through the prayers of his servant, Saint Nicholas.

Status: told in the ninth-century Life by Michael the Archimandrite. Charles Jones says the story was told by the sixth century. In the Golden Legend of 1275 the sailors find the full amount at Alexandria.

Source: Michael the Archimandrite, Life, secs. 37–39; Jacobus de Voragine, The Golden Legend; Cioffari.

Saint Nicholas leaves gold on a grain ship

A sermon under the name of Saint Andrew of Crete tells the famine story differently. It says Saint Nicholas persuaded some crews to come into the harbor although they had not meant to stop there. Saint Nicholas was seen standing among the sailors like a sailor himself, and when he vanished from the ship he left three gold coins behind. In the tenth century Saint Symeon Metaphrastes wrote that Saint Nicholas appeared at night to a seaman who dealt in grain. Saint Nicholas put three measures of gold in the man’s hand as a pledge and told him to sell the grain at Myra.

Status: told in a sermon of the early eighth century under the name of Saint Andrew of Crete, and again by Saint Symeon Metaphrastes in the tenth century. Michael does not mention the gold.

Source: Encomium, under the name of Saint Andrew of Crete; Symeon Metaphrastes, selections.

Sailors call on Saint Nicholas in a storm

Michael tells how sailors in a storm called to Saint Nicholas by name. By God’s order he appeared to them and said, “You called me, and I am here to help you.” The sailors saw him working with them at the ropes and poles, and with God’s help he brought the ship to a calm anchorage. On shore they found him in the church among the other clergy, and they recognized him although they had never seen him before. They fell at his feet with thank-offerings. Saint Nicholas knew their secret sins and told them to turn their hearts to God and give up injustice, greed, and sexual sin.

Status: told in the ninth-century Life by Michael the Archimandrite. This story is the root of Saint Nicholas’s care for sailors. A sermon of the early eighth century carries the name of Saint Andrew of Crete. It praises him for turning “a hurricane into a breeze and a storm into a calm.”

Source: Michael the Archimandrite, Life, secs. 34–36; Encomium, under the name of Saint Andrew of Crete; Cioffari.

Saint Nicholas destroys the temple of Artemis, Myra

Michael says that Saint Nicholas went through his district and pulled down the temples of idols. He then overthrew the temple of Artemis at Myra and dug up its foundations. That temple was taller and richer in ornament than any other building. Michael says the power of God drove the demons out through his servant, and they cried out that he was harming them. The historian Gerardo Cioffari says that inscriptions from Myra support the size and fame of the temple.

Status: told in the ninth-century Life by Michael the Archimandrite.

Source: Michael the Archimandrite, Life, secs. 28–29; Cioffari.

Heretics who meet Saint Nicholas give up false beliefs

Michael heard an old tradition about Saint Nicholas from a friend of his, a devout monk. The monk said Saint Nicholas looked worthy of honor and like an angel, and a sweet smell came from him. Those who were with him became better just by seeing him. A heretic is a person who holds a belief the Church rejects. If a heretic met Saint Nicholas as he passed by, the heretic at once lost the disease of false belief and accepted the faith.

Status: told in the ninth-century Life by Michael the Archimandrite. Michael says it came to him by word of mouth from the monk.

Source: Michael the Archimandrite, Life, sec. 40.

After Saint Nicholas’s death

Angels come for Saint Nicholas when he dies, Myra

Saint Nicholas reposed in the Lord. Michael says he went to his eternal rest, rejoicing with choirs of angels. Michael adds that he never stops praying for those who call on him in faith. The Golden Legend (1275) says that Saint Nicholas asked God to send his angels. He bowed his head, saw them come, and knew that he would die. He began Psalm 30 and prayed, “Lord, into your hands I give my spirit.” The company of heaven sang with great melody as he died.

Status: told by Michael the Archimandrite in the ninth century and in the Golden Legend of Blessed Jacobus de Voragine in 1275. The Golden Legend dates his death to the year 343.

Source: Michael the Archimandrite, Life, sec. 41; Jacobus de Voragine, The Golden Legend.

Perfume and oil flow from the tomb at Myra

Michael says that Saint Nicholas was buried in the local church and at once his body gave off a sweet-smelling perfume. Michael adds that it turned away every harmful power, to the glory of Christ who had glorified him. The Golden Legend (1275) says that when he was buried in a marble tomb, a spring of holy oil flowed from his head to his feet. It says that the oil still came from his body and healed many sick people.

Status: told by Michael the Archimandrite in the ninth century and in the Golden Legend in 1275. The oil flowed at Myra for centuries and later at Bari.

Source: Michael the Archimandrite, Life, sec. 41; Jacobus de Voragine, The Golden Legend.

Oil stops when a bishop is driven out

The Golden Legend (1275) adds a story about the oil at the tomb. A later bishop was driven from his office, and the oil stopped. It flowed again when he came back.

Status: told in the Golden Legend of Blessed Jacobus de Voragine in 1275.

Source: Jacobus de Voragine, The Golden Legend.

A demon’s flask is thrown into the sea

Michael tells of pilgrims who set out by ship for Myra after Saint Nicholas died. Saint Nicholas had once driven a demon from the temple of Artemis. Now the demon took the shape of a woman and gave them a flask of something that looked like oil. It asked them to pour the liquid on the lamps at the saint’s shrine. On the first night Saint Nicholas appeared to one of the men and said, “Get up at dawn and throw the foreign flask you carry into the deep sea.” When the man threw it in, flame and dark smoke rose from the water. The swell drove the ship about until Saint Nicholas came to their help and saved them. When they were safe, they gave thanks to God and to Saint Nicholas. They thanked God as the maker and protector of his creation, and the saint as the one through whom God’s blessings had come.

Status: told in the ninth-century Life by Michael the Archimandrite.

Source: Michael the Archimandrite, Life, secs. 44–48.

A glass vial does not break, Myra, 1087

In April 1087 men from Bari came to Myra to take the holy relics of Saint Nicholas. Nicephorus of Bari tells that a priest among them set a glass vial of the saint’s holy oil on a column. It fell on the marble floor with a loud clang and did not break. The Bari men praised God, and they took it as a sign that God and his saint were willing for him to go with them. The Greek account names the priest Lupus.

Status: recorded by Nicephorus of Bari in 1087, soon after the holy relics reached the city, and in the Greek account of the translation.

Source: Nicephorus of Bari, Translation of St Nicholas, tr. Jones; Greek Anonymous account, tr. McGinley and Musurillo.

Saint Nicholas threatens to leave Myra in a vision

Nicephorus of Bari tells that a young man named Matthew drew his sword on one of the monks and demanded to be shown the tomb. A gentler monk then told the Bari men of a vision from a year before. Saint Nicholas had appeared to three people of Myra. The townspeople had fled to a mountain twelve stades away, about a mile and a third. Saint Nicholas told the three to call the townspeople back, or he would move elsewhere.

Status: recorded by Nicephorus of Bari in 1087 and in the Greek account of the translation.

Source: Nicephorus of Bari, Translation of St Nicholas, tr. Jones; Greek Anonymous account of the Translation, tr. McGinley and Musurillo.

The coffin is full of holy oil, Myra, 1087

Nicephorus of Bari tells that Matthew put away his sword, took an iron mallet, and broke open the tomb at Myra. The coffin was full of the holy oil up to the navel of the body. A scent rose from it, and the men felt that they stood in God’s paradise. The scent reached the men at the ships, nearly three miles away. The Greek account adds that the holy relics glowed like coals of fire and that Matthew kissed them. Then the men carried the holy relics to the ships, singing praises to God.

Status: recorded by Nicephorus of Bari in 1087, soon after the holy relics reached the city, and in the Greek account of the translation.

Source: Nicephorus of Bari, Translation of St Nicholas, tr. Jones; Greek Anonymous account, tr. McGinley and Musurillo.

The ships sail on once stolen relics are returned

Nicephorus of Bari tells that on the voyage home a north wind blew against the Bari ships all the way to Patara. The sailors began to think that the saint did not wish to go with them. At Perdikca the sea was as calm as they could wish, but they had no strength left to set out again. The sailors accused one another of taking pieces of the body, and five of them gave back the pieces they had taken. Every man swore on the Gospels that he held no more pieces, and then the priests said Mass. Then the winds fell and the swell went down, and they sailed on under full sail with joy.

Status: recorded by Nicephorus of Bari in 1087. He says God himself taught them that the saint’s body was never to be divided. God taught them through the wind and the sea, and not through an angel or a dream.

Source: Nicephorus of Bari, Translation of St Nicholas, tr. Jones.

A sailor dreams of Saint Nicholas’s promise at sea

Nicephorus of Bari tells that a sailor named Disigius fell asleep after the ships passed Culfus Trachiae. He saw Saint Nicholas in a dream. Saint Nicholas said, “Do not be afraid, for I am with you. In twenty days we will all arrive together at the city of Bari.” The sailors rejoiced.

Status: recorded by Nicephorus of Bari in 1087.

Source: Nicephorus of Bari, Translation of St Nicholas, tr. Jones.

A bird touches the casket at sea, 1087

Nicephorus of Bari tells of something that happened on the voyage home. A bird like a lark landed on the casket that held the body of Saint Nicholas and gently touched it, singing softly. Then it circled the ship. Nicephorus praises God, who teaches even the birds to honor his saint. He says the sailors told him this themselves.

Status: recorded by Nicephorus of Bari in 1087 from the sailors’ own report.

Source: Nicephorus of Bari, Translation of St Nicholas, tr. Jones.

Forty-seven people are cured at Bari, May 1087

Nicephorus of Bari and the Greek account say that God healed forty-seven sick people at the body of Saint Nicholas after it reached Bari on 9 May 1087. The sick came from all parts of the city and lay down beside the body in deep devotion. Nicephorus says the cures came that night and the next day, and the Greek account says that night and the next morning. The sick included a nobleman of Bari, a man withered on his left side, and a deaf and mute man. Nicephorus also lists lame people, blind people, and a man from Pisa with a withered arm and club feet. The Greek account adds that many who were well in body received conversion and the salvation of their souls. Alban Butler gives the number cured on the first day as thirty.

Status: recorded by Nicephorus of Bari in 1087, soon after the holy relics reached the city, and in the Greek account of the translation.

Source: Nicephorus of Bari; Greek Anonymous account; Alban Butler, Lives of the Saints, 6 Dec. (1866 ed.), vol. 12, pp. 91–94.

Saint Nicholas appears to a monk at Bari, 1087

Nicephorus of Bari tells of a vision that came to a monk at Bari on a Thursday after the body arrived. Saint Nicholas told the monk to speak to anyone who had lost hope of healing. He said that by God’s will he would go away to Greece, but first they would see a great miracle. The Greek account puts the vision on the fifth day and names the monk Mark. In it Saint Nicholas says, “By God’s will I am leaving the Roman world. Where I go, I go only as a visitor. Here I will stay forever.”

Status: recorded by Nicephorus of Bari in 1087 and in the Greek account of the translation.

Source: Nicephorus of Bari, Translation of St Nicholas, tr. Jones; Greek Anonymous account, tr. McGinley and Musurillo.

A possessed young man is healed at Bari

Nicephorus of Bari says that God made a young man whole at Bari on the same Thursday. A demon had held him for five years, and he was deaf, mute, and blind. The Greek account tells of a man cured before sunrise on the day of the monk’s vision. It says an evil, deaf, and mute spirit had tormented him. It does not mention the five years or the blindness.

Status: recorded by Nicephorus of Bari in 1087 and in the Greek account of the translation.

Source: Nicephorus of Bari, Translation of St Nicholas, tr. Jones; Greek Anonymous account, tr. McGinley and Musurillo.

The manna of Bari flows from the tomb

The shrine at Bari records a fragrant liquid that comes from the tomb of Saint Nicholas, and the people call it manna. The St Nicholas Center says that the rector of the basilica collects it at the May feast. Butler writes that a law of the emperor Manuel mentions a fragrant oily matter at Bari. Balsamon reports that law. Orthodox writers say the manna has kept flowing since the holy relics were examined and laid in a new tomb in the crypt in 1953–1957.

Status: recorded by the shrine at Bari and by Orthodox writers of today.

Source: St Nicholas Center, “Saint in Bari”; Alban Butler, Lives of the Saints, 6 Dec. (1866 ed.), vol. 12, pp. 91–94; “History of the Veneration of Saint Nicholas of Myra,” Mystagogy Resource Center.

Stories Told of Saint Nicholas in Later Centuries

In the ninth and tenth centuries the Lives of Saint Nicholas of Myra and Saint Nicholas of Sion were joined into one. Saint Nicholas of Sion was an abbot near Myra, and he died in the sixth century. The stories below come from later tellers, and a few come from the abbot’s Life. Each one names its teller.

The baby Saint Nicholas nurses once on fast days

Michael says that Saint Nicholas nursed on most days like any baby. On Wednesdays and Fridays he took milk only once, at the set hour. Michael says the Lord showed in this what kind of man Saint Nicholas would be.

Status: told in the ninth-century Life by Michael the Archimandrite.

Source: Michael the Archimandrite, Life, sec. 5; Cioffari.

The storm calmed on the pilgrimage to Jerusalem

The Vita Compilata tells of a voyage to Jerusalem. It is a ninth- or tenth-century book that joins the Lives of two saints named Nicholas. The pilgrim found a ship from Ascalon at Myra, with a captain named Mena. A favorable wind blew, and he came to Jerusalem in a few days. In the tenth century Saint Symeon Metaphrastes added to this voyage the story of a storm calmed at sea.

Status: told in the Vita Compilata and by Saint Symeon Metaphrastes in the tenth century. The voyage belongs to the Life of Saint Nicholas of Sion.

Source: Vita Compilata, sec. 21; Pearse, “Literary Development”.

A sailor named Ammonios is raised from the dead

Saint Symeon Metaphrastes wrote in the tenth century that a sailor named Ammonios was raised from the dead.

Status: told by Saint Symeon Metaphrastes in the tenth century. Like the calmed storm, it comes from the Life of Saint Nicholas of Sion.

Source: Pearse, “Literary Development”.

Saint Nicholas prays all night for tax relief, Constantinople

The Praxis de tributo tells how Saint Nicholas went to Constantinople to win relief from a tax. He prayed all night at the church of the Mother of God at Blachernae.

Status: told in the Praxis de tributo, a Greek text in circulation by the end of the seventh or the eighth century. Its language points to Saint Nicholas of Sion. Our sources give no more of the story.

Source: Cioffari, “Nicholas of Patara”.

Saint Nicholas strikes the heretic Arius at Nicaea

The Council of Nicaea in 325 was a great meeting of bishops that the emperor Constantine called. The story tells that Saint Nicholas lost his temper there and struck a heretic. Later tellers name the man as Arius. The St. Nicholas Center tells the later tradition that the bishops stripped Saint Nicholas of his robes and put him in chains. In the night Christ and the Most Holy Mother of God came to his cell and asked why he was there. “Because of my love for you,” he answered. Christ gave him the Book of the Gospels. The Mother of God gave him a bishop’s vestment, so that he was dressed as a bishop again. The historian Gerardo Cioffari writes that the slap “was well in tone with Nicholas’ character.”

Status: told from the late Middle Ages on. The St Nicholas Center says the story is more than five hundred years old.

Source: Cioffari, “Nicholas of Patara”; St Nicholas Center, “Bishop Nicholas Loses His Cool”.

A Note on the Sources

These accounts come to us through the Lives, the hymns, and the records of the shrine at Bari. Our sources record no formal Church inquiry into them. Scholars place the Praxis de stratelatis anywhere between about 400 and the sixth century. Cioffari holds that Nepotianus and Ablabius were real men, and he calls the dream visits in the Praxis a common device in saints’ Lives. He reports three very old versions of the story of the gold, and they differ. The oldest has two girls and a young Saint Nicholas who takes money from his living parents for them, and another has four girls. He thinks the election of a layman is probably true, and he calls the famine story too much like other stories.

Michael’s famine chapters are missing from many manuscripts. Anrich questioned whether Saint Andrew of Crete wrote the sermon under his name. Cioffari thinks the story of the sailors fits Saint Nicholas of Sion better, and he calls the baby’s fasting a common kind of story in monks’ Lives. Sources give the year of death as 342, 343, 345, or 352.

For 1087, Nicephorus names Turks as the threat and the Greek account names Persians. Nicephorus is thought to have written within about a week of the body’s arrival, but he does not say he was there. The Greek account survives in a manuscript of the thirteenth century. Only Nicephorus gives the five years and the blindness of the young man healed at Bari. None of our sources reports an outside study of the oil or the manna.

Cioffari notes that the church of the Mother of God at Blachernae, where that story has Saint Nicholas pray, was built about 450. Michael’s Life and the Golden Legend do not tell the story of the slap, and Cioffari calls it a popular development. No writer who was at the council names Saint Nicholas, and the earliest sign of him there is a list of bishops from about 515. Scholars dispute how to read that list.
