## 1. What survives

- Writings by Nicholas: none found. No letter, sermon, rule, or recorded saying that scholars accept as his. [S10, S6, S1]
- Catholic Encyclopedia: "scarcely anything historically certain about him except that he was Bishop of Myra in the fourth century." [T-S6]
- Nothing written in his lifetime survives (biography Note on the Sources; notes.md S2, S3, S10).
- What survives about him is writing by others: Praxis de stratelatis (oldest; date disputed, about 400 to 6th c.), Eustratios (after 583), Michael the Archimandrite's Life (814–842), Metaphrastes (10th c.), the Bari translation accounts (1087). Dates and rights: see notes.md sources S2, S3, S7–S9, S11. [S11, T-S18]
- Original Greek texts: Anrich 1913/1917 is the critical edition of the Greek texts (title and date from Cioffari's bibliography). Not read. [T-S17]
- Free English translations: Michael (Sanidopoulos site), Proclus, Andrew of Crete, Vita Compilata, Metaphrastes selections (St Nicholas Center). See notes.md.

## 2. The big ideas

- None found. No source I read gives a doctrinal statement that Nicholas himself wrote or spoke in a way that has an early witness.
- Doctrinal speeches in later Lives (the Trinity, Arius) are put into his mouth by Michael and later writers. Cioffari calls the Trinity speech a "popular development" and says "edifying episodes". [T-S17]
- Michael's Life names Arius and Sabellius as errors Nicholas opposed but does not mention Nicaea (notes.md Other, S2 sec.25–26). That is a teaching of Michael, not evidence of Nicholas's own teaching.

## 3. The everyday teaching

- None found as spoken teaching.
- The Church's reading of his life as teaching is in the troparion (Q6) and in the Greek homily on Thursday: "Faith, meekness, temperance, humility, material poverty are the characteristic features of the apostolic life." [T-S4; homily, translator Sanidopoulos; this is a modern preacher's reading, not Nicholas's words]

## 4. A special devotion or perspective

What the Church took from his example. Each item: what the source says. Mark = documented (a source shows the link) or claimed.

- Hidden giving. The dowry-gold story is the root of the gift tradition. In Michael (c.820–840) he makes the father swear silence (notes.md S2 sec.17). Metaphrastes calls it a "unique deed" known to the whole people (via S5, P.G. 116, 328A). Documented as a story; the event itself is not provable. [S5, S10]
- Defender of the condemned. The oldest story (Praxis) is about saving the innocent. The kontakion for 6 December says this in a hymn: "saving the innocent from death." [T-S1; Praxis in S3, S11]. Documented link between oldest story and hymn text: the hymn names the act; no source I read says the hymn writer used the Praxis. I mark that connection my own reading.
- Humility. The Greek tradition keeps the word "sinner" in his mouth in three early stories (notes.md S2, S3). The troparion says "Through humility, you attained the heights; and through poverty, riches." [T-S1] Documented in both places; the link between them is my reading.
- Model bishop, successor of the Apostles. Greek homily: he is celebrated on Thursday "because he is the authentic successor of the Holy Apostles." [T-S4] Modern preacher's claim; I found no early source giving this as the reason for the Thursday slot.
- Patron of sea travelers, merchants, children, others. CE: "mariners, merchants, bakers, travellers, children". [T-S6] Roots: sailors' story (Michael sec.34–36; Cioffari says it fits Nicholas of Sion better but is the root of the sea patronage, notes.md S10); pilgrims stopping at Myra on the way to the Holy Land (S5).

## 5. Influence during life

- Documented by an early source: none beyond the Praxis stories (rescue at Myra; the three officers). The Praxis is the best-attested story but is dated 5th–6th c. and the dating is disputed. [S3, S11]
- Eustratios of Constantinople (after 583) cites the three-generals miracle "as evidence that souls may work independent from the body" (Wikipedia, tertiary; Eustratios's Greek text not read); the same source says he credits a lost Life. [T-S20]. Cioffari also names Eustratios as the first hint of the three-innocents story. [T-S17]
- Nicaea 325: see Q8. Not shown by an early source.
- No pupils, converts, order, or debate with a source close to his time. Not found.

## 6. Influence after death (time order)

Marks: D = documented (a source shows the link), C = claimed.

Early cult (5th–8th c.)
- Fifth-century records show pilgrimage to his grave: C. This is Sanidopoulos's statement, "Fifth century records indicate..." It names no records. [S5]
- Life of Nicholas of Sion (c.564, about 250 years after Nicholas of Myra by Wikipedia's count) briefly names a visit by Nicholas of Sion to Nicholas's tomb. Wikipedia credits Jeremy Seal that a tomb that could be visited is almost the only firm proof that Nicholas was real. D for tomb in Myra by the 6th c., but the source is tertiary. [T-S20]
- Justinian (527–565) built a church to him at Blachernae, Constantinople. Sanidopoulos says so; CE says "As early as the sixth century Emperor Justinian I built a church in his honour." Butler dates the church "about 430", which cannot be Justinian. Conflict noted in notes.md. D by two sources, no primary text read. [S5, T-S6, S1]
- Wikipedia says a church was built over his bishop's church at Myra on the orders of Theodosius II and his remains moved to a sarcophagus there. Not checked elsewhere. C. [T-S20]
- Sinai icon, 7th–8th c.; Santa Maria Antiqua fresco, Rome, early to mid 8th c.: art. Captions only (notes.md S10 and Methodius page). D for the objects; I did not see the objects' dating sources.
- Andrew of Crete's homily, early 8th c., "at his grave site": "pillar and support of the Church" (P.G. 97, 1191–1206). [S5]. The Sanidopoulos page says it is the oldest encomium that survives. Andrew's authorship is doubted by Anrich per the biography's Note on the Sources.

Hymns and Thursday (East)
- Troparion, tone 4, 6 December (OCA English): "The truth of things revealed you to your flock as a rule of faith, / a model of meekness, and a teacher of temperance. / Through humility, you attained the heights; / and through poverty, riches. / O Father and Hierarch Nicholas, intercede with Christ God that our souls may be saved." [T-S1]
  - Greek wording: not reached. A search-result summary quoted "Κανόνα πίστεως καί εἰκόνα πραότητος, ἐγκρατείας διδάσκαλον" but I did not fetch it from a page; do not use as a quotation until checked.
  - Other English forms: Greek Orthodox "a canon of faith, an icon of meekness, and a teacher of abstinence"; Byzantine Catholic "a model of faith"; Antiochian "a measure of faith". [T-S3]
- Kontakion, tone 3 (OCA English): "You were shown forth in Myra as an officiant of the sacred rites, for you fulfilled the Gospel of Christ ... by laying down your life for your people, and saving the innocent from death. Therefore, you were sanctified as a great initiate of God's grace." [T-S1] (OCA lays out the lines with slashes; I joined them.)
- Who wrote the hymns: not found. Sanidopoulos says the liturgical services were "considerably enriched" by Theodore Studite (d. 826) and Patriarchs Nikephoros (d. 829) and Methodios (d. 847). C. No text named. [S5]
- Thursday in the weekly cycle: ROCOR talk: "The fifth day – Thursday – is dedicated to the holy Apostles and also to Saint Nicholas. The fact that Saint Nicholas has been given a place in the weekly cycle indicates that great veneration that the Church accords him." [T-S5] The same fact in T-S4: hymns for him on "most every Thursday," in the Octoechos. Reason and date of this arrangement: not found. D for the practice now; origin not documented.
- OCA entry on 9 May: "Each week on Thursday, the Russian Orthodox Church honors his memory in particular." [T-S2]
- Russia: Christianity and veneration said to arrive together (OCA 9 May: "brought from Greece simultaneously with the acceptance of Christianity"); churches and monasteries, children named Nicholas, wonderworking icons of Mozhaisk, Zaraisk, Volokolamsk, Ugreshsk, Ratny; "There was no house or temple in the Russian land in which there was not an icon of Saint Nicholas the Wonderworker." [T-S2] D as a modern Orthodox statement; no early source.
- Russian Life and Translation account "by a contemporary" of 1087; the envoy Theodore of Metropolitan John II of Kiev wrote the Skazanie (Narration) and "became instrumental" in the 9 May feast in Kiev province. [S5, T-S2] The text itself not read.
- Patriarch Kirill, 22 May 2017: the Kiev metropolitan (Ephraim II, d. 1092 by Kirill) set the date of the 9 May commemoration after Pope Urban II sent a relic fragment; Kirill: "We think it happened in 1091 or 1932" (as printed; the second year looks like a typing error). C: a modern church leader's reasoning. [T-S9] Conflict with Sanidopoulos (envoy Theodore of John II). Both are secondary.
- Nikodemos of the Holy Mountain wrote a service for the Translation (notes.md S18). Greek lands keep the Translation on varied days. [S18]
- Russian 29 July "Nativity of St Nicholas," revived 2004 under Alexis II. [S17]

Bari and the 9 May feast (West and East)
- 9 May 1087: relics reach Bari. Documented by Nicephorus within about a week and the 13th-c. Greek account. [S8, S9, notes.md]
- OCA (9 May): "A year afterwards, a church was built in the name of Saint Nicholas and consecrated by Pope Urban II." Other sources: the crypt finished by October 1089, Urban laid the relics under the altar. [T-S2, T-S8, T-S11]. The OCA "a year afterwards" conflicts with 1089 in two other sources; use the 1089 date.
- Synod of Melfi, 10–15 Sept 1089: the Benedictine Elias invited Urban II to Bari to consecrate the crypt and make him archbishop. Wikipedia, tertiary. D for the invitation, with sources named on that page (Loud 2007; Vernon 2023). [T-S12]
- First Crusade pilgrims visited the relics. Kent's page: "The relics were visited by a number of those embarking on the First Crusade." [T-S11]
- Hayes 2016 (abstract): the cult served Bari's Norman rulers and the city's economy; "economic developments, church politics and the demands of the First Crusade merged to render Nicholas an ideal patron for the city." Argues earlier cult existed in Normandy and in Byzantine southern Italy. [T-S13] Article not read.
- OCA: at first the 9 May feast was kept only in Bari; "It was not adopted in the other lands of the Christian East and West" at first; the Greek Church did not establish it, since they "regarded the loss of the relics of Saint Nicholas as a sad event." Russia established it "soon after the year 1087." [T-S2] D as OCA statement; no primary text.
- Date today: Catholics keep 9 May (Gregorian); Russian Orthodox keep 9 May Old Style, which falls on 22 May in the Gregorian calendar. [T-S9]
- Manna: the Bari festival ends with the extraction of the manna by the rector; painted manna bottles became a local art of Apulia. [T-S8]
- Basilica: "a particularly fine example of Romanesque architecture and served as a prototype for many other churches and cathedrals." [T-S8] Claim, no source named.
- Orthodox chapel in the crypt, 1966; Dominican community since 1951. [T-S8]
- Russian Orthodox church of St Nicholas at Bari, "built at the beginning of the past century to welcome pilgrims" (Benedict XVI, 2009). [T-S10] D (Vatican text).

Spread of churches (West)
- Sanidopoulos: first Western church in his honor in the Lateran area of Rome built by Pope Nicholas I (858–867); "From that time on, hundreds of churches". C: no source for the Rome church named. [S5]
- CE: "In Italy his cult seems to have begun with the translation of his relics to Bari, but in Germany it began already under Otto II, probably because his wife Theophano was a Grecian." Bishop Reginald of Eichstaedt (d. 991) wrote a metric Vita. Also: "his name occurs in the liturgy ascribed to St. Chrysostom." CE says so as fact; the CE gives no text. [T-S6] I did not check the Chrysostom liturgy text.
- Patronage list, CE 1911: Greece, Russia, Naples, Sicily, Lorraine, Liège, Campen, Corfu, Freiburg, Moscow; mariners, merchants, bakers, travellers, children. [T-S6]
- Wikipedia lists far more (Aberdeen, Galway, Liverpool, Amsterdam, toymakers, pawnbrokers, archers, brewers). Tertiary, no source per item. [T-S20]

Gift-giving tradition
- Origin story: the dowry gold (Michael; Metaphrastes P.G. 116, 328A). D. [S2, S5]
- 11th–12th c. West: "the saint's image changed ... from a rather severe figure to the compassionate children's friend, giving gifts on St. Nicholas Day." As early as 1163 a high feast day at Janskerk, Utrecht. French nuns left gifts outside doors of children in need in the 12th c. [T-S7] Cites Wouters 2008, Meisen 1981, Seal 2005 for some points. The nuns claim has no named source on the page. C.
- 15th c. Swiss writer Hospinian (as quoted by Seal): parents on the vigil of St Nicholas "convey secretly presents ... to their little sons and daughters who were taught to believe that they owed them to the kindness of St Nicholas." He links this to the three daughters story. [T-S7] D as a witness to the custom; the 15th-c. date and the quote come from the St Nicholas Center page quoting Seal, not read in Hospinian.
- 16th c. Naogeorgus: a German verse describing hidden gifts on the eve. [T-S7]
- CE 1911: "In Germany, Switzerland, and the Netherlands, they have the custom of making him the secret purveyor of gifts to children on 6 December"; in the US he "has become identified with Santa Claus who distributes gifts to children on Christmas eve." [T-S6]
- Pearse: Santa Claus derives "at some remove" from the medieval legends. [T-S18]
- Hageman's review of Jones (1979): Jones traces the legend; from Bari Nicholas "legends, rituals, dramas, and dedications spread to just about every country in western Europe"; Reformation halted public cult but folk gift-giving continued in England, parts of Germany, Low Countries; Jones holds the New York cult was a late 18th–early 19th c. invention "fostered largely by Washington Irving"; Hageman disputes that. [T-S14] Jones's book itself not read (copyrighted 1978).
- Boy bishops: Wikipedia, "In Germany and Poland, boys have traditionally dressed as bishops and begged alms for the poor." [T-S20 Saint Nicholas Day page]. Origin not checked.
- Chimneys: 13th-c. art shows gifts by chimney, said the St Nicholas Center page. Claim. [T-S7]
- Dutch Sinterklaas and Santa Claus: Wikipedia and the Christophers column say Dutch settlers in New York and writers such as Irving and Clement Clarke Moore helped; both tertiary. [T-S20, T-S21] Jones disputes the earlier Dutch carry-over (T-S14).
- In sum: the story as origin of the gifts, documented by the (late) texts; the step from story to a Dec 6 custom for children, documented from the 12th–15th c. by the St Nicholas Center page; link to Santa Claus, documented only at a distance (Pearse, CE, Jones via Hageman).

Art (short)
- Dowry scene "one of the most popular scenes in Christian devotional art" (Wikipedia). [T-S20] Tertiary.
- Icons show him with an omophorion and a Gospel book in the East (Wikipedia infobox; from the page's data). [T-S20]
- Bari image "combines Eastern and Western images." [T-S8]

## 7. The Church's view today

- Catholic calendar: 6 December, "St Nicholas, Bishop." On the Liturgy Office list, an entry with no rank is an Optional Memorial. [T-S15] The Roman Martyrology says of him: "of whom it is related, among other miracles, that, while at a great distance from the emperor Constantine, he appeared to him in a vision and moved him to mercy so as to deter him from putting to death some persons who had implored his assistance." [T-S16; old translation; the current Latin was not checked]
- Orthodox: Thursday and 6 December, 9 May, and (Russian) 29 July. See Q6. OCA 9 May: "Both the East and the West know of this great Wonderworker." [T-S2] (the OCA quotes an old Russian Life)
- Orthodox view of rank: Sanidopoulos says he is the most venerated saint after the Mother of God and John the Baptist. [S5] A ROCOR talk gives the Thursday slot as a sign of "great veneration." [T-S5]
- Benedict XVI, 1 March 2009 (message): "The Russian people have never faltered in their love for this great Saint who has always sustained them in moments of joy and difficulty." Hope that Bari continues as "a natural bridge to the East." [T-S10]
- Kirill, 22 May 2017: "We're divided by the calendar reform but still are not divided by the date on which we venerate St Nicholas and the delivery of the relics from Myra in Lycia to Bari." Veneration "unites" the two Churches (headline). A relic fragment was in Moscow 22 May to 12 July 2017 and in St Petersburg until 28 July. [T-S9]
- Pope Francis: invited Middle East church leaders to Bari in 2018 to pray for peace; Bari chosen as "window into the East" and home of the relics of Nicholas, "venerated by the Western and Eastern Christians alike." His letter (24 Nov 20xx; the page text is cut off at the year; it says 50th anniversary of the Pontifical Basilica) praises the Basilica's "specific vocation intended to inspire the journey of Christian unity." [T-S8; I did not read the Vatican original]
- Catechism of the Catholic Church: not found. I searched the web twice and found no paragraph that names Nicholas. I did not scan the full text. Do not say the Catechism cites him.
- John Paul II, 7 June 1998: visit to the parish of San Nicola di Bari, Rome. I read the homily; the passages I found only greet the parish and do not teach about Nicholas. Not useful. [vatican.va, grep]

## 8. Doubtful and disputed

Nicaea and Arius: early sources
- No early source in the record. Nicholas is not named by people who were at the council: "he is never mentioned in any writings by people who were at the council." [T-S20, tertiary; also notes.md: not named by Athanasius or Eusebius, S15]
- Earliest sign: Theodore the Lector, c.510–515 (Wikipedia: "tenth line" of a list of attendees in his Tripartite Epitome). [T-S20] Cioffari: "The participation in the council of Nicea is affirmed by Theodore the Lector, around 515 A.C. The lack of his name in the most ancient lists makes us suppose as most probable that he took the name not from another list, but from the ancient Life of the Saint quoted by Eustratios of Constantinople in the year 583." [T-S17] (the date "515" is as printed there; Wikipedia says 510–515; Pearse says Theodore died by 518.)
- Cioffari cites Edward Schwartz, Über die Bischoflisten der Synoden von Chalkedon, Nicaea und Konstantinopel (1937), p.63, and Hansen's 1971 edition of Theodore. Neither read. [T-S17]
- Pearse: Anrich says Theodore's entry is a later insertion; Cioffari says it is genuine (notes.md S11).
- CE 1911: "There is reason to doubt his presence at Nicaea, since his name is not mentioned in any of the old lists of bishops that attended this council." [T-S6] Too flat; he is on Theodore's list (disputed text).
- First telling of Nicaea in a Life of Nicholas: Nicetas of Paphlagonia, before 890; Synaxarion life c.900 (adds jail and Nicaea). [T-S18] Michael (c.820–840) does not mention Nicaea (notes.md).
- Slap of Arius: Cioffari lists it as a "popular development" with the brick-and-flame Trinity speech; "The slap itself, however, was well in tone with Nicholas' character." [T-S17] Cioffari says it did not enter the Lives of the 8th–10th c. and "even later it was one of the less known aspects." [T-S17]
- Dating the slap legend: St Nicholas Center says "This legend is over 500 years old" [T-S19]; my earlier note says Wikipedia gave the 14th century. In this pass the Wikipedia page I fetched only said "Late, unsubstantiated legends" [T-S20]. So: 14th c. not confirmed.
- Popular pages repeat Nicaea as fact, for example the Christophers column: "he's supposed to have participated" in Nicaea and "came down firmly on the side of the divinity of Jesus." [T-S21] Weak.
- The Orthodox Church (OCA) says nothing of Nicaea in the Dec 6 troparia I read. [T-S1]
- Verdict for the writer: earliest mention c.515 (disputed); no eyewitness; the slap is much later. Say plainly "told later" or "a late story."

Other disputed or doubtful items
- Wrong credit: gifts and Santa Claus. Not his own act as known to us; a legend grew from the dowry story. Pearse: "at some remove." [T-S18]
- Jones's review says "in 1979 the Pope officially removed Nicholas from the hagiography." [T-S14, Hageman] The Liturgy Office calendar today still lists him (6 December) with no rank, which means an Optional Memorial. [T-S15] So the review line looks wrong or loose. Do not use it. If needed, say "his Roman feast was reduced in rank in the 1969 calendar reform"; I have NOT sourced that. Not found.
- Sayings credited to Nicholas that are not his: none found in any source I read. Words put in his mouth by later Lives: "Behold, you have called me, and I am here to help you" (Michael, c.820–840); "Nicholas, a sinner" lines (Michael and Praxis); the Trinity brick speech. These are authors' words, not records of his speech. [notes.md S2, S3; T-S17]
- A "Nicholas" prayer or saying you may meet on the web: I did not search for these quotes and found none in sources. Not found.
- The Nicholas of Sion mixing (storm, pilgrimage, healings): see notes.md; in Michael, Metaphrastes, Vita Compilata. [S5, S10, S11]
- Early Church said he was imprisoned under Diocletian ("confessor"): first seen in Synaxarion c.900. Butler: "The silence of other authors make many justly suspect these circumstances." [S1, S11]
- Date of the Thursday arrangement and of the hymns: not found.
- OCA 9 May says Khumeid of Rhodes in 792 robbed a tomb beside Nicholas's; this is a modern Orthodox entry; not verified. [T-S2]
