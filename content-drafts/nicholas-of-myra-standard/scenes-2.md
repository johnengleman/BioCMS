## SC-05 — The prison and the dreams (Constantinople)
- Status: full
- When and where: In the reign of Constantine, after the officers came home from Phrygia. Place: Constantinople, a prison and then the emperor's palace and court. The year is not given. In Proclus the freed men then travel to Myra: "on November fourth" and arrive "April thirteenth" [S12 sec.5].
- Who was there: the three army officers Nepotianus, Ursus, and Eupoleonis; the emperor Constantine; the prefect Ablabius; the prison warden; the master of forces who envied the officers; Nicholas, appearing in dreams while he is at Myra [S3].
- What happened, in order:
  1. The officers return to Constantinople and are praised in the palace. The master of forces grows envious and promises the prefect Ablabius 1,700 pounds of gold to destroy them [S3].
  2. Ablabius tells Constantine the officers plot a revolt "for money and bribes". Constantine puts them in custody without a hearing [S3].
  3. The conspirators press Ablabius: why keep them alive? Ablabius says again they are plotting. Constantine orders them beheaded by the sword during the night [S3].
  4. Ablabius tells the prison warden to prepare the three men for death that night. The warden weeps and tells them to send away any silver or gold they own [S3].
  5. The officers tear their clothes and their hair and throw themselves in the dust, crying out, "What evil did we do?" [S3].
  6. Nepotianus remembers what Nicholas did for the three men in Lycia and prays aloud. Then all three pray together [S3].
  7. That same night Nicholas appears to Constantine, orders him to free the three, and threatens a revolt and wild beasts. Constantine asks who he is and how he got into the palace. Nicholas names himself and vanishes [S3].
  8. The same night he appears to Ablabius with the same demand and a threat of illness, worms, and the ruin of his family. Ablabius asks who he is. Nicholas names himself and vanishes [S3].
  9. In the morning Constantine sends for Ablabius to tell him his dream. Ablabius sends back the same messenger with his own dream, which is the same [S3].
  10. Constantine has the officers brought before his court, with the prefect and the common people. He asks what magic they use that they can trouble the emperor's sleep. They stand silent, staring at the ground. Nepotianus says they know no magic and offers to take any torture if they have done evil [S3].
  11. Constantine asks if they know anyone named Nicholas. They cry out in one voice, "Lord God of Saint Nicholas, hear us". Nepotianus tells what Nicholas did [S3].
  12. Constantine frees them, tells them to cut their hair, put on their uniforms, thank Nicholas, and be kind to him. He sends gifts to Nicholas: holy vessels, a gold Gospel book, two gold candlesticks, and a gold dish set with precious stones [S3].
  13. The officers go to Myra, honor Nicholas, cut their hair, change their clothes, and give alms from their own goods. They do this for many years [S3].
- Details: The charge is plotting revolt for money, from Ablabius, on hearsay [S3]. The sentence is death by the sword that night [S3]. The warden's weeping and his advice to send off silver and gold [S3]. The officers tear their clothes and hair [S3]. Nicholas threatens Constantine with "wild beasts" and Ablabius with "worms" [S3; S12 sec.2 gives the worms and the ruin of the house]. Ablabius's name means "harmless" in Greek; the Praxis plays on it ("stricken in neither conscience nor mind" [S3]; S12 note 18 explains the pun). The emperor's gifts: holy vessels, a gold Gospel, two gold candlesticks, a jeweled gold dish [S3].
- Words spoken (our own plain English; the originals were not read):
  - Officers, praying together before the emperor: "Lord God of Saint Nicholas, hear us." (quotation, the one short quotation from S3, English by Jones 1978, locator: the scene where Constantine asks if they know any Nicholas.) plain: "God of Saint Nicholas, hear us."
  - Nicholas to Constantine in the dream (paraphrase of S3, no quotation marks): he says he is Bishop Nicholas, a sinner, who lives in the great city of Lycia.
  - Nicholas to Ablabius in the dream (paraphrase of S3): he says he is Nicholas, a sinner and servant of God, and the metropolitan at Myra.
  - Nicholas to Ablabius, in Proclus (public domain, S12 sec.2): "O you who are disabled in soul and thought, release those who were to be unjustly punished by you. For if you disobey me, you will not escape the punishment that was given to you through my lips, but you will receive these things from heaven. For you received vessels of silver and gold through your willingness to deprive these people of life and light." plain: "You who are crippled in soul and mind, free the men you meant to punish unjustly. If you disobey me, you will not escape the punishment I have spoken, and it will come from heaven. You took silver and gold vessels as the price for taking these men's lives."
  - Constantine, Proclus version (S12 sec.4): "How is it that you have sent a certain Nicholas to punish me?" plain: "Why did you send this man Nicholas to punish me?"
  - Constantine to the officers, Proclus version (S12 sec.5): "It is not I who grant you temporary life, but he who is called upon by you." plain: "It is not I who give you this short life. It is the one you called on."
  - Constantine in S3 (paraphrase): he says it is not he who gave them their lives, but Saint Nicholas, whom they called on.
- Inner life: The officers' prayer asks Nicholas to be near though far away, and says that the God of Nicholas should save them as he saved the three men in Myra [S3, paraphrase]. Nicholas names himself "a sinner" to both men [S3, S12 does not repeat this]. Constantine, in Proclus, asks the officers to carry back a message that Nicholas should not repay his anger with anger, but ask God to forgive him [S12 sec.5].
- Testimony: Proclus, encomium sec.4-5, told as the officers' and emperor's words. Speaker: the encomiast Proclus (c. 440 or later; attribution disputed, a retelling of the Praxis per S12 note 51). Rights: public-domain. Copied exactly: "And he [i.e., Constantine] ordered that the detained be released, telling in detail what he had seen in an ecstasy from the holy man, and he spoke in the midst of all the people and Senate, calling these people sorcerers and saying, "How is it that you have sent a certain Nicholas to punish me?" But they bowed their heads and, with pitiful and poor voices, (they) said, "We have committed no wrong against your happy reign."" ... "And so he whose head was adorned with precious stones, who was dressed in a purple robe and who possessed all ornament, replied, "It is not I who grant you temporary life, but he who is called upon by you. Hasten, then, and when you reach his feet, kiss them, telling him to make mention of me, too, to God. After receiving, then, these small blessings, bring them to him, telling him not to repay anger [with anger], but to ask for forgiveness for what I have done toward God.""
  plain: The emperor freed the prisoners. He told the whole people and the Senate what he had seen in his vision of the holy man. He called the prisoners sorcerers and said, "Why did you send this Nicholas to punish me?" They bowed their heads and said in weak voices, "We have done no wrong against your reign." Then the emperor, in his purple robe and jewels, said, "It is not I who give you your life. It is the one you called on. Go, kiss his feet, and ask him to remember me before God. Give him these gifts. Tell him not to answer my anger with anger, but to ask God to forgive what I have done."
- What changed: The officers go free and carry gifts to Myra, and the emperor owes his own peace to Nicholas. The name Nicholas is now known in the capital.
- Not in the sources: The prison building, its look, or how long they were held (S3 and S12 give only "that night"). What the officers wore in prison, except that they tore their clothes. The emperor's feelings beyond "consumed with wrath" before and the order to free them after. How Nicholas knew of their danger. Any crowd noise or weather. Where in Constantinople the dream happened. A year. A statement that Nicholas physically travelled to the capital (Proclus says he "reached the capital city", and the note S12 n.51 says this was by vision, not in body). The two sources differ on the order: in S3 the officers are brought before the court first, and then Constantine says it is not he who gave them life. In S12 Constantine orders them released first. The writer should follow S3 for the order.
- Sources: S3 (the story); S12 sec.2-5 and notes 51-58; S10 (notes: Nepotianus and Ablabius are known men; Cioffari calls the dream visits hagiographic devices).

## SC-06 — Grain ships at Andriake (Myra's harbor)
- Status: full
- When and where: A famine in Lycia, no date. Place: Andriake, the harbor of Myra, three miles from the city [S3 gives the distance; S2 sec.37 gives the harbor]. The sources say nothing of the year.
- Who was there: Nicholas; the captains of the Alexandrian grain ships [S2 sec.37; S4]; the people of the trading station at Andriake, who tell Nicholas the ships are there [S2 sec.37]. In S7 and S13, other versions.
- What happened, in order (S2 version, Michael; the oldest Life, 9th c.):
  1. Grain runs short in Lycia. Alexandrian transport ships full of grain are steered into the harbor of Andriake [S2 sec.37].
  2. People at the trading station tell Nicholas. He comes from Myra to Andriake [S2 sec.37].
  3. He orders the captains to unload a little from each ship so that "we may not perish from the famine" [S2 sec.37].
  4. The captains say the grain belongs to the state of the ruling city and they cannot. He tells them to take a hundred measures from each cargo, and he will see to their indemnity with the treasury official in Constantinople [S2 sec.37].
  5. They give the grain. With a good wind they reach Byzantium and measure the cargo. It is the same as when they got it in Alexandria. They tell the treasury officials, and everyone praises God [S2 sec.38].
  6. Nicholas receives the grain and distributes it to everyone. It lasts two years, and the people keep some for sowing [S2 sec.39].
- Other versions:
  - Golden Legend (S4, 1275): he hears that ships of wheat have come into the harbor, goes at once, and asks the sailors for "an hundred muyes" (about 400 bushels, a gloss in the text) from each ship. They say they dare not, because it is measured and they must account for it in the emperor's granaries in Alexandria. He promises "in the truth of God" that it will not be less. They find the full measure at Alexandria. He gives out the wheat as each has need, enough for two years, to eat and to sow.
  - Andrew of Crete (S13, early 8th c.): Nicholas is seen standing by the seamen "in the manner of a sailor". Here Nicholas persuades ship crews who did not intend to sail there to come through the harbor. He leaves "three gold coins" behind after he vanishes from the ship.
  - Metaphrastes (S7, 10th c.): Nicholas appears at night to a seaman who deals in grain, puts three measures of gold in his hand as a pledge, and tells him to go to Myra and sell the grain to the citizens (paraphrase; facts-only source).
- Details: Alexandrian ships; the harbor Andriake; the number 100 measures per ship; two years of grain; some kept for seed [S2]. The sum paid: Michael gives no coin; Andrew says "three gold coins"; Metaphrastes says three measures of gold. Michael's captains are afraid because the grain is state property [S2 sec.37; S4].
- Words spoken:
  - Nicholas, to the captains, reason for the request. Quote (S2 sec.37, English by the Pearse team, short): "in order that we may not perish from the famine". plain: "so that we do not die of hunger."
  - Nicholas, to the captains, the instruction (paraphrase of S2 sec.37): he tells them to take a hundred measures out of each cargo, and he says he will answer for the loss to the treasury official in Constantinople.
  - The captains (paraphrase of S2 sec.37): they say the grain belongs to the state and they are not able to do this.
  - Golden Legend (S4), the captains: "Father we dare not, for it is meted and measured, and we must give reckoning thereof in the garners of the Emperor in Alexandria." plain: "Father, we do not dare. The grain is counted and measured, and we must account for it in the emperor's granaries in Alexandria."
  - Golden Legend (S4), Nicholas: "Do this that I have said to you, and I promise, in the truth of God, that it shall not be lessed or minished when ye shall come to the garners." plain: "Do what I ask. I promise, in the truth of God, that your load will not be smaller when you reach the granaries."
- Inner life: Nothing in these passages from Nicholas himself. In S2, the people praise God and say God "always gives grace to those who love him" (paraphrase, sec.38).
- Testimony (public domain): Golden Legend, "The Miracle of the Wheat" [S4], copied exactly: "It was so on a time that all the province of S. Nicolas suffered great famine, in such wise that victual failed. And then this holy man heard say that certain ships laden with wheat were arrived in the haven. And anon he went thither and prayed the mariners that they would succour the perished at least with an hundred muyes [about 400 bushels] of wheat of every ship. And they said: Father we dare not, for it is meted and measured, and we must give reckoning thereof in the garners of the Emperor in Alexandria. And the holy man said to them: Do this that I have said to you, and I promise, in the truth of God, that it shall not be lessed or minished when ye shall come to the garners. And when they had delivered so much out of every ship, they came into Alexandria and delivered the measure that they had received. And then they recounted the miracle to the ministers of the Emperor, and worshipped and praised strongly God and his servant Nicholas."
  plain: Once the whole province of Nicholas had a great famine, and food ran out. The holy man heard that ships full of wheat had come into the harbor. He went at once and begged the sailors to help the starving with a hundred muyes (about 400 bushels) of wheat from each ship. They said, "Father, we do not dare. It is counted and measured, and we must account for it in the emperor's granaries in Alexandria." The holy man said, "Do what I ask. I promise, in the truth of God, that it will not be less when you reach the granaries." When they had given that much from each ship, they came to Alexandria and handed over the full measure they had been given. They told the emperor's officials what had happened and praised God and his servant Nicholas.
- What changed: Myra eats for two years, and the grain crews carry news of Nicholas back to the capital and across the sea [S2 sec.38-39].
- Not in the sources: How long the famine lasted before the ships came. The size of the crowd at the harbor. Any speech by the citizens. A confrontation between Nicholas and the captains beyond their objection. The captains' names, the ships' names, or the weather. The captains' arrival port differs by source (Constantinople in S2; Alexandria in S4). In the Michael Life the grain is measured at Constantinople, and the loss is "indemnified" to a treasury official, while S4 says Alexandria. Michael's chapters 37-39 are missing from many manuscripts and sit elsewhere in the Life in others (note in S2 text), so the story is probably a later addition. The "three gold coins" are in Andrew, not in Michael. Hence a writer must not combine all three versions into one. The sources do not say the grain was multiplied in Michael's words; they say the cargo was found "in the same state" on arrival.
- Sources: S2 sec.37-39 and the manuscript note; S4 "The Miracle of the Wheat"; S13 (Andrew, sec. on the steersman); S7 "Famine"; S10 via notes (Cioffari: "too generic").

## SC-07 — Sailors in the church (at sea, then Myra)
- Status: full
- When and where: undated. First at sea, in a storm, in an unnamed place. Then in the church at Myra [S2 sec.34-35]. The sources do not say which church.
- Who was there: Nicholas; a group of sailors [S2 sec.34]. No names.
- What happened, in order (S2, Michael):
  1. Sailors on the sea meet a great swell and a storm raised by a fierce wind. They see they will certainly die. They remember Nicholas and call to him by name [S2 sec.34].
  2. At that hour Nicholas appears to them "by God's order" and tells them, "Behold, you have called me, and I am here to help you" [S2 sec.34].
  3. The sailors see him helping at each part of the boat, "with the ropes and poles" [S2 sec.34]. He brings them to a calm anchorage [S2 sec.34].
  4. On shore they ask who saved them and wish to thank him. They learn he is in the church, and go there [S2 sec.35].
  5. They see that he is "unadorned" and has entered like one of the many clergy. They have never seen him before, yet they know him by what they saw on the ship [S2 sec.35].
  6. They approach slowly, fall at his feet, give thank-offerings, and tell how he appeared when they first called [S2 sec.35].
  7. Nicholas, seeing them, "perceived what they did in private" and tells them to know themselves, to turn their hearts to please God, and to give up injustice, greed, and sexual immorality [S2 sec.36].
- Details: ropes and poles [S2 sec.34]. The sailors know him "by his manifestation and help" [S2 sec.35]. He is plain-dressed ("unadorned") among the clergy [S2 sec.35]. Michael's note says the sailors had never seen him in life.
- Words spoken:
  - Nicholas on the ship, quote (S2 sec.34, short): "Behold, you have called me, and I am here to help you." plain: "You called me, and I am here to help you."
  - Nicholas in the church, rebuke (S2 sec.36, paraphrase, no quotation marks): he tells them, "children", to know themselves and turn their hearts to please the good Master. He says their companions, injustice and plain greed, along with sexual immorality, call down God's correction in their lives the way illness calls for doctors. He quotes Scripture: do no evil and evil will not overcome you (Sirach 7:1 as cited in S2). He tells them to live well and keep the body holy, "without which no one will see God" (Hebrews 12:14 as cited in S2). He ends by saying that the virtue of the soul draws out God's help in their trials.
  - plain version for the writer to adapt: "Look at yourselves, children. Turn your hearts toward pleasing the good God. Your injustice, your greed, and your impure lives call for God's correction, as sickness calls for a doctor. Do no evil, and evil will not overcome you. Learn to live well, and the Lord will help you when trouble comes."
- Inner life: Michael says Nicholas appeared "by God's order" [S2 sec.34] and that he saw their secret deeds "by the clear-sighted operation of his most-pure soul" [S2 sec.36, paraphrase]. Nothing in his own words about prayer in this scene.
- Testimony: none at length. Michael (S2) has no stated public-domain license; the passage above is given as paraphrase and short quotations. Cioffari (S10, facts-only) says this story fits Nicholas of Sion better, but calls it the origin of the sea patronage.
- What changed: The sailors know his face and his name and go away scolded. The story gives the sea patronage its start. In the outline's flow he remains a plain man among his clergy.
- Not in the sources: The sailors' number, home port, cargo, or destination. A storm lasting a certain time. A calming spoken as a command (Michael says he helped with the ropes and brought them to anchor). Any miracle in the church. A reply from the sailors to his rebuke. Michael's text does not say whether the church was Myra's cathedral (the outline says Myra; Michael says only "the church"). The notes' claim that he is in "his church" rests on S2 sec.35 saying he was in the church. Cioffari, in S10 via the notes, says the story fits Nicholas of Sion better. The Nicholas of Sion pilgrimage storm is a different story and must not be added.
- Sources: S2 sec.34-36 (fetched in full this session); S10 via notes.

## SC-08 — His death (Myra, 6 December, mid-4th century)
- Status: full
- When and where: 6 December [S1, S5 via notes]. Year uncertain: 343 in the Golden Legend [S4]; the notes list 342, 345, and 352 from other sources [notes, S1, S5, S6]. Place: Myra, in Lycia, his own city. The room and the hour are not given.
- Who was there: Nicholas. In S4: the angels he sees, and the "celestial company" heard singing. In S7: the bishops and all the clergy who carry his body [S4; S7]. No named witness.
- What happened, in order:
  1. Nicholas lives to a very great age, "full of days", and falls ill for only a short time [S7, paraphrase of Jones translation].
  2. Golden Legend: when God wills him to depart, he asks God to send him his angels. He lowers his head and sees them come, and so he knows he will die [S4].
  3. He begins Psalm 30 (Latin "In te Domine speravi", Vulgate numbering, as the Golden Legend gives it) and says it "unto, in manus tuas", the line "into your hands" [S4].
  4. Metaphrastes: while saying the thanksgivings that are said at death, he gives up his spirit [S7, paraphrase].
  5. He dies, "with great melody sung of the celestial company" [S4].
  6. His body, carried on the hands of the bishops and all the clergy with torches and lights, is laid in the sacred enclosure (temenos) at Myra [S7, paraphrase].
  7. Michael: his body is buried in the local church and at once "gushed forth a sweet-smelling perfume" [S2 sec.41, short quote].
  8. Golden Legend: when he is buried in a marble tomb, a spring of oil flows from his head to his feet, and the oil still flows in the writer's day [S4].
- Details: marble tomb [S4]. Torches and lights [S7]. A "sweet-smelling perfume" [S2 sec.41]. Oil from the head to the feet [S4]. The Golden Legend adds that the oil stopped when a later bishop was driven from his see and ran again when he came back [S4].
- Words spoken: "Lord, into thine hands I commend my spirit" [S4, Caxton English, public domain]. plain: "Lord, into your hands I give my spirit." The Golden Legend also quotes the psalm's opening in Latin: "In te domine speravi" ("I have hope in thee O Lord"). plain: "In you, Lord, I put my hope." The Latin original of the Golden Legend was not read here, so the Latin words are as the St Nicholas Center page prints them.
- Inner life: He prays for angels and then says Psalm 30 [S4]. In Metaphrastes he is "rendering those lauds and thanksgivings to God which are said in death" [S7, paraphrase].
- Testimony: Golden Legend, "The Death and Burial of Nicholas", copied exactly [S4, public domain]: "And when it pleased our Lord to have him depart out this world, he prayed our Lord that he would send him his angels; and inclining his head he saw the angels come to him, whereby he knew well that he should depart, and began this holy psalm: In te domine speravi ["I have hope in thee O Lord"], unto, in manus tuas , and so saying: "Lord, into thine hands I commend my spirit," he rendered up his soul and died, the year of our Lord three hundred and forty- three, with great melody sung of the celestial company. And when he was buried in a tomb of marble, a fountain of oil sprang out from the head unto his feet; and unto this day holy oil issueth out of his body, which is much available to the health of sicknesses of many men."
  plain: When it pleased God for Nicholas to leave this world, he asked God to send him his angels. He bowed his head and saw the angels come to him, and so he knew he was about to die. He began the psalm "In you, Lord, I have put my hope" and said it as far as "into your hands". Saying, "Lord, into your hands I give my spirit," he gave up his soul and died, in the year 343, while the company of heaven sang with great melody. When he was buried in a marble tomb, a spring of oil flowed from his head to his feet. To this day holy oil flows from his body, and it helps many sick people.
- What changed: The oil and perfume at the tomb begin, and pilgrims will come (S2 sec.41-42; S7 "Pilgrims flocked to Myra").
- Not in the sources: Any last words to his people or his clergy (none in S2, S4, or S7). The name of any bishop at his bed. His age at death (S7 says only "very advanced age"). The cause of the illness. The weather, the hour, or the room. How long the funeral lasted. A crowd at the funeral (S7 names only bishops and clergy). That the psalm was spoken aloud rather than in prayer. The death year (the Golden Legend's 343 is one claim among 342, 343, 345, and 352; write "about the middle of the fourth century"). The outline's line "Metaphrastes' exact wording of the last moments" is answered by the paraphrase above; the quotation is from a facts-only translation, so it is not copied here. Michael does not mention angels or Psalm 30 at the death; he says Nicholas "went to his eternal rest, rejoicing with choruses of angels" (paraphrase, S2 sec.41).
- Sources: S4 "The Death and Burial of Nicholas"; S7 "The Death of Nicholas"; S2 sec.40-42.
