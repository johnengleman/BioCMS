Patara was a town of Lycia, in the southwest of what is now Turkey. There a rich Christian couple died and left everything to their only son. As a boy he had kept away from the theater and from the world of business, and he was always in church. Now he had his parents' land, their money, and their goods, and he had no father on earth. So he decided that from then on God would be his father. He asked God to take his life and all he owned, if God was willing, and he prayed in the words of a psalm:1

"Lord, teach me to do what you want, because you are my God."

— Saint Nicholas, after his parents' death, praying Psalm 143:10

He began to give his wealth away and never stopped. In this way he stored it where nothing could take it, in heaven. He meant to give where no one would see. He wanted no name for himself but one: a sinner. All his life he tried to stay hidden, but the people he helped would not let him.2

In the fourth century Saint Nicholas became bishop of Myra, another city of Lycia. The Church never forgot him. By the end of the sixth century his story was already being written down. In the sixth century the emperor Justinian built a church in his honor in Constantinople. Before the year 800 his face had been painted on an icon at the monastery on Mount Sinai. Today the Orthodox Church honors him above every saint except the Mother of God and Saint John the Baptist.3

Two things set him apart: a giver's wish to stay out of sight, and the courage to stand between the innocent and a bought judge. The first shows itself at a neighbor's window.4

Saint Nicholas Throws Gold Through a Window

Next door to him lived a man who had once been rich and well known. Now he had nothing, not even enough to eat. He had three daughters. They were so poor that no man would marry them lawfully. Every bride needed a dowry, the money her family brought to her marriage, and he had none to give. So he settled on a terrible plan. He would put his daughters in a brothel and live on what they earned.5

Saint Nicholas heard of it and was horrified. Sayings of Solomon came into his mind: "God loves a cheerful giver." "Whoever pities a beggar will himself be fed." And one more: "Save those who are led away to death." The three daughters were already being led away to the death of their souls.6

He could have knocked at the man's door with an offer of help, but he would not. To walk in with money would shame a man who had once been rich, and it would put Nicholas's own name on the gift. He wanted to spare the father, and he wanted to stay hidden.7

So at night he filled a bag with gold and carried it to the neighbor's house. He threw it in through the window and hurried home, uneasy at the thought that someone might have seen him.8

In the morning the father found the bag. He worked the string loose and stared at what was inside. He was afraid it was false gold, so he tested it with his fingertips until he was sure. Then he wept. He went through the names of everyone he knew and could think of no one who would do such a thing. So he thanked God for it. With the gold he married off his eldest daughter.9

Even after the wedding, word reached Saint Nicholas that the father was getting ready to sell his second daughter. So a second bag came through the same window in the night, as rich as the first. This time the father could not speak. He lay with his face to the ground and prayed in his heart: "Show me, merciful Master, your angel among men." Then he married off his second daughter.10

Now he stayed awake. Night after night he sat up and watched, sure that the giver would come again for the third daughter. Late one night Saint Nicholas came back with silent steps. He threw a third bag through the window and turned for home.11

The father heard the gold land in the house. He ran out after the giver, who was running too:12

"He called: 'Sir, do not run away. Let me see you and know you.' He ran harder and saw that it was Nicholas. At once he knelt and tried to kiss his feet, but the saint would not allow it. He only asked the man not to tell anyone about this as long as he lived."

— Blessed Jacobus de Voragine, The Golden Legend, 127513

The giver was the young man from next door. His family was too well known for him to pass as a stranger. The father poured out his thanks and called the young man the savior of himself and his three daughters, after God. "If Christ had not stirred up your goodness," he said, "we would long ago have destroyed our lives in a shameful trade. The Lord has saved us through you." Saint Nicholas raised him from the ground and let him go in peace.14

He had been seen by only one man, and that man had sworn to be silent. One night a voice would tell a stranger his name.15

Myra Chooses Saint Nicholas as Its Bishop

Myra was the chief city of Lycia. When its bishop died, the bishops of the nearby cities gathered there with the leading clergy of Myra to find a worthy man to follow him. One of them urged the others to pray about it, and they prayed. Then the Lord showed one of the bishops what to do. "Go to the church at night and stand at the door," a voice told him. "Take the first person who comes in quietly, before anyone else, and make him bishop. His name is Nicholas."16

The bishop told the others. They stayed deep inside the church and kept praying while he went out to the entrance to wait. In the early hours of dawn a man came up to the church alone. The bishop took hold of him and asked, "What is your name, child?" The answer came in a plain, gentle voice:17

"Nicholas, a sinner, a servant of your Holiness, master."

— Saint Nicholas at the door of the church of Myra18

He had given his name, and with it the only title he wanted. The bishop was moved by the answer. "Come with me, my child," he said. "I have something to show you." He led Saint Nicholas in to the other bishops. They gave glory to God, brought him into the middle of the church, and showed the people the shepherd God had sent them. As the news spread, crowds poured in.19

Saint Nicholas held back. He was slow to accept the honor, and he could hardly bring himself to climb to the bishop's throne. The people of Myra received him with joy and set him on it all the same. The man who had run from one father's thanks now sat where a whole city could see him.20

As bishop he taught his people to believe in the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit. He turned them away from the teachings of Arius and Sabellius, which were flooding the world in those days. He went through the country around Myra tearing down the temples of the idols. Myra still had its temple of the goddess Artemis, the tallest building in the city. Saint Nicholas pulled it down and dug up even its foundations. The demons who lived in it fled, crying out that God was driving them from their home.21

Saint Nicholas Feeds Myra in a Famine

As the years passed, his people ran to him in every trouble. He was a father to orphans and a champion of widows, and men who wanted to harm the innocent learned to fear his rebuke. In hard times he became the grain merchant of his whole country.22

One year the grain ran out across Lycia. Then grain ships from Alexandria came into Andriake, the harbor of Myra. The people of the trading station there sent word to their bishop, and he came down from the city. He asked the captains to unload a little from each ship, "so that we do not die of hunger."23

The captains refused. The grain belonged to the state, they said, and they could not touch it. Saint Nicholas had an answer. "Take a hundred measures out of each cargo," he told them, "and I will answer for your loss to the treasury official in Constantinople." He was giving his own word to the treasury so that his people could eat. The captains gave him the grain.24

A good wind carried the ships on to Constantinople. There the captains measured their cargo and found the full amount they had loaded in Alexandria. They told the treasury officials what had happened, and everyone praised God, who always gives grace to those who love him.25

In Myra, Saint Nicholas measured out the grain to everyone. It fed the city for two years, with enough left over to sow the fields. The people thanked God as they took it, and they lived on God's kindness through the prayers of his servant.26

Sailors Find Saint Nicholas Among His Clergy

Out at sea a fierce wind raised a great swell and then a storm. The crew of a ship saw that they were going to die. They had never seen the bishop of Myra, but they knew his name and called on him. In that very hour he was there among them, sent by God. "You called me, and I am here to help you," he said. They saw him in every part of the ship, working beside them at the ropes and poles. With God's help he brought them safe to a calm anchorage.27

Ashore, they wanted to thank the man who had saved them. They learned that he was in the church and went to find him. He had come in plainly dressed, like any one of his many clergy. Still they knew him at once from the ship. Slowly they came toward him and fell at his feet. They gave him their thank-offerings and told him how he had come to them the moment they called.28

Saint Nicholas looked at them and saw what they did in private. He did not take their thanks and send them home. "Know yourselves, children, I beg you," he said, "and steer your hearts toward pleasing the good and compassionate Master." Their injustice, their greed, and their impure lives were calling down God's correction, he told them, as sickness calls for a doctor. "Do not do evil things, and evil will not overcome you." If they learned to live well and kept their bodies pure, the Lord would help them in the trials to come.29

He was gentle with everyone, but fierce with sinners. Once, on a visit to the churches of his flock, he met Theognes. Theognes was a bishop of the Marcianists, a group apart from the Church. Saint Nicholas brought the man back to what was good, though a little anger had come between them. He called to Theognes in a voice both mild and loud, with the words of the apostle: "Come to reconciliation, brother, before the sun sets on our anger."30

Both sides of him would be needed on the day his city came looking for him.

Saint Nicholas Takes the Sword at Byrra

In the days of the emperor Constantine, a ship full of soldiers lay at anchor in Andriake. The emperor had sent them to put down unrest in another province, and they were waiting for a wind. Three officers commanded them. One of them was named Nepotianus.31

Some of the soldiers went ashore to buy food. Some looters dressed as soldiers were caught robbing. A crowd began to riot in the square and mobbed the soldiers as if they were the thieves. The noise carried all the way up to Myra. Saint Nicholas walked down to the harbor to calm the riot. The officers hailed him, and the soldiers who knew him embraced him. "Where do you come from," he asked, "and on whose business, and why?" They told him they came in peace and asked for his prayers. He invited them up to Myra for his blessing and a meal. The soldiers admired the bishop and his gentle way of speaking, and they ordered the crowd to be quiet.32

Then people came from the city with terrible news. "Lord, if you had been in the city," they said, "three innocent men would not have been handed over to death." A judge had ordered three men beheaded. The whole city was in an uproar because its bishop could not be found. When Saint Nicholas heard it, his heart sank.33

He took the officers with him and crossed the city. In a square called Leonti he asked people coming away from the sentencing whether the three men were still alive. They were still alive, he was told. They were just ahead, at the shrine of the martyrs Crescentius and Dioscorus. As they talked, Saint Nicholas said that the condemned men should already be on their way out. At the gate he learned where the beheading was to be: at a place called Byrra. Then he ran.34

A great crowd stood in front of the executioner, who held his sword up, ready. The three men had been put in their places with their hands tied behind them. Linen cloths covered their faces. They were on their knees with their heads bowed, waiting to die.35

Saint Nicholas seized the sword out of the executioner's hand and threw it to the ground. The righteous are bold as a lion, says the Book of Proverbs. He freed the three men from their chains and took them with him into the city. He had put his own life between the sword and three condemned men, as his Master had done.36

From Byrra he went straight on to the governor's hall and thrust the door open. A guard had already told the governor, Eustathius, what had happened, and Eustathius came forward to honor the bishop. Saint Nicholas turned away from him. "You sacrilegious shedder of blood!" he said. "How dare you face me, when you have been caught in so many evil deeds?" He would not spare the governor or forgive him, he said. He would tell the emperor Constantine about every sin Eustathius had been caught in, and about the way he ruled his province.37

Eustathius fell to his knees. "Do not be angry with your servant, lord," he begged. He blamed two officials of the state, Eudoxius and Simonides. But Saint Nicholas had already learned the price of the three lives. The governor was to receive more than two hundred pounds of silver for their deaths. Saint Nicholas answered him:38

"Eudoxius and Simonides did not do this. Silver and gold did."

— Saint Nicholas to the governor of Lycia39

The officers began to plead for the governor, and they pleaded hard. Once the charges against the three men were cleared, Saint Nicholas pardoned him. He had walked in calling the man a killer, and he left him forgiven. Then at last he gave the officers the meal he had promised. They asked him to pray for them, received his blessing, and sailed away.40

They had seen a bishop's anger and a bishop's mercy in one day. One of them would remember it in a prison cell.

Three Officers Call on Saint Nicholas in Prison

The officers finished their work and came home to Constantinople. The city gave them a great welcome, and the emperor's court praised them. Then the devil stirred up envy in the heart of the army's commander. He went to the emperor's prefect, Ablabius, and promised him seventeen hundred pounds of gold to destroy the three.41

Ablabius went to Constantine. "Most pious lord," he said, "a great conspiracy has been formed against you by the officers of the army." They were working for money and bribes, he said. The emperor was consumed with rage, and he had them thrown into prison without a hearing. That was not enough for those who plotted against them. "Why do you put them in prison but leave them alive?" they pressed the prefect. So Ablabius went back to the emperor and said the officers were still plotting from prison, and Constantine ordered the three officers beheaded that very night.42

The warden of the prison came to them in tears. "Sorrow grips me, and fear and trembling," he said. "I wish I had never seen you or heard of you. You are ordered to die tonight." He told them to send off whatever they could give away: silver, gold, or anything else. The three men tore their clothes and pulled out their hair. They threw themselves down in the dust and cried out, "What evil did we do, that we should die so miserably?"43

Then Nepotianus remembered Myra: the crowd at Byrra, the raised sword, the three men on their knees, and the bishop who had saved them. With tears he began to pray. He begged the God of Saint Nicholas to have mercy on them, and to save them now as he had saved those three men in Lycia. "Saint Nicholas, servant of Christ," he prayed, "though you are far from us, bring your prayers near to us." Then all three prayed together, as if with one voice.44

Saint Nicholas was in Myra, far away. But God had given him the grace to help those far away who called on him in faith. That same night he stood before the emperor in a dream. "Constantine, rise and free the three officers you have put in prison," he said. "They were condemned on hearsay." He warned that if the emperor refused, a revolt would rise against him and his body would be thrown to the wild beasts. "Who are you?" the emperor asked. "And how did you get into my palace?"45

"I am Bishop Nicholas, a sinner," he answered. "I live in the chief city of Lycia." Then he was gone.46

That night he also went to Ablabius with the same demand. If the prefect did not free the three innocent men, he would fall ill and end as food for worms, and his whole family would perish. "Who are you," Ablabius asked, "and where do you come from, to speak to me like this?"47

"I am Nicholas, a sinner, a servant of God," he said. "I am the chief bishop in Myra." And he vanished as before. He had come to the emperor and to his prefect with a warning. To both he gave the name he had once given at a church door.48

When Constantine woke, he sent a messenger to tell Ablabius what he had seen and heard. The prefect sent the messenger back with his own story. It was the same dream.49

The emperor had the three officers brought before him, with the prefect, the court, and the common people looking on. "Tell me," he said, "what kind of magic do you work, that you can trouble us like this in our sleep?" They stood silent, staring at the ground. He asked again. At last Nepotianus spoke. "Most pious lord, your servants know nothing of magic." If they had plotted any evil against the emperor, he said, let him order the worst tortures for them.50

Then the emperor asked, "Do you know anyone by the name of Nicholas?"

At that name their hearts rose. As if with one voice, the three men cried out:51

"Lord God of Saint Nicholas, hear us."

— Nepotianus, Ursus, and Eupoleonis before the emperor Constantine52

They begged God to save them, as he had saved the three men in Myra. "Tell me," said the emperor, "who is this Nicholas?" And Nepotianus told him what Saint Nicholas had done.53

Constantine heard him out and set the three men free. "I did not give you your lives," he said. "Saint Nicholas gave them, the one you called on." He told them to cut their hair, put on their uniforms again, and go and thank him. Then, in his purple robe and jewels, the emperor gave them a message to carry:54

"Go quickly. When you reach him, kiss his feet and ask him to remember me before God, too. Take him these small gifts. Tell him not to repay anger with anger, but to ask forgiveness for what I have done against God."

— The emperor Constantine to the three officers, in a sermon under the name of Saint Proclus55

They carried the emperor's gifts to Myra: sacred vessels, a Gospel book all of gold, two gold candlesticks, and a gold dish set with precious stones. The giver who had thrown his gold through a window at night had now been named before an emperor's whole court. Three lives had been saved because men called on his name, and he had not left Myra.56

The three officers set out for Myra on the fourth of November and reached it on the thirteenth of April. There they honored the bishop who had saved them. They cut their hair and changed their clothes, and with tears they gave their own goods to the poor: gold and silver and clothing. They went on doing it for many years, praising the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit.57

The Death of Saint Nicholas at Myra

The Church has always remembered how he looked. He had a venerable face, like an angel's, and a sweet fragrance came from him, so that just to see him made people better. A heretic who met him as he passed by would give up his false belief and accept the faith.58

Saint Nicholas stayed in Myra and grew very old, full of days. Then he had a short illness. Part of him wanted to stay in the body, and part of him longed just as much to be set free. He died on 6 December, about the middle of the fourth century.59

He had begun his life with God by praying a psalm, and he ended it the same way:

"When it pleased God for Nicholas to leave this world, he asked God to send him his angels. He bowed his head and saw the angels come to him, and so he knew he was about to die. He began the psalm 'In you, Lord, I have put my hope' and said it as far as 'into your hands.' Saying, 'Lord, into your hands I commend my spirit,' he gave up his soul and died … while the company of heaven sang with great melody."

— Blessed Jacobus de Voragine, The Golden Legend, 127560

Bishops and all the clergy carried his body with their own hands, with torches and lights, and laid it to rest in the church at Myra. At once the body gave off "a sweet-smelling perfume." Holy oil flowed from his tomb, and the sick were healed by it.61

The heir who had once prayed "Teach me to do what you want" had ended with "Into your hands." He went to his rest among the choirs of angels, and he has never stopped praying for those who call on him in faith.62

Pilgrims Come to His Tomb at Myra

Pilgrims came to Myra from every side for the perfume and the healing oil of his tomb. Many stopped there on their way to the Holy Land and asked him to keep them safe at sea. In the early 700s a preacher at Myra thanked him on his feast for his "miraculous and ardent help." He called Saint Nicholas the pilot of all who sail, "turning a hurricane into a breeze and a storm into a calm."63

The Church remembers too that he sat among the bishops at the great Council of Nicaea in 325, which met to declare that the Son is equal to the Father. "The admirable Nicholas helped to bring this about as a member of the sacred synod," wrote Saint Symeon Metaphrastes. Later tellers also remembered that he had been imprisoned for the faith under the emperor Diocletian. They told how he struck the heretic Arius at the council and was put in chains. That night Christ and the Mother of God came to his cell and gave him a Gospel book and a bishop's stole.64

For more than seven hundred years the tomb stayed at Myra. By 1087, fear of the Turks had driven the people of the city to a mountain twelve stades away, about a mile and a third. Four monks stayed to keep the shrine. Then forty-seven armed men walked up from the harbor.65

Sailors Carry His Holy Relics to Bari

The men came from Bari, a port in the south of Italy. They were on ships trading with Antioch. They had come for the body of Saint Nicholas. Two pilgrims went ahead to look and found no Turks. Then forty-seven of the men went up to the shrine with their weapons, while the rest guarded the ships.66

They prayed at the altar and asked the four monks to show them where the saint lay. The monks showed them the place where they drew the holy oil with a sponge. Then the monks understood what the strangers wanted. They would rather die, they said, than let him be taken. The Bari men offered gold, and the monks tossed it aside. So the sailors tied them up and set guards on the gates.67

One of the priests with the sailors had set a glass vial of the holy oil on top of a low column. It fell with a loud clang onto the marble floor, but it did not break. The men crowded round and praised God. They took it as the saint's own answer: he was willing to go with them.68

A young sailor named Matthew drew his sword, seized one of the monks, and swore he would run him through. Another monk begged him to stop. A year before, he said, Saint Nicholas had appeared to three people of Myra. He had warned that if the citizens did not come back from the mountain to guard their city, he would go somewhere else.69

Matthew put away his sword and took up an iron mallet. He broke through the marble pavement and found a tomb of white marble underneath. The others were afraid to strike it. Matthew struck. The coffin inside was filled with holy oil as high as the navel of the body. Such a perfume rose from it that the men felt they were standing in paradise. The breeze carried it down to the ships, nearly three miles away.70

The monks wept. "Our lord and dear father," they cried to him, "why do you leave us when we already suffer so much?"71

A priest named Grimoald wrapped the bones in new silk. Some of the men wanted to take the old wonder-working icon of the saint as well, but they were stopped. Saint Nicholas did not wish to leave Myra with nothing. Then the men lifted the body onto their shoulders and carried it down to the ships, singing praises to God.72

When the monks were set free, the people of Myra came down to the shore, men and women of every age. They waded into the sea in their clothes and shoes and caught hold of the ships' rails. "Give back our father and our lord," they begged. At sunset the ships put out to sea. The sound of mourning from the shore followed them across the water for nearly two miles.73

The voyage began badly. A north wind and the tides fought them all the way to Patara, where Saint Nicholas was born. The sailors began to fear that he did not want to go with them. Worn out, they put in at a place called Perdikca. There they accused one another of stealing from the relics, and they brought out the Gospels so that every man could swear. Five sailors confessed that they had taken pieces, and they gave them back. The priests said Mass, the winds fell, and the ships sailed on. Later a sailor named Disigius fell asleep and saw Saint Nicholas, who told him, "Do not be afraid, for I am with you. In twenty days we will all arrive together at the city of Bari."74

They reached Bari on 9 May 1087, in the first watch of the evening. The whole city ran to the harbor, and the clergy came down singing in their vestments. Some citizens wanted the body in the cathedral, and the sailors wanted a new church for it. The abbot of Saint Benedict's monastery kept it until the city could agree. When Archbishop Ursus returned, he planned to take the body by force. Both sides took up arms, and two or three men were killed. Then the crowd carried the body to the church of Saint Eustratius in the old governor's court, singing "Lord, have mercy." They pulled down the altar there to build one for Saint Nicholas.75

The sick came that same night. In that night and the next day, forty-seven men, women, and children were healed. On the fifth day Saint Nicholas appeared to a monk named Mark. He sent him to the people of Bari with a message: "By God's will I am leaving the Roman world. Where I go, I go only as a visitor. Here I will stay forever."76

Both Churches Keep His Feast

In October 1089 Blessed Pope Urban II laid the holy relics of Saint Nicholas beneath the altar of the new crypt at Bari, where they rest today. Both Churches keep his feast on 6 December, the day he died. On that day the Catholic Church's book of saints, the Roman Martyrology, names only one of his miracles. It tells how he appeared to the emperor Constantine from far away and moved him to mercy. On 9 May the Catholic Church and the Russian Orthodox Church remember the day he came to Bari, a feast the Church of Kiev took up soon after 1087.77

At Bari the holy oil kept flowing. People call it the manna of Saint Nicholas, and every year the May festival ends when the rector of the basilica draws it from the saint's tomb. Sailors and travelers, merchants and bakers, and children took him for their patron.78

In many places parents kept a custom on the eve of his feast. They would secretly give their boys and girls small gifts of many kinds. The children believed that Saint Nicholas went here and there through the towns and villages with his servants, came in through the closed windows, and handed out the gifts himself. This custom grew from the story of a purse of money thrown secretly, at night, through a window to save three daughters.79

The secret one father had sworn to keep became a secret that parents kept for their children.

A Note on the Sources

Nothing written in Saint Nicholas's own lifetime has come down to us. The Catholic Encyclopedia of 1911 says that scarcely anything about him is historically certain except that he was bishop of Myra in the fourth century. Sources give the year of his death as 342, 343, 345, or 352.

The oldest account is the Praxis de stratelatis, which tells only the story of the three condemned men and the three officers. Anrich dated it between 460 and 580, and Cioffari between the saint's death and the middle of the fifth century. Eustratios of Constantinople quoted it after 583. The Praxis names a judge called Datianus as the man who sentenced the three men. Later it has Saint Nicholas face the governor, Eustathius, and Michael names Eustathius too. This account tells only the meeting with Eustathius and leaves Datianus unnamed. The sermon under the name of Saint Proclus retells the Praxis and gives the officers' journey dates; it is listed among works wrongly credited to him. The emperor's words "I did not give you your lives" follow the Praxis; the message after them comes from the sermon.

Between 814 and 842 the monk Michael the Archimandrite wrote the first full Life. It is the source for the gold, the election, the famine, the sailors, and the burial. Its famine chapters are missing from many manuscripts. The Golden Legend differs from Michael in three places. In it the grain is accounted for at Alexandria, and the third bag holds twice as much gold as the others. At his election Saint Nicholas gives only his name, without the word "sinner." Michael's translator thinks the father's oath of silence was to last as long as Saint Nicholas lived. Cioffari thinks the popular election of a layman is likely true, because Gratian's Decretum records it. As Cioffari notes, the inscriptions of Opramoas confirm that the temple of Artemis was the tallest building in Myra.

A second Saint Nicholas was abbot of Holy Sion and bishop of Pinara, and he died in the sixth century. By the tenth century parts of his Life were told of the bishop of Myra: a pilgrimage to Jerusalem, a storm calmed on that voyage, and many healings. They are left out here. Anrich doubted that Saint Andrew of Crete wrote the sermon under his name, and its translator notes that its miracle part may mix in the abbot's stories. Cioffari treats the meeting with Theognes as the bishop of Myra's, though he cannot say for certain who Theognes was. He thinks the sailors' story fits the abbot of Sion better, but he calls it the root of Saint Nicholas's patronage of sailors.

Michael does not mention the Council of Nicaea or a prison under Diocletian. Pearse's list gives a eulogy by Nicetas of Paphlagonia, written before 890, as the first mention of Nicaea. It gives a Synaxarion life of about 900 as the first mention of the prison. Butler notes that the silence of other authors makes many suspect the prison story. His name is missing from the oldest and shorter lists of the council's bishops. Theodore the Lector's list of about 515 includes it. Anrich took that entry for a later insertion, and Cioffari holds it genuine. Cioffari counts the slap of Arius among the "popular developments" that grew up around the council.

Butler dates Justinian's church to about 430, but Justinian ruled from 527 to 565; this account follows the Orthodox history, which places the church in his reign. The Roman Martyrology is quoted from an older English text; the current Latin text was not checked.

For the taking of the relics there are two accounts: Nicephorus of Bari, written in 1087, and a Greek account surviving in a thirteenth-century manuscript. Nicephorus says the people of Myra feared the Turks, and the Greek account says Persians. Nicephorus dates the departure from Myra to 11 April, and the Greek account to eleven days before the first of May. Nicephorus counts two dead in the fight at Bari, the Greek account three. Both count forty-seven cured in the first night and day; Butler gives thirty. By "the Roman world" the Greek writer meant the Greek empire of his own day, which still called itself Roman.

The custom of gifts on the eve of his feast is described by Hospinian in his Latin book on the origins of the Christian feasts. His words were read in John Brand's Observations on Popular Antiquities of 1813, which quotes the Latin in full; the translation is ours.

How This Life Was Researched

This life rests on twenty-eight sources, all named in the list below. Twenty were gathered in the first research on Saint Nicholas, and seven more pages were each used to check a single fact. For this rewrite one more was read: Brand's Popular Antiquities, to confirm Hospinian's own words. The old texts were read in English translations. The oldest are the Praxis de stratelatis and the sermons under the names of Saint Proclus and Saint Andrew of Crete. Then come the Lives by Michael the Archimandrite and Saint Symeon Metaphrastes, the Golden Legend, and the two accounts of the voyage of 1087. Only Hospinian's Latin was translated from the original. Fourteen of these texts were saved in full for this rewrite and read again, and every quotation was checked against them. The numbered notes give the exact place of each story and quotation. Popular retellings often leave out the price in the officers' story. The governor was to receive more than two hundred pounds of silver, and the prefect was promised seventeen hundred pounds of gold.
