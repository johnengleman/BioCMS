# Nicholas of Myra — teachings and influence outline

Facts come only from notes-teachings.md (T-S keys), notes.md (S keys), and biography.html. No new research.

## 1. Kind of saint

**A witness.** He left no writing and no saying with an early witness (T-S6, S10, notes-teachings Q1–Q3). The Church took its teaching from the stories of his deeds, and his cult grew after death into one of the largest in Christendom.

**Planned length: about 1,900 words** (range 1,700–2,200). Do not pad toward 2,500.

## 2. Central claim

Nicholas left the Church no words, yet the Church sings that his life was "a rule of faith" and "a teacher of temperance." It remembers him for three things the oldest stories show: he defended the condemned, he gave in secret, and he called himself a sinner. Above all it remembers him as the saint people call on by name, and that habit of calling on him spread from one tomb in Lycia to Russia, Bari, and the December gift-giver, and still links East and West.

## 3. Section plan

### Opening (no heading, 180–220 words)

- Hook: the troparion for 6 December (OCA English, tone 4, T-S1). Quote the first two lines only: "The truth of things revealed you to your flock as a rule of faith, / a model of meekness, and a teacher of temperance." Then the turn: the man the hymn calls a teacher left no book, no letter, no sermon, and not one saying that scholars accept as his.
- So what did he teach? Answer in one sentence: what the hymn means is his deeds, read by the Church as a lesson.
- Kind of influence, one sentence: across the whole Church, East and West, but through example, prayer, feasts, and popular custom, not through ideas.
- Do NOT open with the three officers. The biography already opens there.

### What Nicholas Left Us (220–280 words)

- Say it plainly: nothing in his own hand. The Catholic Encyclopedia's view that almost nothing is certain except that he was bishop of Myra in the fourth century (T-S6). Paraphrase; the biography already quotes this.
- What the Church has instead, in time order, one clause each (S11, T-S18, biography Note on the Sources):
  - Praxis de stratelatis, the oldest story (date disputed, about 400 to the 6th century).
  - The Encomium attributed to Proclus (authorship doubted by Anrich).
  - The sermon credited to Andrew of Crete, early 8th century (authorship doubted).
  - Michael the Archimandrite's Life, 814–842, the first full Life.
  - Symeon Metaphrastes, 10th century; Nicephorus of Bari, 1087.
  - The hymns of the Eastern services (troparion, kontakion; authors unknown).
- Words in his mouth: one short paragraph. Later Lives give him a speech on the Trinity and set him against Arius and Sabellius. Cioffari calls the Trinity speech a "popular development" (T-S17). Michael names the errors but not Nicaea (notes.md S2 sec. 25–26). These are the teaching of his biographers, not of Nicholas. This paragraph replaces the "Taught About God" section.
- Where a reader should start (three items): the Praxis (Jones translation, via Sanidopoulos); Michael's Life (Quinn and Pearse team); the 6 December troparion and kontakion (OCA).

### What Nicholas Taught About God — LEFT OUT

One line in the essay is not needed; the paragraph above covers it. Reason: no doctrinal statement of his survives; the doctrinal speeches are by Michael and later writers.

### What Nicholas Taught About Everyday Life (500–580 words)

Lead-in, two sentences: the Church read lessons from his deeds. A modern Greek homily for Thursday names "faith, meekness, temperance, humility, material poverty" as "the characteristic features of the apostolic life" (T-S4; label it a modern preacher's reading). Each `<h3>` gives the lesson, the scene in one or two sentences (the biography tells it in full), and the Church's words for it. Never write "Nicholas taught" or "Nicholas said" without naming the text.

1. **`<h3>` Give in a way no one sees.** Scene: the three bags of gold at night; he makes the father swear silence (Michael sec. 17, S2). Metaphrastes later calls it a "unique deed" known to the whole people (S5, PG 116 328A). Lesson: the gift that shames no one. Note in one clause that the core story is old, with three very different early versions (S10), but the event cannot be proved. Do not re-tell the chase.
2. **`<h3>` Stand between the innocent and the sword.** Scene: Byrra, the sword taken from the executioner (Praxis, S3). His word to the governor, in our own English as in the biography: "Eudoxius and Simonides did not do this. Silver and gold did." Attribute: "in the Praxis." Church's word: the kontakion, tone 3 (T-S1): "by laying down your life for your people, and saving the innocent from death." Say that the hymn's link to the Praxis is our reading; no source says the hymn writer used it.
3. **`<h3>` Call yourself a sinner.** Three times in the early stories he gives his name as "a sinner": at his election (Michael sec. 23) and in the dreams of Constantine and Ablabius (Praxis). Church's word: troparion, "Through humility, you attained the heights; and through poverty, riches" (T-S1). Say the two are joined by the essay's own reading.
4. **`<h3>` Feed people, then tell them the truth.** Two short scenes: the grain ships at Andriake, where he takes the risk on himself (Michael sec. 37–39); the rescued sailors, whom he rebukes for greed and impurity (Michael sec. 36). Use the scripture line Michael gives him only with a frame ("Michael has him quote..."). Lesson: mercy and correction come from the same bishop. Mention Cioffari: the sailors' story may belong to Nicholas of Sion (S10).

### Nicholas's Special Devotion — LEFT OUT

Reason: no source says he saw any part of the faith in a new way. The traits the Church took from him are covered above.

### How Nicholas Changed the Church in His Lifetime (130–170 words)

- Honest and short. No pupils, converts, community, or debate are known from any source near his time (notes-teachings Q5).
- The only old witness is the Praxis: a governor rebuked and pardoned, three men freed, and three officers who gave alms for years after (biography). One sentence; do not retell.
- Nicaea in two or three sentences: his name first appears in a list of the bishops at Nicaea about 510–515, by Theodore the Lector; the oldest, shorter lists omit him; Anrich calls the entry a later insertion, Cioffari calls it genuine (T-S17, S11). No one at the council names him. Michael (c. 820–840) does not mention Nicaea; the first Life to do so is before 890 (T-S18). The slap of Arius: "a late story" (T-S17).

### How Nicholas's Influence Grew After Death (580–650 words)

Five links, in time order. One `<h3>` each is optional; plain paragraphs are fine at this length.

1. **A tomb people visited (6th–8th c.).** The Life of Nicholas of Sion (c. 564) mentions a visit to his tomb; Seal treats a visitable tomb as almost the only firm proof he existed (T-S20, tertiary: attribute to Seal). Eustratios of Constantinople (after 583) cites the officers' story (T-S20, T-S17). Justinian's church at Blachernae, 6th century (S5, T-S6). His image at Sinai and in Santa Maria Antiqua, Rome, before 800 (biography). The Andrew of Crete sermon at the grave calls him "pillar and support of the Church" (S5, PG 97 1191–1206). Use that phrase, not the ship's-pilot passage the biography already quotes. Theme: the oldest story is about strangers calling on him by name; the cult kept that shape.
2. **Thursday and the hymns of the East.** Thursday is kept for the apostles and Nicholas: "The fact that Saint Nicholas has been given a place in the weekly cycle indicates that great veneration that the Church accords him" (ROCOR talk, 1995, T-S5). Hymns to him "most every Thursday" in the Octoechos (T-S4). Say that the date and reason of this arrangement are unknown. Russia: veneration came "simultaneously with the acceptance of Christianity," and "There was no house or temple in the Russian land in which there was not an icon of Saint Nicholas the Wonderworker" (OCA, T-S2; label it a modern Orthodox statement). Name the wonderworking icons of Mozhaisk and Zaraisk.
3. **Bari, 1087–1089: a Western shrine for an Eastern saint.** One sentence on the theft (biography has it). Urban II lays the relics under the crypt altar, October 1089 (T-S8, T-S11); the Synod of Melfi invitation by Abbot Elias (T-S12). First Crusade pilgrims visited the relics (T-S11). Hayes: "economic developments, church politics and the demands of the First Crusade merged to render Nicholas an ideal patron for the city" (T-S13, abstract). The East split: Kiev took up the 9 May feast within a few years (T-S2, T-S9); the Greek Church did not, because it "regarded the loss of the relics of Saint Nicholas as a sad event" (OCA, T-S2). Nikodemos later wrote a Greek service for the translation (S18).
4. **Churches and patrons across the West.** CE: in Germany the cult "began already under Otto II, probably because his wife Theophano was a Grecian"; Bishop Reginald of Eichstätt (d. 991) wrote a verse Life (T-S6). From Bari, legends, plays, and church dedications spread across western Europe (Jones, via Hageman, T-S14). Patronages, short list: Greece, Russia, Lorraine, Moscow; sailors, merchants, travelers, children (T-S6). One clause on roots: the sailors' story and the pilgrims passing through Myra (S10, S5).
5. **The gift-giver.** The step from the dowry gold to a December custom. By 1163, a high feast at Janskerk, Utrecht (T-S7). A Swiss writer, Hospinian, as quoted by Seal: parents "convey secretly presents ... to their little sons and daughters who were taught to believe that they owed them to the kindness of St Nicholas" (T-S7; attribute "as quoted by Seal"). Naogeorgus's 16th-century German verse (T-S7). The Reformation stopped the public cult, but the folk gift-giving went on in parts of England, Germany, and the Low Countries (Jones, via Hageman, T-S14). CE 1911: in the US he "has become identified with Santa Claus" (T-S6). Close with Pearse: Santa Claus derives "at some remove" from the medieval legends (T-S18). Mention the dispute in one sentence: Jones says the New York figure was an invention of the late 1700s and early 1800s, "fostered largely by Washington Irving"; Hageman disputes it.

### Nicholas in the Church Today (220–270 words)

- Catholic: 6 December, "St Nicholas, Bishop," an optional memorial (T-S15). Point to the Roman Martyrology entry without re-quoting it (the biography quotes it). Note that it names only the officers' story (T-S16).
- Orthodox: 6 December, 9 May, every Thursday, and in the Russian Church 29 July, revived 2004 (S17, T-S5). Orthodox writers rank him after only the Mother of God and John the Baptist (S5; attribute).
- Bari as a meeting place: Dominicans keep the basilica since 1951; an Orthodox chapel in the crypt since 1966 (T-S8).
- Benedict XVI, 1 March 2009, at the handing of the Russian church in Bari to the Moscow Patriarchate: "The Russian people have never faltered in their love for this great Saint"; hope for Bari as "a natural bridge to the East" (T-S10).
- Patriarch Kirill, 22 May 2017, when a relic fragment came to Moscow and St Petersburg (22 May to 28 July): "We're divided by the calendar reform but still are not divided by the date on which we venerate St Nicholas and the delivery of the relics from Myra in Lycia to Bari" (T-S9).
- Pope Francis chose Bari for the 2018 prayer for peace with Middle East church leaders, as a "window into the East" (T-S8).
- End on the central claim: the saint who left no words is the one both Churches still pray to together.

## 6. Accuracy notes

- Every "saying" is a later author's text. Frame each: "in the Praxis," "Michael has him say." The Praxis English (Jones, 1978) is copyrighted: use our own English, as the biography does.
- Slap: the biography says "first in the 1300s," but notes-teachings could not confirm the 14th century. The essay says "a late story" and gives no century.
- Nicaea list date: "about 510–515" (Cioffari prints 515). Disputed authenticity; say so.
- Justinian's church: say "6th century." Butler's "about 430" conflicts and cannot be Justinian.
- Kiev and 9 May: Sanidopoulos credits the envoy Theodore; Kirill credits Metropolitan Ephraim II and a relic from Urban II. Say "within a few years" and "the Church of Kiev."
- Urban II consecrated the crypt in 1089, not "a year afterwards" as OCA says.
- Catechism: it does not name him. Do not use Jones's line that the Pope "removed" him in 1979; the 1969 rank change is unsourced.
- Proclus and Andrew of Crete: write "attributed to" or "credited to."
- The Francis letter date is cut off on the page; date only the 2018 meeting.
- Names: Nicholas of Myra, the Wonderworker; Ablabius; Nepotianus; Eichstätt; Metaphrastes.
