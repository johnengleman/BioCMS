This list gathers the miracles attributed to Saint Nicholas of Myra, with the source of each account and its status. Nicholas lived in the fourth century, long before the Church held formal inquiries into miracles, so none of these accounts was examined by a canonization process. Some are honored in the liturgy of the Catholic and Orthodox Churches, some are recorded by the shrine at Bari, and some are popular tradition. Each entry names the oldest source that the research found for it. The list makes no claim of scientific proof, and it does not dismiss any account.

During Nicholas’s life

Three innocent men saved at Byrra

The governor Eustathius condemned three innocent men of Myra to death. Nicholas ran to the place of execution at Byrra, took the sword from the executioner as it was raised, and set the men free. He then went to the governor, refused his greeting, named bribery as the real cause, and later forgave him at the request of three imperial officers. This is the oldest known story of Nicholas, from the Praxis de stratelatis, which scholars date anywhere from soon after Nicholas’s death to the late sixth century. Eustathius, the account says, was to receive more than two hundred pounds of silver for the executions. Status: popular and liturgical tradition; the oldest known story of Nicholas, with a topography of Myra that Cioffari finds reliable.

Source: Praxis de stratelatis, trans. Charles W. Jones; Gerardo Cioffari, “Nicholas of Patara, Bishop of Myra”.

Three generals saved through dreams, Constantinople

The three officers who had been in Myra at the time, Nepotianus, Ursus, and Eupoleonis, were later framed in Constantinople and condemned. They prayed to the God of Saint Nicholas. That night Nicholas appeared in a dream to the emperor Constantine and to the prefect Ablabius, named himself a sinner and the bishop of Myra, and the emperor set the officers free. In the Praxis the prefect Ablabius had been promised seventeen hundred pounds of gold to destroy them, and Nicholas ordered the emperor to free the three men. The story is in the Praxis de stratelatis, in a sermon attributed to Proclus of Constantinople (which scholars doubt he wrote), and in a fragment quoted by Eustratios of Constantinople in the late sixth century. Status: liturgical and popular tradition; the older Roman Martyrology singles out this appearance to Constantine as the miracle to remember on his feast; the frame (Nepotianus and Ablabius) is historical according to Cioffari.

Source: Praxis de stratelatis, trans. Jones; Proclus (attributed), Encomium on Saint Nicholas; Roger Pearse, “Literary Development of the Life of St Nicholas”.

Gold for three daughters

A man in Nicholas’s city had three daughters and no dowry for them. Nicholas threw a bag of gold through his window at night, then a second, and on the third night the father stayed awake and caught him. Nicholas made him swear to tell no one. The first full telling is in the Life by Michael the Archimandrite (early ninth century), but Cioffari reports three very old versions: in the one that seems oldest there are two girls, and the boy Nicholas takes the money for them from his own living parents, and another version has four girls. Status: popular and liturgical tradition; the source of Nicholas’s patronage of gift-giving.

Source: Michael the Archimandrite, Life, secs. 10–18; Cioffari, “Nicholas of Patara”.

Grain ships at Andriake

During a famine at Myra, Alexandrian grain ships bound for Constantinople lay at Andriake. Nicholas asked each to unload a hundred measures and promised that they would suffer no loss. Michael says that the cargo was found unchanged at Constantinople, and that the grain fed Myra for two years. A sermon attributed to Andrew of Crete (early eighth century) adds that Nicholas stood among the sailors and left three gold coins on the ship. Status: popular tradition; Cioffari calls the story generic, notes that Charles Jones places it in circulation by the sixth century, and thinks that Nicholas, if anything happened, was only indirectly involved.

Source: Michael the Archimandrite, Life, secs. 37–39; Andrew of Crete, Encomium; Cioffari.

Sailors in a storm who called his name

Sailors caught in a storm called on Nicholas. He appeared among them and said, “Behold, you have called me, and I am here to help you,” and they were saved. Later they found him in the church at Myra, dressed like one of the many clergy, and recognized him at once; he told them that their greed and lust invited God’s correction. Status: popular tradition and the origin of his patronage of sailors. Cioffari thinks the story fits Nicholas of Sion better, but it stands in Michael’s Life of the bishop of Myra.

Source: Michael the Archimandrite, Life, secs. 34–36; Cioffari.

The temple of Artemis at Myra

Michael writes that Nicholas tore down the temple of Artemis at Myra, the temple that surpassed every other building in height and ornament, and that the demons who lived in it fled. Ancient inscriptions from Myra support the size and fame of the temple, according to Cioffari. Status: popular tradition.

Source: Michael the Archimandrite, Life, secs. 28–29; Cioffari.

After Nicholas’s death

Fragrant oil from the tomb at Myra

Michael writes that after Nicholas was buried in his church, his body gave off a sweet-smelling perfume. The Golden Legend (1275) says that a fountain of oil sprang from his head to his feet and that oil still flowed from his body. Status: a phenomenon reported for centuries at Myra and later at Bari.

Source: Michael the Archimandrite, Life, sec. 41; Jacobus de Voragine, The Golden Legend.

The demon’s flask of oil

Michael’s Life tells, among the wonders after Nicholas’s death, of pilgrims bound for Myra. A demon that Nicholas had driven from the temple of Artemis took the form of a woman and gave them a flask of what looked like oil, to pour on the lamps at his shrine. In the night Nicholas appeared to one of the men and told him to throw it into the sea. When he did, flame and smoke rose from the water and the ship was thrown into danger, and then they were rescued. Status: popular tradition.

Source: Michael the Archimandrite, Life, secs. 44–48.

The vial that did not break, Myra, April 1087

When men from Bari came to take the relics in April 1087, a Bari priest named Lupus dropped a glass vial of the saint’s oil on the stone floor of the church, and it did not break. The sailors took it as the saint’s consent. Status: popular tradition, told in the Greek Anonymous account (where the priest is Lupus) and by Nicephorus of Bari.

Source: Greek Anonymous account of the Translation, trans. McGinley and Musurillo; Nicephorus of Bari, Translation of St Nicholas, trans. Jones.

The coffin full of liquid, Myra, 1087

When Matthew of Bari broke open the tomb, the coffin was found full of liquid up to the navel of the body, and a fragrance rose so strong that, as Nicephorus tells it, it reached the men at the ships nearly three miles away. Nicephorus of Bari, whose account is thought to have been written within a week of the relics’ arrival at Bari, records it. Status: recorded by an account written within days of the event.

Source: Nicephorus of Bari, Translation of St Nicholas, trans. Jones.

Forty-seven cured in the first night and day, Bari, 9 May 1087

On the night the relics arrived in Bari and the day that followed, forty-seven people were cured, according to Nicephorus and the Greek Anonymous account. Butler gives the number as thirty, cured on the first day. Status: recorded in the accounts of the translation.

Source: Nicephorus of Bari; Greek Anonymous account; Alban Butler, Lives of the Saints, 6 Dec. (1866 ed.), vol. 12, pp. 91–94.

A young man possessed for five years, Bari, May 1087

Among the cures of the first week at Bari, Nicephorus records a young man who had been possessed for five years and was deaf, mute, and blind. He was healed on the Thursday after the relics arrived, the same day, Nicephorus says, that the saint had a vision told to a monk, promising a great miracle before he departed. Status: recorded in an account written in 1087.

Source: Nicephorus of Bari, Translation of St Nicholas, trans. Jones.

The manna of Bari

A fragrant liquid has been reported at the tomb in Bari since the translation. Butler cites a law of the emperor Manuel, reported by Balsamon, that mentions “a fragrant unctuous matter” at Bari. A modern Orthodox compilation states that the liquid still flows after the relics were re-examined and placed in a new tomb in 1953–1957. Status: recorded by the shrine; not independently examined in the sources read.

Source: Alban Butler, Lives of the Saints, 6 Dec. (1866 ed.), vol. 12, pp. 91–94; “History of the Veneration of Saint Nicholas of Myra,” Mystagogy Resource Center.

Stories later told of him that belong to Nicholas of Sion

From about the tenth century, writers merged the Life of Nicholas of Myra with that of Nicholas, abbot of Holy Sion, who died in the sixth century. The accounts below are often told of the bishop of Myra, but they come from the abbot’s Life.

The storm calmed on the pilgrimage to Jerusalem

The Vita Compilata, a ninth- or tenth-century merger of the two Lives, tells of a voyage to Jerusalem on a ship from Ascalon whose captain was named Mena. Symeon Metaphrastes added the calming of the storm on such a voyage. The episode belongs to Nicholas of Sion; Michael’s Life of the bishop has no storm on a pilgrimage. Status: popular tradition, attached to the wrong Nicholas.

Source: Vita Compilata, sec. 21; Pearse, “Literary Development”.

The sailor Ammonios raised from death

Symeon Metaphrastes, in the tenth century, added the raising of a sailor named Ammonios who had died on the voyage. Like the storm, it comes from the tradition of Nicholas of Sion. Status: popular tradition, attached to the wrong Nicholas.

Source: Pearse, “Literary Development”.
