A Prisoner Who Laughed in Chains

In 1202 or 1203, in the war between Perugia and Assisi, a band of young men from Assisi was captured and held in Perugia for a year. One of them puzzled the others. He was the son of a rich cloth merchant, used to fine clothes and to being first among his friends, and in prison he made jokes about his chains. At last one of his fellow prisoners told him he was mad. “What think ye of me?” he answered. “I shall be worshipped by the whole world.” There was one knight among the prisoners whom all the rest avoided, and the merchant’s son would not avoid him.

It sounded like the boast of a spoiled young man. Yet little more than twenty-five years later, a pope came to Assisi, wept as he preached on that young man’s life, declared him a saint, and kissed his tomb. In France, his first biographer wrote within a year of that day, the king and queen and all the great lords “ran to kiss and worship the bolster which Saint Francis had used in his sickness.” Eight hundred years after his death, the Catholic Church has given a whole year, from January 2026 to January 2027, to his memory.

The boast came true, by a road that no one in that prison would have guessed. Francis of Assisi won the honor of the world by going down. He got off his horse to kiss the hand of a leper. In front of the bishop he gave his clothes back to his father and stood there naked. He lived by begging, and he died on the bare ground in a habit that had been lent to him, so that he owned nothing at all. Two years before his death, on a mountain called La Verna, marks like the wounds of nails appeared in his hands and feet, and he did everything he could to hide them.

How did a merchant’s son who wanted glory come to want only what he called following the footsteps and the poverty of Christ? And why did the world honor him for it? He gave his own answer at the end of his life, in one sentence about lepers: what had seemed bitter to him, he wrote, was turned into sweetness. The story of how that happened begins in Assisi, in the house of a cloth merchant named Pietro di Bernardone, whose wife had their son baptized John.

The Cloth Merchant’s Son

The boy was born around 1182, and the name the world remembers, Francis, came to him later. He grew up in his father’s trade and was good at it. Thomas of Celano, who wrote the first Life of Francis within three years of his death, calls him “a shrewd trader but a most ostentatious spender,” and the leader of his friends in all their follies. Three of his companions remembered a young man “by nature joyous and merry,” who dressed richly, loved songs, and loved the French language. Long after he had given up everything else, he would still sing God’s praises in French, and even beg in it.

The year in prison ended, and a long illness followed it. When he could go out again, he walked into the countryside leaning on a stick and found that the fields, which had always delighted him, gave him no pleasure at all. Then a new plan took hold of him. A nobleman was getting ready to ride to Apulia, and Francis decided to go with him and win a knighthood. Before he set out he met a knight who had fallen into poverty, and he gave the man his own new clothes.

He set out telling his friends that he was going to be “a great prince.” He got as far as Spoleto. There, in the night, a voice asked him where he was going, and when he said he was going to war in Apulia, it asked him who could do better for him, the servant or the lord. “The lord,” said Francis. “Why, then,” the voice answered, “art thou seeking the servant instead of the lord?” The young man who had ridden out to become a knight turned around and went home.

Back in Assisi, his friends chose him as master of their revels, Celano says, because they knew he would pay for the feast. When the company went out afterwards, he walked behind them with a staff in his hand, then fell behind altogether. A great sweetness had come over him, so strong, as he himself told it later, that he could not speak or move from the spot. His friends asked whether he was thinking of taking a wife. “I will marry a nobler and fairer bride than you ever saw,” he answered, “who shall surpass all others in beauty and excel them in wisdom.”

He did not tell them who she was. In Assisi, painters would one day put the wedding on a wall, and they gave the bride her name. The fresco is called the Marriage of St. Francis to Poverty.

What Was Bitter Turned Sweet

For a time he tried to understand in secret what was happening to him. He took to praying in a cave outside Assisi while a friend waited for him nearby. He went to Rome as a pilgrim, and at St. Peter’s, seeing how small the offerings were, he flung his purse through the grating. Then he changed clothes with a beggar and sat among the poor at the door of the church, begging in French and eating with them. Celano adds that he would gladly have done the same at home, but was ashamed to do it before those who knew him.

One thing he still could not bear. Lepers filled him with horror, and Celano says he used to hold his nose against them from two miles off. One day, riding on horseback, he met a leper. He got down from his horse, gave the man money, and kissed his hand, and the leper gave him the kiss of peace. Soon afterward he went to the house where the lepers lived and gave alms to each one of them. At the end of his life, in the Testament he left his brothers, this was the first thing he remembered:

“The Lord gave me, Brother Francis, to begin to do penance in this way. When I was in sins, it seemed too bitter to me to see lepers. The Lord himself led me among them, and I showed them mercy. And when I left them, what had seemed bitter to me turned into sweetness of soul and body.”

— Francis of Assisi, Testament, 1226 (our translation from the Latin)

That sentence holds his whole life in small. What he had hated became sweet to him, and he gave the credit to the Lord: the Lord gave, the Lord led. His next sentence is shorter still: “And afterward I stayed a little while and left the world.” The sweetness, though, did not yet tell him what to do. The early Lives say that came from a crucifix.

Around 1205 or 1206, Francis went into the old church of San Damiano to pray. The building was falling into ruin, and one poor priest served it. Celano’s first Life tells no more than that. The three companions, writing in 1246, tell what happened next: the painted figure of Christ on the cross spoke to him. “Francis, seest thou not that My House is being destroyed? Go therefore, and repair Me it.” In Celano’s second Life the image moved its lips and called him by name, and from that hour Francis could not stop weeping for the Passion of Christ. He “filled the ways with groanings,” Celano says.

He took the words exactly as they sounded. Here was a church falling down, and he would build it up again. He went to Foligno, sold cloth there, sold his horse as well, and brought the money back to San Damiano. The priest, afraid of Pietro di Bernardone, would not take it, so Francis threw it on a window sill, “heeding it as little as dust.” Then he hid from his father’s anger for about a month in a pit. When at last he walked back into Assisi, the townspeople who had known the merchant’s elegant son threw mud and stones at him and called him mad. Pietro dragged him home and chained him in a dark place. While her husband was away, his mother set him free.

Pietro came home, found his son gone, and went to the law.

Naked Before the Bishop

The consuls of Assisi would not take the case. Francis, they told the angry father, “hath gone out of our jurisdiction.” So Pietro went to the bishop of Assisi, Guido. When the summons came, Francis answered it gladly: “Unto the lord Bishop will I come, for he is the father and lord of souls.”

The bishop told him that the money belonged to his father and that he must give it back. Francis went into a room of the bishop’s house. When he came out he was naked, carrying his clothes in his arms, and the people standing there saw a hair-shirt on his body. He gave back the money and the clothes together, and he said:

“Henceforth I may freely say ‘our Father who art in heaven,’ not any more ‘father Pietro di Bernardone,’ to whom lo! I not only restore the money but give up all my clothes.”

— Francis of Assisi, as remembered in Thomas of Celano’s Second Life, 1244–47 (tr. A. G. Ferrers Howell)

Some of those watching wept. The bishop covered him with his own mantle. The young man who had given his new clothes to a poor knight had now given away the last of them, and with them the name of his father.

He left the city with nothing and went on his way singing God’s praises in French. Robbers stopped him and demanded to know who he was. “I am a herald of the great King,” he said. “What is that to you?” They threw him into a pit of snow. “Lie there,” they told him, “thou clownish herald of God!” For some days he worked as a scullion in a monastery kitchen, and at Gubbio a friend gave him a tunic. After that he served among the lepers for a time, and then he went back to San Damiano, where he still had a church to rebuild.

He carried stones for its walls and begged oil for its lamp. Once, at a house where men were gambling, he was too ashamed to beg in front of them and turned away. Then he went back, confessed his shame to them aloud, and asked for the oil in French. Whenever the Spirit set him on fire, Celano says, his words came out in French, and that day, in front of everyone, he foretold in French that San Damiano would one day be a monastery of consecrated virgins. The priest fed him on syrups and sweets until Francis saw that a poor man must eat as the poor ate, and begged a bowl of scraps from door to door. When he sat down to it, the three companions say, “no rich syrup had he ever tasted aught so delightsome.”

His father cursed him whenever they met, so Francis hired a beggar to bless him every time it happened. One winter morning his brother saw him at prayer in rags, shivering with cold, and said to a man of the town, “Ask Francis to sell you now a pennyworth of sweat.” Francis answered with a smile. “Indeed I will sell my sweat very dear to my Lord.”

In the third year after his conversion he repaired another forsaken church, a little chapel of the Virgin Mary in the plain below Assisi called the Portiuncula. There, one day at Mass, he heard the Gospel in which Christ sends the twelve apostles out to preach and tells them to take nothing with them. Afterward he asked the priest to explain it. When he understood, he cried out, in Celano’s words, “This is what I wish, this is what I am seeking, this I long with all my inmost heart to do.” He took off his shoes, laid down his staff, threw away his leather belt, and tied a cord around his waist instead. From then on he greeted everyone he met with the words, “The Lord give thee peace.” Soon men of Assisi began to come and ask to live as he did.

The Book Opened Three Times

Bernard and a man named Peter came first. The three of them went to the church of St. Nicholas to ask God what to do, and Francis opened the Gospel book three times. The first time it fell open at Christ’s words to the rich young man, to sell what he had and give to the poor. Next came the command to take nothing for the road, and then the call to deny oneself. “This is our life and Rule,” Francis said. Bernard sold everything he owned and gave it to the poor.

Giles came next, and others after him. When they were still a handful of poor men, Francis told them what he saw ahead: “Frenchmen are coming, Spaniards hastening, Germans and English running.” He sent them out in pairs with one instruction, “Cast thy thought on the Lord and He will nourish thee.” On the roads they were laughed at; people pushed dice into their hands and pulled their hoods from behind. Even Bishop Guido told Francis plainly that their way of life seemed to him “hard and harsh.”

Around 1209 or 1210, Francis took his brothers to Rome to ask Pope Innocent III to approve the way they lived. A cardinal urged Francis to join one of the existing monastic orders, and then became the brothers’ advocate. The Pope did not say yes at once. “Go, my son,” he told Francis, “and pray God that He reveal unto thee…” Francis went, and came back with a parable about a poor woman and a king. The three companions add that Innocent had dreamed that the Lateran basilica was leaning as if to fall, and that a small religious man was holding it up with his back. In 1210 the Pope approved their life by word of mouth. Francis told it later in the plainest terms:

“The Most High himself showed me that I should live according to the form of the holy Gospel. And I had it written in a few words and simply, and the lord pope confirmed it for me.”

— Francis of Assisi, Testament, 1226 (our translation from the Latin)

The few words were enough to live by. Within two years, the prophecy he had called out in French at San Damiano began to come true.

A Door Walled Up with Stones

In Assisi lived a girl called Clare. She was about eighteen and beautiful, and her family wanted her married. She had heard of Francis and wanted to hear him, and Francis, who had heard of her, was no less eager to speak with her. So she began to slip out of her father’s house in secret to meet him. A friend named Bona di Guelfuccio went with her many times, and remembered long afterward that he always urged Clare to turn to Jesus Christ. Clare’s first biographer puts it in one sentence: “To her his words seemed a flame and his deeds more than human.”

When she asked him what to do, Francis gave her a plan. On Palm Sunday she was to go to the blessing of the palms with everyone else, “dressed out and adorned,” and on the night after, she was to leave the town. The Sunday came, and Clare went into the church in her best clothes among the crowd of women. When the others pressed forward to receive their palms, she stayed in her place, and Bishop Guido came down the steps himself and put a palm in her hands. That night she did not go out by the ordinary door. She went to another one, which had been blocked up with a mass of beams and stones, and she broke it open, her biographer says, “with a strength that astonished herself.”

Down in the plain, at the Portiuncula, the brothers were keeping vigil at the little altar with lighted torches. They received her there, and before that altar her hair was cut off. It was 1212, by the usual reckoning. Bona was not there to see it; she was in Rome for Lent. Francis took Clare to the nuns of San Paolo, and when her relatives came after her, she held on to the altar cloths and uncovered her shorn head. Soon afterward, on Francis’s advice, she went to San Damiano, the church he had rebuilt with his own hands, and she stayed there for the rest of her life, some forty-two years.

After three years, one of her sisters later testified, it was at Francis’s prayers and insistence that Clare accepted the government of the community; he all but compelled her. When she ate nothing at all on three days of the week and fell ill from it, he and the bishop ordered her to eat at least half a small loaf on those days. Yet he visited the sisters seldom, and some of the brothers wondered why. “Think not, dear brethren,” he answered, “that I love them not perfectly; for if it were a fault to cherish them in Christ, was it not a greater fault to have united them to Christ?” Once, when he was finally persuaded to preach to them, he sat down in silence inside a circle of ashes, put the rest of the ashes on his head, recited the psalm “Have mercy on me, O God,” and walked out. The sisters wept.

Shortly before he died he wrote to them one last time. Clare copied the words into the Rule she wrote for her sisters, and that is how they have come down to us:

“I, little Brother Francis, want to follow the life and poverty of our most high Lord Jesus Christ and of his most holy mother, and to hold to it to the end. And I ask you, my ladies, and I give you counsel, that you live always in this most holy life and poverty.”

— Francis of Assisi, last will for Clare and her sisters, 1226 (our translation from the Latin)

Women were following him now as well as men. Four years after that Palm Sunday, a visiting churchman wrote down what he had seen of them.

A Sermon to the Birds

In July 1216 a priest named Jacques de Vitry, newly chosen bishop of Acre in the Holy Land, came to the papal court at Perugia and found Pope Innocent III dead and not yet buried. Thieves had stripped the body in the night, and the Pope who had once dreamed of a small religious man holding up the Lateran lay almost naked in the church. In all that region, he wrote that October, he had found one comfort. Men and women, many of them rich and worldly, had left everything for Christ, and they were called the Friars Minor, the lesser brothers. By day they went into the towns and villages to draw souls away from the world, and at night they went back to their hermitages to pray. Jacques did not name Francis. Within a few years he would see him for himself.

The Rule Francis wrote in 1221 shows that life in rough detail. The brothers, he wrote, “ought to rejoice when they live among people who are lowly and despised, among the poor and the weak, the sick, the lepers, and beggars by the roadside.” They were to own nothing and to look after one another as family. One night a brother cried out in the dark, “I am dying, brethren, see, I am dying of hunger!” Francis got up at once, had the table laid with whatever rough food there was, and began to eat first, so that the starving brother would not be ashamed to eat alone. “Let the charity be your example, not the food,” he told them afterward.

Near Bevagna, Celano says, Francis came upon a great gathering of birds, doves and rooks and others. He went in among them, and they did not fly away; he even touched them with his tunic as he walked. Then he preached to them:

“My brother birds, much ought ye to praise your Creator, and ever to love Him who has given you feathers for clothing, wings for flight, and all that ye had need of.”

— Francis of Assisi, in Thomas of Celano’s First Life, 1228–29 (tr. A. G. Ferrers Howell)

The birds listened and spread their wings. At Alviano the swallows kept up their noise while he tried to preach, so he turned to them first. “My sisters, the swallows, it is now time for me to speak too, because you have been saying enough all this time. Listen to the word of God and be in silence, and quiet, until the sermon is finished!” They were quiet. He picked worms up off the road so that they would not be trodden on, Celano remembered, and in winter he had honey and the best wine set out for the bees, so that they would not die of cold.

He could be as hard on himself as he was gentle with the birds. Once, after eating some chicken, he had himself dragged through the streets of Assisi by a rope. “Come, look at the glutton who has been battening on poultry that he has been eating when you did not know it!” he called out. Another time, at the hermitage of Poggio near Christmas, he told a large crowd that had come to hear a saint that he had something to confess: “during all this fast I have been eating food seasoned with lard.”

Francis tried more than once to carry the Gospel far beyond Umbria. A ship bound for Syria was driven off course by contrary winds, and a voyage toward Morocco ended in Spain, where illness turned him back. In 1219 he tried again, and this time he reached a war.

A Friar Before the Sultan

In 1219, the thirteenth year after his conversion, Francis crossed the sea. He left two brothers in Italy as his vicars, and he took Peter Cathanii with him. The crusader army was camped before the city of Damietta in Egypt, and in the summer the soldiers were preparing to attack. Francis heard of it and was grieved. “If they fight on such a day the Lord hath showed me that the Christians will not prosper,” he told his companion. “But if I tell them this I shall be deemed a fool, while if I keep silence I shall not elude my conscience. What, then, thinkest thou?” His companion’s answer was blunt: “Make no account of being judged by men, for thou art not now first beginning to be deemed a fool.”

So Francis warned the army, and the army laughed at him. On the day of the battle, in suspense, he sent his companion up to look out. Twice there was nothing to see. The third time, the Christian army was in full flight. Celano counts six thousand killed and captured, and he says Francis mourned most of all for the Spaniards, whose greater daring in the fight had left only a few of them alive.

In those same months he did something stranger. Jacques de Vitry, the bishop who had admired the brothers in Perugia, was with the army, and he wrote it down. Coming to the camp before Damietta, he says, Francis was so carried away by the fervor of his spirit that he went over to the Sultan’s camp without fear, armed with the shield of faith. On the road the Saracens seized him. “Ego sum Christianus,” he said, “ducite me ad Dominum vestrum”: I am a Christian; take me to your lord. Jacques tells what followed:

“When they had dragged him before him, the cruel beast, seeing him, was turned to gentleness at the sight of the man of God, and for some days listened most attentively as he preached the faith of Christ to him and his men. At last, afraid that some of his army would be converted to the Lord by the power of his words and go over to the Christian army, he had him led back to our camp with all reverence and safety, and said to him at the end: ‘Pray for me, that God may deign to reveal to me the law and faith that pleases him more.’”

— Jacques de Vitry, bishop of Acre, who was at Damietta, about 1220–25 (our translation from the Latin)

The Sultan of Egypt was Malik-el-Kamil, as Pope Francis named him in 2020. Jordan of Giano, who wrote down the brothers’ memories, adds that on the way Francis was beaten, and that, not knowing their language, he kept shouting through the blows, “Soldan, Soldan!” Celano says the Sultan received him with great honor, offered him gifts, and “gazed on him as a man unlike almost all men.” Some forty years later Bonaventure has Francis offer to walk into a fire with the Sultan’s priests to prove his faith. Jacques, who was there, says nothing of any fire, and nothing of the Sultan wishing to become a Christian in secret, a story the Little Flowers tells a century later. What the bishop in the camp set down was a man who walked into the enemy camp, preached for days with little result, and was sent home with honor and a request for prayer.

Jacques admired the brothers, but not without fear. That spring he wrote that their way of life seemed to him very dangerous, because untested young men were being sent out two by two, and several men of his own household had already joined them. Celano, for his part, says the Lord did not give Francis what he longed for there, but kept in reserve “the privilege of an unparalleled favour.” Readers have taken that to be the stigmata, five years away, on a mountain.

On the road home Francis was worn out, and for a while he rode an ass while Brother Leonard of Assisi walked behind. Leonard was tired too, and a sour thought came to him: “This man’s parents and mine were not wont to play together as equals. But now he is riding, and I on foot am driving his ass!” Francis got down at once. “No, brother,” he said, “it is not meet that I should ride and thou be walking, because in the world thou wast nobler and mightier than I.” Leonard turned red, fell at his feet in tears, and confessed what he had been thinking.

By then a messenger had already crossed the sea to find Francis. At home, the brothers were falling apart.

A Tug on Elias’s Tunic

The messenger, a lay brother, told Francis what had happened in Italy. The two vicars had held a chapter and added to the Rule: new fasts, and no meat even on days when meat was allowed, unless someone offered it. Jordan of Giano tells what Francis did. Meat had been set before him and Peter Cathanii, and Francis turned to Peter, whom he always called “lord” because Peter was learned and noble. “Lord Peter, what shall we do?” “Ah, lord Francis, what pleases you, for you have the authority.” “Let us eat, then,” said Francis, “according to the Gospel, what is set before us.”

There was worse news. A woman over the sea who had the gift of prophecy was telling the brothers, “Go back, go back, because through the absence of Brother Francis the Order is troubled, split and scattered.” And this, Jordan adds, was true. One brother had gone behind Francis’s back to get letters from the Holy See; another had gathered a crowd of lepers and set out to found an order of his own.

Celano says that in these years Francis dreamed of a little black hen with feathered legs and countless chicks, which pressed round her but could not all fit under her wings. When he woke he explained it himself. “I am this hen,” he said, “small in stature and black by nature.” The chicks were the brothers, too many now for his strength to defend. He would entrust them to the Church of Rome.

So when he came home, with Elias and Peter Cathanii, he did not go first to the brothers who were causing trouble. He went to Pope Honorius III. He lay down at the Pope’s door, not daring to knock on the room of so great a prince, and waited until the Pope came out. “Father Pope, God give you peace.” “God bless you, son.” Then Francis made his request. A pope was busy with great affairs, he said, and the poor could often not get near him. “You have given me many popes; give me one I can speak to when I need to, who will hear and settle the affairs of my Order in your place.” “Whom do you want me to give you, son?” “The Lord of Ostia.” It was Cardinal Hugolino, and the Pope granted him. Hugolino cancelled the letters and sent the would-be founder away in disgrace. The brothers had heard that Francis was dead; when they learned he was home, Jordan says, it seemed to them that a new light had risen.

Francis did not take back the government of the Order. At a chapter at Michaelmas in 1220 he gave it away. “Henceforth I am dead to you,” he said; “but here is brother Peter Cathanii, and let you and me all obey him.” He bowed down before Peter and promised him obedience, and the brothers wept aloud as if they had been orphaned. Then he prayed: “O Lord, I commend to Thee the family that Thou hast hitherto committed to me.” Peter died the following March, and Brother Elias took his place.

At Pentecost in 1221 the brothers gathered again at the Portiuncula, and Jordan was there. He reckoned that about three thousand came; the Mirror of Perfection, compiled a century later, says five thousand. They lived under shelters in a wide fenced field. The townspeople brought so much bread and wine that after seven days the brothers had to shut the gate. Jordan never saw another chapter like it.

Francis was weak, so Elias spoke to the chapter for him, and Francis sat at his feet. Near the end, Francis remembered that no brothers had yet settled in Germany; those sent before had been badly treated. He tugged at Elias’s tunic. Elias bent down, listened, straightened up, and announced: “Brothers, thus says the Brother.” That was what they called Francis, simply the Brother. He asked who would go, and about ninety brothers rose, ready to die there. Jordan had no wish to go. He was afraid of the Germans, and when one of the volunteers told him he belonged with them, he protested, “I am not of yours; I came to know you, not to go with you.” Elias ordered him to decide. “I want neither to go nor to stay,” Jordan said, “but whatever you command me, I will do.” Elias sent him. Forty years later he told it himself: “This is Brother Jordan of Giano, who writes this to you, who came to Germany by such a chance.” In the same company, crossing the Alps, went Thomas of Celano, who would write the first Life of Francis.

The founder who sat at another man’s feet could still hold a city. On 15 August 1222 a student named Thomas of Spalato heard him preach in the main square of Bologna, where almost the whole city had come together. Everything he said, Thomas remembered, was aimed at ending feuds, and noble families that had shed one another’s blood for years made peace. People pushed toward him to tear off a scrap of his habit. Thomas also set down what he looked like. His habit was dirty, he wrote, his person unimpressive, and his face plain.

He no longer governed the brothers. He still meant to show them, without words when he could, what a lesser brother was.

Sitting in the Ashes

One Easter at the hermitage of Greccio he found that the brothers had laid the table for the feast with white cloths and glass. He slipped out, borrowed a poor man’s hat and staff, and came back to the door as a pilgrim, begging alms “for the love of the Lord God” for a poor sick stranger. The brothers invited him in. He asked for a dish and sat down alone on the ground by the fire, with the ashes for his table. “Now I am sitting like a Lesser Brother,” he said, and then, to the brothers: “I saw a table spread and adorned, and knew that it was not the table of poor men who go from door to door.”

He did the same to a cardinal. Dining with Hugolino, he slipped out at the dinner hour to beg, came back with scraps of black bread, and shared them out among the knights and chaplains at the cardinal’s table. Afterward Hugolino embraced him. “My brother, why hast thou put me to shame in the house which is thine and thy brethren’s, by going for alms?” Francis was not sorry. “I have rather shown you honour,” he said. “For I have a royal dignity and a preeminent nobility, to follow that Lord, who, though He was rich, for our sakes became poor.” Even at the barber’s he had a point to make. “Take care not to make me a large tonsure,” he would say. “For I wish that my simple brethren may have a share in my head.”

On 29 November 1223 Pope Honorius III confirmed the final Rule in a bull that begins Solet annuere, and the original was long kept at the Sacro Convento in Assisi. The Rule’s words on poverty are short: “The brothers shall make nothing their own, not house, nor place, nor anything,” because “the Lord made himself poor for us in this world.” Francis had wanted one more line, Celano says: that the Holy Spirit, the Minister General of the Order, rests equally on the poor and simple. The bull had already been sealed.

He was not always gentle. When some of the Order’s leaders steered it toward the ways of the older orders, he raised himself up in his sickbed and burst out: “Who are they that have snatched the Religion of myself and the brethren out of my hands? If I get to the chapter-general then I will show them what my will is!” He wrote to the whole Order that he would not count as his brothers those who refused to pray the Office as the Rule required, or who wandered outside its discipline. In the same letter he confessed that he himself had not kept the Rule as he should, through negligence, or illness, or because he was unlettered. To one minister he wrote words of a different kind. Whatever stood in his way, even if it came from the brothers, he must treat as grace, and let it be for him “more than a hermitage.” No brother, however much he had sinned, should leave his presence without mercy, if he asked for mercy. “If he does not ask,” Francis added, “you ask him.”

He wrote to the whole world as well, and addressed the letter to everyone: “To all Christians, religious, clerics and lay people, men and women, all who live in the whole world, brother Francis, their servant and subject.” The young prisoner who had said the whole world would honor him now wrote to it as its servant.

Late in 1223, with Christmas coming, he had an idea for Greccio.

A New Bethlehem at Greccio

Fifteen days before Christmas, in the third year before his death, Francis sent for a man of Greccio named John and told him what he wanted to do:

“I would make memorial of that Child who was born in Bethlehem, and in some sort behold with bodily eyes His infant hardships; how He lay in a manger on the hay, with the ox and the ass standing by.”

— Francis of Assisi, in Thomas of Celano’s First Life, 1228–29 (tr. A. G. Ferrers Howell)

Bonaventure adds that Francis first asked the Pope’s permission. On Christmas night there were torches, an ox and an ass, and a manger full of hay, and Mass was sung over the manger. Francis, in a deacon’s vestments, sang the Gospel and then preached to the people. When he said “Bethlehem,” Celano remembers, it came out like the bleating of a sheep, and whenever he said the name of Jesus he licked his lips.

One man there saw a vision. A child lay lifeless in the manger, and Francis came and woke it. Celano does not name the man; Bonaventure says it was John of Greccio himself. The hay from the manger was kept afterwards, Celano says, and it cured sick animals and helped women in hard labor. Greccio, he wrote, was made a new Bethlehem. Eight hundred years later, the Holy See would call that night the first Nativity scene.

Christmas made him extravagant. One year Christmas fell on a Friday, and there was talk of eating no meat. Francis would not hear of it. “Thou sinnest, brother, in calling the day on which a Child has been born to us Friday,” he said. “I would that the very walls should eat flesh on such a day, or, if they cannot, that they should at any rate be greased outside!”

The next summer he climbed a mountain called La Verna to keep a Lent of fasting, and he came down marked for the rest of his life.

The Seraph on La Verna

He went up in August 1224, two years before his death, with a few brothers, Leo among them, to fast from the feast of the Assumption until the feast of St. Michael at the end of September. Bonaventure says that Francis opened the Gospel book three times on the mountain, as he had done with Bernard at the beginning, and each time it opened at the Passion of Christ.

Then, about the feast of the Exaltation of the Holy Cross, as Bonaventure tells it, early in the morning, Francis saw a seraph with six wings, fiery, fixed to a cross. Celano, writing a few years after Francis’s death, gives no day, but tells what followed. Marks of nails began to appear in his hands and feet. A wound opened in his right side, and it bled so often that his tunic and drawers were many times sprinkled with blood.

Francis did everything he could to hide them. He rarely washed his hands, only the fingers, and his feet even more rarely; when someone asked to kiss his hand, he held out only his fingers, or his sleeve. When a companion saw the marks on his feet and asked, “What is this, good brother?” Francis answered, “Mind your own business.” When the same brother shook out his tunic and asked about the blood on it, Francis put his finger to his eye. “Ask what that is, if you don’t know it to be an eye.” Brother Pacifico, who addressed him as “dearest mother,” once kissed both his hands so that a curious friar could see them, and winked. Francis suspected what they had done and called him back. “God pardon thee, brother, for thou givest me much distress sometimes.” His rule, Bonaventure says, was Secretum meum mihi: my secret is mine.

On the mountain, Leo was in trouble of his own. He was struggling with a temptation, and Francis called for ink and parchment and wrote out for him the praises of God that he had been turning over in his heart. Celano says the temptation left Leo at once. Francis told him to keep the page until his death. It reads like a man running out of words for God:

“You are humility. You are patience. You are security. You are rest. You are gladness and joy. … You are our hope. You are our faith. You are our great sweetness. You are our eternal life, great and wonderful Lord, almighty God, merciful Savior.”

— Francis of Assisi, Praises of God, La Verna, 1224 (our translation from the Latin)

On the same parchment he wrote a blessing in the old words of the Book of Numbers, ending “The Lord bless you, Brother Leo,” and drew the Tau, the cross shaped like the letter T, so that it cut across Leo’s name. Leo kept the page and noted in its margin when it was made. It was in the Lent of St. Michael, two years before Francis’s death, after the vision of the seraph and the printing of the wounds of Christ in his body. It is kept at the Sacro Convento in Assisi, where the convent’s records have mentioned it since 1348.

Only one other piece of Francis’s own handwriting is known, and it too was written for Leo. It is a short letter with no date, kept in the cathedral of Spoleto, and in it Francis speaks to his friend like a mother:

“I say it to you, my son, as a mother would. … In whatever way seems best to you to please the Lord God and to follow his footsteps and his poverty, do it with the blessing of the Lord God and with my obedience. And if you need it for your soul, or for some other comfort, and you want, Leo, to come to me, come.”

— Francis of Assisi, Letter to Brother Leo, undated (our translation from the Latin)

He came down from La Verna with hands he kept covered. Around that time, at Foligno, Elias saw in a vision an aged priest dressed in white, who told him that eighteen years had passed since Francis’s conversion, and that he would live two more.

Brother Fire and Brother Sun

Those two years were years of pain. A disease of the eyes was taking his sight, and he rode from place to place on an ass. Elias and Cardinal Hugolino persuaded him to see doctors, and near Rieti a surgeon put his iron in the fire to cauterize the side of his face. Francis spoke to the fire. “My brother fire, who dost outvie all other things in splendour, the Most High hath created thee mighty, fair, and useful. Be kind to me at this hour, be courteous, for I have loved thee of old in the Lord.” He made the sign of the cross over it. The brothers, overcome with pity, fled, and the iron was drawn hissing from his ear to his eyebrow. When they came back he was smiling. “Faint-hearted and poor-spirited ones, wherefore did ye fly? I tell you of a truth I felt no heat of fire nor any pain in my flesh.” Then he turned to the surgeon: “If the flesh is not well burnt, apply the iron again.” The surgeon told the brothers he had seen wondrous things that day.

Four brothers looked after him, Leo, Angelo, Rufino, and Masseo. Sick and half blind, he wanted to start over and go back to serving the lepers. “Let us begin, brethren, to serve the Lord God,” he would say, “for hitherto we have profited little or nothing.”

One night, in great pain, he began to feel pity for himself, and prayed to Christ for patience. A voice asked him: if in exchange for his sufferings he were given a treasure so great that the whole earth turned to gold would be nothing beside it, would he not rejoice? “I should rejoice indeed,” he said, “yea, and above measure I should rejoice.” “Exult, then,” the voice answered, “for thy sickness is the earnest of My kingdom.” Then, Celano says, he made his “Praises concerning the Creatures.” A later collection places that night in a hut by San Damiano, where for sixty days he could not see the light, and mice ran over him and over his table as he ate. The old manuscript at Assisi agrees that he made the song while he lay ill at San Damiano. He made it in the Italian of Umbria, the language people spoke in the street, and its refrain is two words that would travel the world, Laudato si’, praised be:

“Most High, all-powerful, good Lord, yours are the praise, the glory, the honor and every blessing. … Praised be my Lord for our sister, mother earth, who sustains and governs us and brings forth many fruits with colored flowers and grass.”

— Francis of Assisi, Canticle of Brother Sun, San Damiano, about 1225 (our translation from the Umbrian Italian)

In those last months he asked a brother whether his conscience was right to trouble him over the care he now let others give his body. The brother asked how well that body had obeyed him. “I bear it witness, my son,” said Francis, “that it has been obedient in all things and has spared itself in nothing.” Then he spoke to it directly: “Rejoice, brother body, and forgive me, for behold now I gladly fulfil thy desires.”

In the spring of 1226, at Siena, he began to vomit blood. Elias came to him, and he was taken to Cortona, where his belly and legs began to swell. Probably that summer he set down his Testament, a last plea to keep things simple: “As the Lord gave me to say and write the Rule and these words simply and purely, so you should understand them simply and purely.” Then he asked to be taken home. Celano says Assisi sent messengers, formally appointed, to demand delivery of his person, so that the city would not “give their glory to another,” and when he came the people rejoiced.

Our Sister, Bodily Death

On the way home the knights from Assisi stopped at a poor village called Satriano, hungry, and found nothing there to buy. They came back to Francis and asked him to share his alms. “You find not because you trust more in your flies than in God,” he told them; flies were what he called coins. He sent them back round the same houses to beg for the love of God, and they came back with more than their money could have bought.

The last weeks are where memory and legend mix most, and much of what is told about them comes from the Mirror of Perfection, a collection of the brothers’ stories put together around 1318. In its telling, Francis lay in the bishop’s palace at Assisi while the bishop and the governor of the city were so bitterly at odds that the bishop had excommunicated the governor, and the governor had forbidden anyone to sell to the bishop. Francis added a verse about pardon to his song and sent two brothers to sing it in front of both men. The governor listened with his hands joined, in tears, and then said that “had any one slain my kinsman, or my son, him also I would forgive,” and fell at the bishop’s feet. The same book says the men of Assisi kept watch round the palace every night, afraid the brothers would carry his body off to another city, and that Francis had his praises of God sung day and night. When Elias worried what people would think of such gladness in a dying man, Francis reminded him of his own vision at Foligno: “Give me leave, brother, to rejoice in the Lord and in His praises and in mine own infirmities.”

Celano’s second Life is plainer. To the doctor Francis said: “Brother physician, boldly foretell that death is at hand, for it will be the gate of life to me.” And to death itself he said, “Welcome, my sister Death.” The Mirror says he called for Brother Angelo and Brother Leo to sing to him of Sister Death, and added a last verse to his song:

“Praised be my Lord for our sister, bodily death, from whom no living person can escape. Woe to those who die in mortal sins; blessed are those whom death finds in your most holy will, for the second death will do them no harm.”

— Francis of Assisi, Canticle of Brother Sun, verse on death, traditionally added in 1226 (our translation from the Umbrian Italian)

He asked to be carried down to the Portiuncula, and the Mirror adds that halfway there he had his litter turned toward Assisi and blessed the city. It tells too how the Lady Jacoba dei Settesoli came from Rome with ash-grey cloth and almond cakes just as the brothers were writing to ask her for them, and how Francis let her in.

At the Portiuncula he told the brothers, “See, my sons, that ye never desert this place. If you are driven out by one way, come back by another.” He crossed his arms so that his right hand rested on Elias, and blessed them all. He forgave them: “I remit all the offences and faults of my brethren as well absent as present, and so far as I may, I absolve them.” Then bread was brought, and he blessed it and gave each of them a piece.

Then he asked them to lay him naked on the bare ground, and to leave him there after he died “for so long a time as one might take to walk a mile gently.” The warden of the brothers brought a tunic, a pair of drawers, and a little cap of sackcloth to cover the scars of the cautery. He told Francis that they were only lent to him, under obedience, and that he had no power to give them away. Francis rejoiced. He would die owning nothing at all, not even the clothes he died in. With his left hand he covered the wound in his side. “I have done my duty,” he told the brothers, “may Christ teach you yours.”

He asked to hear the Gospel of John read, the passage that tells of the Passover. Then he began, as best he could, the psalm that opens, “I cried unto the Lord with my voice,” and he went on with it, Bonaventure says, until he came to the verse, “The righteous wait for me until you reward me.” On that Saturday evening, the Mirror says, a great flock of larks came down over the roof of the house and wheeled round it in a circle, singing. Francis died at nightfall on 3 October 1226. Elias, writing within days, dated it to Sunday, the fourth of October, “at the first hour of the night” before it.

One of the brothers saw his soul rise to heaven like a star, as large as the moon, carried on a white cloud. Far away, Brother Augustine, minister of the brothers in the Terra di Lavoro, who was dying and had long since lost his speech, suddenly cried out, “Wait for me, father, wait! See, I am now coming with thee!” and died.

Clare at the Little Window

Elias wrote at once to all the provinces of the Order. “Before I begin to speak, I sigh,” his letter begins, “for the fear that I feared has come upon me, and upon you.” They were orphans without a father, he wrote, robbed of the light of their eyes. Like a second Jacob, Francis had blessed all his sons before he was taken from them and forgiven every fault done or even thought against him, Elias added, by any of them. Then he announced something new:

“I announce to you a great joy and a new miracle. Such a sign has not been heard since the beginning of the world, except in the Son of God, who is Christ the Lord. Not long before his death our Brother and Father appeared as one crucified, carrying in his body five wounds, which are truly the stigmata of Christ.”

— Brother Elias, letter to the provinces of the Order, October 1226 (our translation from the Latin)

His hands and feet, Elias wrote, had what seemed the marks of nails driven through from both sides, showing the blackness of the nails, and his side looked as if a lance had pierced it, and often bled. In this first announcement there is no mountain, no seraph, and no date; those come from the Lives written later. Elias added what had happened to the body. While Francis lived, there was no beauty in his face, and no limb of his body was free of great suffering. Now he was beautiful to look at, shining with a wonderful whiteness, and his limbs, which had been stiff, had become soft and could be turned this way and that, like a child’s. Every priest was to say three Masses for him, every cleric the psalter, and every lay brother five Our Fathers. He signed it “Brother Elias, sinner.”

The day after his death the body was carried to Assisi in procession, with branches of olive. On the way they stopped at San Damiano. The coffin was opened at the little window through which the sisters received Communion, and Clare came with her sisters, Celano writes, “to look upon the father that spake not to them.” They cried out, “Father, father, what shall we do?”

In Assisi the crowds saw the marks for themselves. Celano says the black nails against the white flesh did not horrify those who looked; they added grace to the body, “even as little black stones in a white pavement are wont to do.” A knight of the town named Jerome, who doubted, moved the nails with his own fingers in front of his fellow citizens, Bonaventure says. Then he swore on the Gospels to what he had touched. The people of Assisi, Jordan of Giano remembered, were afraid that the men of Perugia would come and take the body by force, because of the wonders God had worked through it. So they laid him near their own walls, in the church of San Giorgio, where he had first learned his letters and where he had first begun to preach.

The sick began to come at once. On the very day of the burial, Celano says, a girl with a bent neck was healed beneath the coffin. A deaf and mute boy of Castel della Pieve spoke his first words: “Saint Francis lives.” Celano wrote all of these down because they were read aloud at Assisi two years after the funeral, in front of a pope who had been Francis’s friend.

The Pope Who Was His Friend

Cardinal Hugolino, the protector Francis had begged for at the papal door, was now Pope Gregory IX. In 1228 a revolt drove him out of Rome, and he came to Assisi, where the cardinals began to examine the miracles. Bonaventure says the Pope gave the task to the cardinals least in favor of the cause. At a last meeting at Perugia, as Celano records it, they declared that his life needed no miracles to prove it, for it was a life “which we have seen with our eyes, handled with our hands.”

On 16 July 1228 the Pope came to San Giorgio, where Francis lay. He preached on a text from Ecclesiasticus, “As the morning star amid vapour,” and he wept as he spoke. The miracles were read aloud and preached on, and the people, Celano says, were “greatly wearied by the suspense.” At last Gregory pronounced the decree: Francis was to “be numbered in the roll of saints, and that his festival be kept on the day of his death.” The Te Deum was sung, and the Pope kissed the tomb of the man who had once laughed in a Perugia prison. The bull that announced it to the Church, Mira circa nos, set the feast on the fourth of October and spoke of “many brilliant miracles” without naming one or mentioning the wounds. It speaks instead of the Pope himself, who now had in heaven “a patron whose friendship We enjoyed on earth.”

Elias was already building a great church for the body, on a hill once known as the Hill of Hell, which a papal letter now called the Hill of Paradise. On the eve of Pentecost, 25 May 1230, the body was to be carried there in a wooden chest on a cart drawn by oxen draped in purple. What happened that day is known mainly from the Franciscan annalist Wadding, writing four centuries later, and from an angry papal bull he printed. The citizens of Assisi turned out in force, with guards and soldiers, afraid that someone would steal their treasure. When the procession reached the church, Wadding writes, the city’s rulers held it back, and the bull says they seized the body in pride and tumult and would not let the friars honor it as they should. The Pope laid the church under penalties until the town made amends. Some friars blamed Elias; Wadding thought Elias may have hidden it from thieves. However it was done, the body was buried far down beneath the high altar, and no one knew the place anymore.

It stayed lost for nearly six hundred years. Searches in 1755 and 1802 failed, and another was halted when the French came. In 1818 Pope Pius VII allowed a new search, and after fifty-two nights of cutting through solid rock, on 12 December, they found a stone coffin of Roman travertine, caged in iron, under heavy slabs. Inside, according to a modern account of the excavation, lay the saint’s remains, with a rough stone under the head for a pillow. Beside them were a few coins, some small beads, and a silver ring set with a carnelian cut with the figure of Minerva. A crypt was built so that pilgrims could come down to him. In 1934 the remains of Leo, Rufino, Angelo, and Masseo, the brothers who had nursed him at the end, were moved into niches in the crypt wall, and the casket of the Lady Jacoba was set at the stairs that lead down to it.

Why the World Still Comes

In 1939 Pope Pius XII named Francis, with Catherine of Siena, patron of Italy, and in 1979 John Paul II named him patron of those who promote ecology. When a new pope was elected in March 2013, a cardinal embraced him in the conclave and told him not to forget the poor. He thought at once of Francis of Assisi, he said later, “the man of poverty, the man of peace, the man who loves and protects creation,” and he took his name. Two years later his encyclical on the care of creation took its title from the Canticle, and in 2020 he signed another, Fratelli tutti, “in Assisi, at the tomb of Saint Francis,” on the eve of the saint’s feast.

Stories gathered around Francis as they gather around few people. The Little Flowers, written down a century after his death, tells how he tamed a wolf at Gubbio and taught Leo that perfect joy was to be beaten at their own door and to bear it. These are legends, loved because they sound like him. The most famous prayer printed under his name, which asks God to make the one who prays an instrument of his peace, is not his at all; it first appeared, unsigned, in a Paris magazine in 1912. His own words are plainer. “Where there is love and wisdom,” he wrote, “there is neither fear nor ignorance.”

In the Year of St. Francis, from 21 February to 22 March 2026, his remains were brought up from the crypt into the lower church and shown to the public at length for the first time. More than 350,000 pilgrims came, the Franciscans reported. The young prisoner of Perugia had said the whole world would honor him, and it has. He had his own measure for that kind of honor. “As much as a person is before God,” he wrote, “so much he is, and no more.” The song he made near the end asks nothing for himself. Its last line turns to the reader:

“Praise and bless my Lord, give him thanks, and serve him with great humility.”

A Note on the Sources

The earliest document of Francis’s death is Elias’s letter, written within days; it announces the stigmata but names no mountain, seraph, or date, and the copy that survives is addressed to the minister of France. Celano wrote the first Life in 1228–29, but he had gone to Germany in 1221 and was probably not in Italy at the end, so his account of the last years is probably second-hand. The Three Companions (1246) and Celano’s Second Life (1244–47) add the voice from the crucifix, which the First Life does not mention, and the second dream at Spoleto. Bonaventure (1260–63) adds the day of the stigmata and the fire offered before the Sultan. The Mirror of Perfection (about 1318) and the Little Flowers (after 1322) are late, and the text marks their stories as such. For the chapter of 1221, Jordan of Giano, who was there, counts about three thousand brothers; the Mirror says five thousand.

Jacques de Vitry, who was with the crusaders at Damietta, gives the earliest account of the meeting with the Sultan that we read; he tells of no fire and no conversion. A French chronicle tells of two unnamed clerics who went from Damietta to preach to the Sultan, and its editor of 1871 took them for Francis and a companion and named the Sultan “Coradin” of Damascus, where Jacques says the Sultan of Egypt. Celano gives 16 July 1228 at Assisi for the canonization, and the English edition of the bull carries that date in its heading; the bull’s own dating clause, “at Perugia, on the fourteenth Kalends of August,” falls three days later. Elias dates the death to the first hour of the night before Sunday 4 October, which is nightfall on Saturday 3 October. Celano gives no day for the stigmata; Leo’s note says the Lent of St. Michael, Bonaventure “about the Feast of the Exaltation of Holy Cross,” and tradition 14 September. The body was moved on 25 May 1230 according to Wadding and the Catholic Encyclopedia’s article on Francis, on 21 May according to the 1868 Bonaventure, and a few days earlier according to its article on Elias, which makes 25 May the consecration of the lower church. Wadding prints the bull on the seizure, Speravimus, dated 17 May, before the translation, which cannot be right; the Key to Umbria gives 16 June. Wadding’s account was read in a scanned text, and the details of 1818 come from a modern account. Robinson (1906) is the source for the letter to Leo as the only other autograph and for placing the Testament in summer 1226.

Clare’s Legend gives no year for her flight; Paschal Robinson reckoned it 18 March 1212, while her sisters’ “about forty-two years” before 1253 points to about 1211. Their testimony was read only in Spanish translation. The words Solet annuere begin two bulls, Honorius III’s for Francis’s Rule in 1223 and Innocent IV’s for Clare’s in 1253. The letter to Leo is Epistola VI in the Quaracchi edition. Celano’s two Lives differ in their picture of Elias, warm in the first and cool in the second, written after Elias had led the Order.

How This Life Was Researched

This life draws on thirty-three sources in Latin, Umbrian Italian, Old French, Italian, English, and Spanish, gathered in two research passes. Francis’s writings were read in the 1904 Quaracchi critical edition. His Testament, his last will for Clare, his letter and parchment for Leo, Elias’s letter, and Jacques de Vitry’s account of the Sultan were checked against page scans. The Canticle was read in the transcription of a thirteenth-century manuscript at Assisi. Celano, the Three Companions, Bonaventure, the Mirror, and the Little Flowers were read in English translations of 1868 to 1925; Jordan of Giano, Thomas of Spalato, Jacques de Vitry’s letters, and Wadding were read in Latin, and the sworn testimony of the sisters of San Damiano in Spanish. The research did not read Celano’s Treatise on the Miracles, Julian of Speyer’s Life, Bonaventure’s Minor Legend, the Legend of Perugia and the Assisi Compilation in Latin, the rest of Jacques de Vitry’s Historia occidentalis, the Latin of Clare’s process, the later bulls on the stigmata, the reports of 1818, and modern studies.
