The bishop had a speech problem and spoke little English. Yet he had come to Washington to ask for help for thousands of people.

It was September 1949. Russian families who had fled China were waiting on a small island in the Philippines. They had tents, a camp, and nowhere permanent to go. John Maximovitch had lived among them. Now he was answering questions from American lawmakers. He could not give the refugees a new country himself. He could tell their story to people who had the power to let them in.

Years earlier, John had been a refugee too. He knew what it meant to leave home without knowing where life would lead. His work would carry him from Russia to Serbia, China, Western Europe, and America. Along the way, he taught boys, found homes for children, visited the sick, and helped families start again.

He would become known as St. John of Shanghai and San Francisco. But long before those cities became part of his name, he was a boy called Michael. Historian Matthew Namee traces the Washington visit through John's letters and the work of those who helped him.

A boy with plans to serve

Michael Borisovich Maximovitch was born in 1896 in Adamovka. The village lay in the Russian Empire, in what is now Ukraine. His parents, Boris and Glafira, came from a family with noble roots. They raised him in the Orthodox Christian faith.

He grew up in a world where service to Church and country could shape a whole life. The army, the courts, and government work offered paths for a boy from his background. At first, he thought he might take one of those paths. He did not begin life with a plan to become a bishop in China.

Later church accounts describe a frail child with a strong interest in prayer. They remember his love for the lives of saints. Those stories gave him examples of people who held to their faith through hardship. His parents also taught him to care about truth. As he grew, he began to think more about what kind of service he should offer.

In 1907 he entered the Poltava Cadet Corps, a school with military training. He stayed there until 1914. The school taught order and duty. It also brought him into contact with people who deepened his faith. Among them were the priest Sergius Chetverikov and Bishop Theophan of Poltava.

Michael's interest in Church life grew during these years. Still, his next step was to study law at Kharkov University. He finished in 1918. By then, the Russian Revolution had overturned the old order. Civil war followed. The country in which he had expected to build his future was breaking apart.

A law degree could not shield him or his family from that upheaval. Soon, the question was no longer which career he would choose at home. It was whether the family could remain there at all. The Orthodox Church in America's life gives the main dates of his youth and studies.

Starting over far from home

The Maximovitch family left their homeland during the civil war. Their route took them through Crimea and Constantinople, now called Istanbul. In 1921 they reached the country then known as the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats, and Slovenes. Michael settled in Belgrade.

He had spent years preparing for a profession. Now he had to find a place in a new country. He was one of many Russians trying to rebuild a life after the war. A former title or family name did not pay the bills. Michael helped support himself by selling newspapers while he studied theology, the study of God and faith.

He had not left learning behind. He had changed its focus. At the University of Belgrade, he prepared for work in the Church. He completed his theology degree in 1925.

One of the most important people in his new life was Metropolitan Anthony Khrapovitsky. A metropolitan is a senior bishop. Anthony helped lead the Russian Church abroad and became Michael's spiritual guide. He knew the needs of the scattered Russian people. They had lost homes and schools, but they also needed priests and church communities.

In 1926 Michael became a monk at Miljkovo Monastery. He received the name John, after his relative St. John of Tobolsk. Later that year, he was ordained a priest.

Becoming a monk meant giving up marriage and a private family life. It did not mean that John would spend all his time apart from other people. He began to teach and care for the young. The years ahead would bring him into schools, crowded cities, hospitals, refugee camps, and courtrooms.

He had crossed a border because of war. His choice to become a monk gave his new life a lasting direction. The cathedral's collection of memories describes his life as a young exile; the OCA account confirms his training and ordination.

The teacher in the dormitory

John first taught religion at a Serbian secondary school. He later taught at the seminary in Bitola, a school for future priests. His subjects included Church history and the work of a pastor. He taught students who would one day have to preach, lead services, and care for people in trouble.

He belonged to a rich circle of Serbian church life. Bishop Nikolaj Velimirović admired him. The theologian Justin Popović also worked in this setting. John had studied deeply, and his students could draw on that learning.

But some of their strongest memories came from outside his lessons.

According to recollections kept in his extended church biography, John checked the students' rooms at night. If a boy's blanket had fallen, he put it back over him. He prayed over the sleeping boys and made the sign of the cross.

These were small acts. They did not require a sermon. They gave the students a close view of the person teaching them to care for others.

They also noticed his strict way of life. John allowed himself very little sleep. He often rested in a chair or fell asleep while praying before icons. He fasted and made the Divine Liturgy, the Orthodox Communion service, the center of his day.

Later stories sometimes made these habits sound as if he had no normal human needs. Yet he did become tired, and his health would suffer. His way of life was unusual even among monks. It should not be mistaken for a rule that all Christians must follow.

At Bitola, the pattern of his later work began to take shape. He taught the faith in words, then spent time with the people in his care. He knew his students as more than names on a class list. That habit would remain when the students became parish members, sick people, and children without a secure home.

Sent to a city he had never served

In 1934 the bishops of the Russian Church Abroad chose John to become a bishop himself. They were sending him to Shanghai.

He was in his late thirties. He had been a student, a monk, a priest, and a teacher. Now he would lead a large church community in a country far from the places he knew.

Church accounts say he objected because of his speech problem. A bishop had to preach and speak for others. John did not have the strong, clear voice people might expect from a public leader. Metropolitan Anthony nevertheless trusted him with the work.

John became a bishop in Belgrade and reached Shanghai late that year. The city was a major port with people from many countries. It had also become a refuge for Russians who had escaped revolution and civil war.

Many of these exiles lacked the rights and safety that citizenship could bring. They were no longer at home in Russia, but China was not always a secure home either. Some had money or useful skills. Others lived in great need.

The Orthodox community was not only Russian. It included people with Greek, Serbian, Ukrainian, and Chinese roots. Nor did all the Russians agree about politics or church leadership. John had to work among people who shared parts of a past but did not always trust one another.

He could not meet their needs through Sunday services alone. Children needed schools. Old people needed care. Families needed food and places to turn when illness struck.

As bishop, John helped build up this wider work. The Holy Virgin Cathedral's account describes schools, care for older people, meals for those in need, and medical help. Churches and charity grew together. For a community that had lost so much, these places helped make daily life possible.

A home for children

One of John's main projects was a children's home named for St. Tikhon of Zadonsk. It began soon after he came to Shanghai. Over the years, it cared for many children who lacked a safe place to live.

Not every child there had lost both parents. Some had a mother or father who could not provide a home. War, illness, poverty, and the loss of work could break a family's ability to stay together. The home met that need as well.

John took a personal part in finding and helping children. Later accounts describe him going into dangerous parts of the city to bring abandoned children to safety. But the home did not run through his efforts alone.

Maria Alexandrovna Shakhmatova and other women handled much of its daily work. Someone had to find food, manage money, plan meals, and keep the children clothed. New arrivals meant more beds and more work. A promise to take in another child had to become care that lasted.

Stories about John and Shakhmatova show both their shared purpose and their strain. She sometimes feared that they could not feed more children. John kept pressing to receive those who needed help. Their work depended on people who gave money, time, and goods, as well as on the bishop's faith.

The number of children helped over the home's full history is not certain. Published accounts give different totals. The stories of named children offer a clearer view of what the home meant.

One of those children was Nikolai Massenkoff. His memories, recorded in the cathedral's remembrance booklet, carry the story beyond a list of buildings. They show how the home felt to a boy who grew up there and how his bond with John lasted long after he left China.

The boy who became a singer

Nikolai's mother was a widow. After a flood, she reached Shanghai with her young son and no home. She sought help from John. The children's home was already full, but Nikolai was eventually accepted at the age of two.

He later remembered the layout of the house: girls lived upstairs and boys downstairs. Prayer marked the start and end of the day. The children went to school and learned to take part in church life.

Music became important to Nikolai. He learned hymns by hearing and singing them. As a small altar boy, he wore church robes that were too long for him. These were the details that stayed with him when he looked back as an adult.

He would grow up to become a singer. By then, his life had moved to America, while John's work had taken him to Europe. The distance did not end their bond.

From Paris, John read a Russian newspaper from San Francisco. It carried news about Nikolai's progress in singing. John sent him a letter of praise.

For Nikolai, the letter was more than a kind note from a church leader. John had known him as a small child who needed a home. Now the bishop knew about the work he was doing as a young man. He had not been forgotten when he stopped needing a bed at the orphanage.

Nikolai had never known his own father. He remembered John as a father in his life. His account does not tell us what every child felt. It tells us what one child carried into adulthood: the sense that someone had taken an interest in his future and kept that interest across an ocean. Massenkoff's own recollection is one of several memories from people John knew in Shanghai.

Easter beyond the church walls

John's care also reached people who could not come to church. He visited hospitals, prisons, and private homes. He traveled on foot or by streetcar. His visits might include prayer, confession, and Holy Communion.

To John, a person did not stop belonging to the Church because they were ill or shut away. A church feast should reach them too.

Lydia Ionin remembered one such visit when she was thirteen. At Easter, she went with John to a mental hospital outside Shanghai. Patients gathered for confession and Communion. John also brought small bags of festive food.

The women of the cathedral had prepared the bags. They included eggs, Easter cake, and other food for the feast. The bishop gave them out with a smile.

In Lydia's memory, the visit was not a grand public event. It was a journey to people who could easily be left out of the day's joy. The food, prayers, and visit brought the feast into the hospital.

Many people later told stories of healing linked to John. Some recalled his prayers while they were sick. Others believed he had known of their need without being told. Orthodox Christians honor him as a wonderworker because of such accounts.

Those stories belong to the testimony of the people who told them. Lydia's memory shows something that needed no unusual sign: John went to the patients, and members of his church helped him bring a gift. The work of care included both the bishop at the bedside and the women who prepared the food.

The collection of named witnesses keeps these quieter memories alongside accounts of miracles. Together, they show why people came to seek him out. He was willing to spend time with them where they were.

War returns to Shanghai

In 1937 Japanese forces attacked Shanghai. War brought danger to the city that so many Russians had reached in search of safety. Fighting and occupation made life harder for people who were already poor or far from home.

John stayed with his church community. His later Life tells of journeys through dangerous streets to reach churches and people in need. The details of these stories come through church accounts. They fit the wider pattern remembered by those who knew him: he kept making visits when it would have been easier to stop.

The end of the Second World War did not bring a simple peace to the Russian churches in China. It brought a dispute over who should lead them.

The Russian Church Abroad had formed outside Soviet control. Its bishops served people scattered across many countries. The Church in Moscow, meanwhile, had suffered under the Soviet state. During the war, the state allowed it more room to act. Russians abroad disagreed about what that change meant.

Some hoped that Moscow's church leaders could now serve freely. Others feared that the government still controlled too much of their work. Distance, broken lines of contact, and years of mistrust made the question harder.

John briefly named both the head of the Moscow Church and the head of the Church Abroad in his prayers at services. He later remained under the bishops abroad. Other leaders in China chose Moscow, and the split divided people who had already lived through years of loss.

In 1946 John became an archbishop. His title changed, but the needs around him did not become smaller. Soon, another war would force his people to leave again. The history of the Russian churches in China explains the dispute. Fr. Peter Perekrestov's account adds context from John's words and church records.

Another country to leave

By the end of 1948, Communist forces were winning the Chinese Civil War. Many Russian refugees feared life under another Communist government. They began looking for a way out of Shanghai.

Leaving required more than money for a ticket. They needed somewhere that would let them enter. The Philippines offered a temporary refuge on Tubabao, an island near Samar. The Philippine government and the International Refugee Organization helped make the move possible.

Ships began carrying refugees there in January 1949. At the camp's height, roughly five thousand people lived on the island. Their stay was meant to be temporary. No one could give each family a firm date for a new home.

The camp was hot and wet. Rain, mosquitoes, and illness made daily life hard. People lived under canvas and had to build a working community with what they had.

They set up schools and churches. Priests, nuns, and other refugees helped create places for worship. Families marked church feasts even while they waited for news about visas and travel.

John arrived on May 14, 1949. He stayed in a small room near the camp's church life. Refugees later remembered him walking around the camp at night in prayer.

For people who felt cut off from the world, his presence mattered. Olga Morozova kept a diary of camp life. She recorded the sense of unity he brought. When he left on July 8, she wrote about the pain of seeing him go.

He had been there for less than two months. The next part of his work would take him far away from the people he was trying to help. Fr. Lawrence Cunningham's study of Tubabao draws on refugee diaries and historical research to trace this part of the story.

Asking America to open a door

John went to the United States to seek help for the refugees. By September 6, 1949, he was in Washington.

His English was limited. Nikolai Alexandrov helped translate and prepare the papers needed for the visit. John met officials and spoke before a Senate group dealing with the question of displaced people.

The lawmakers asked about the Russians in Shanghai. They wanted to know the history of the community and the needs of those waiting in the Philippines. John answered questions about people whose lives he had shared.

The task drew on many parts of his past. He knew the loss of home from his own youth. He knew the Shanghai community from years of work. His studies had trained him to deal with ideas and facts. His daily visits had shown him what those facts meant for families.

He was not working alone. Translators, refugee groups, officials, and supporters all played a part. Senator William Knowland of California was an important ally.

The effort did not change American law in a single day. It continued through 1949 and 1950. Changes to the Displaced Persons Act in June 1950 helped open a path for the refugees. In time, families from the camp made new homes in America, Australia, and other countries.

They did not all leave together. John continued to care about those still waiting. In a Christmas letter dated 1951 in the published collection, he wrote to the people left in the Philippines after a storm had destroyed their church. He shared their grief and urged them not to think that disaster meant they were worse than other people. He prayed for a way forward for them.

The letter preserves a harder part of camp life than the familiar stories of protection from storms. People could trust in prayer and still face real loss. John wrote to them within that loss.

Namee's account follows the work in Washington. John's letters, preserved in Russian, show his care for those who had not yet found a new home.

New work in Western Europe

In 1951 John was assigned to Western Europe. His work took him to Paris, Brussels, and other places where Orthodox communities needed a bishop.

Once again, many of the people he served were Russian exiles. Some had been away from their homeland for decades. The years had not removed the need to care for older people, teach children, and keep parish life going.

Money was often short. John's area of care was spread across borders. Travel, letters, visits, and services filled his time.

He also served people who had no Russian family roots. French and Dutch Christians were finding a home in Orthodoxy. John did not think they had to treat their own country's Christian past as foreign.

He studied saints who had lived in Western Europe before the long split between Eastern and Western Christianity. He gathered accounts of their lives and encouraged people to honor them. He also supported efforts to restore old Western forms of worship within Orthodox church life.

For a French Christian, this could mean seeing the faith through saints connected to France. It offered a link between the local past and a present church community. Orthodoxy need not appear only as a faith brought by people from Russia.

John's own life had taught him to cross languages and borders. He could serve in several languages and had lived among people with different customs. His work in Europe drew on that experience.

This was a new part of his service, not simply a pause between China and America. He spent more than a decade caring for these communities. The OCA life describes his work with Western saints and local forms of worship.

Letters to children an ocean away

While John was in Europe, children he had known in Shanghai were growing up in America. He could no longer watch over their lives in person. He used letters to keep in touch.

In an October 1955 letter to boys from St. Tikhon's Home, he told them that distance had not taken them out of his heart. He also challenged their poor attitude toward Russian school. Their American schools mattered, but he wanted them to learn their faith, language, and history too. He argued that knowing Russian could help in work as well as keep them close to their roots.

The letter is loving, but it is not all praise. John still saw himself as responsible for their growth. He expected them to study, even when the subjects did not seem useful to them. The published collection of his letters keeps both sides of that voice.

His letters also show the new problem facing a refugee family after it finds safety. A child can learn a new language faster than an older person. School and work can draw the young into a new way of life. The customs of home can start to feel distant.

John wanted the children to build good lives where they now lived. He also wanted them to know where they had come from. To him, feeding and housing them had been a beginning. Helping them become thoughtful adults was part of the same care.

That helps explain why a letter about a singer's progress mattered so much to Nikolai Massenkoff. John's interest had not ended at the door of the children's home. He kept following the young people as they found their own place in the world.

What his secretary saw

John's life in Europe could be hard on those who worked beside him. His secretary and treasurer, Petr Lopukhin, left letters that show a close view of those years.

Lopukhin admired John's knowledge of the faith. He helped put the bishop's sermons into writing. This could mean working late at night, listening through John's difficult speech and tiredness.

He also grew frustrated. In his view, John was a poor manager. The church had little money. Living conditions at its center in Versailles could be dirty and badly equipped. John accepted hardships that other people found much harder to bear.

His health worried those near him. He had swollen legs and bouts of illness. He could resist rest even when he needed it. Lopukhin's letters describe both deep respect for him and the strain of sharing his daily life.

The letters are one man's view, not a final judgment on every dispute. Still, they keep an important part of the story. A person could love John, value his teaching, and struggle to work with him.

Andrei Fastovskiy's review of the letters examines that mixed picture.

A divided parish in San Francisco

In 1962 John was sent to San Francisco. Many people he had known in Shanghai now lived there. Some of the children once cared for at St. Tikhon's Home were adults with lives of their own.

He came to familiar faces, but not to an easy task.

Archbishop Tikhon, who had led the community, had stepped back because of illness. Work on a large new cathedral had stopped. The church was named for the Mother of God, “Joy of All Who Sorrow.” Its members were divided over money, leadership, and how to move forward.

A cathedral is the main church of a bishop's local community. This one would be a place for worship and a lasting home for a people shaped by years of flight. But the hopes for the building had become tangled in debt and mistrust.

John wanted the work to resume. Others questioned the way church business was being handled. Competing groups argued about who had the right to make decisions. The dispute spread beyond the parish and involved other church leaders.

In 1963 it reached a civil court. Newspaper reports described hearings, court orders, and arguments over the church council. John faced public claims that he had mishandled financial affairs.

The later church biography reports that an outside review of the accounts found no wrongdoing by John or the new council. That report comes through the compiled Life, which includes press accounts; it is not a court record quoted here in full. The case concerned church property, money, and leadership. It should not be confused with a criminal trial.

For a bishop nearing the end of his life, it was a bitter conflict within the very community he had come to serve. The extended Life preserves accounts of the dispute and the reported financial review.

The children stand beside him

Some of John's strongest supporters in San Francisco were people who had grown up in his Shanghai home. They spoke out on his behalf during the dispute.

They knew a part of his life that could not be reduced to church accounts or newspaper claims. They had known him when they needed food, shelter, and a place to belong. Some had crossed the world with the refugee community he helped.

Their support did not settle every question about parish business. It showed how long his care had lasted in their memory. The adults defending him were among the children for whom he had once taken responsibility.

John continued his church work as the conflict went on. The cathedral project moved forward. The building was completed, giving the community a place that would remain tied to his name.

The years in San Francisco brought together many strands of his life. Refugee families, the children's home, long services, and visits to the sick were all part of his work again. So were the demands of leading a church with limited peace inside its own walls.

His story did not end with everyone agreeing about him. It ended with people who had different views of his ways, yet many who felt a lasting debt to his care. Former children of the home would stand close to him once more at his funeral. The church biography connects their public support with those final acts of love.

The man people remembered

By this time, John looked unlike the grand figure people might expect a bishop to be. His clothes and hair could seem untidy. He wore sandals, in part because he had painful trouble with his legs. Strangers could find his speech hard to follow.

Archbishop Peter Loukianoff knew him first as a child and altar server. He later tried to correct some of the simple stories told about him.

John did laugh, Peter said. He could joke with young people. He remembered birthdays and name days, the feast days of the saints for whom people were named. He asked about studies and kept up with events in the world.

Peter also rejected the claim that John was a “fool-for-Christ.” That is a specific kind of saintly life in Orthodox tradition. An unusual appearance alone did not make the label fit.

People close to John came to understand his speech. They knew a well-read man who could speak several languages. His looks did not tell them the whole person.

He could also keep people waiting. A visit to someone in the hospital might take longer than planned. A person in need might stop him on his way. Once a service began, John did not like to rush it.

For those waiting in church, the delay could be difficult. For the person he had stopped to see, the same delay meant time given to them. Both experiences were part of his daily life.

Peter's memories include warmth, learning, illness, and habits that tested people's patience. They give a fuller picture than either a flawless hero or an odd figure in worn clothes. His account in the remembrance booklet brings back the voice of someone who knew John before he became a saint in an icon.

His last journey

In July 1966 John was in Seattle on a church visit. He was traveling with the Kursk-Root Icon of the Mother of God, an image deeply loved in Russian Orthodox life.

He died there on July 2. He was seventy years old. On the older church calendar he used, the date was June 19.

His body was taken back to San Francisco. Bishops, priests, and many other people gathered for his funeral. Former children of the Shanghai home helped carry his bier, the stand on which his body rested.

It was a final return of the care he had given them. He had helped carry them through childhood and the loss of home. Now they helped carry him to his resting place.

San Francisco normally placed limits on burial within the city. The city changed its rules so John could be buried beneath the cathedral he had helped complete.

For much of his adult life, his home had depended on the next place the Church sent him. He had served people in country after country. Now he was buried among a community that had shared part of that long path.

People began visiting his tomb before the Church formally named him a saint. They brought requests for prayer and memories of his help. Some believed that help continued after his death. The OCA life records his death and later recognition; the extended Life describes his funeral and burial.

Remembered as a saint

In 1993 church representatives opened John's tomb as they prepared for his formal recognition as a saint. Fr. Peter Perekrestov was among those present.

He later wrote that the metal coffin and John's church robes had decayed, while the body had remained preserved. Orthodox Christians understand this as a sign of holiness. The account is the testimony of those church witnesses. Perekrestov's account describes what he said he saw.

On July 2, 1994, the Russian Orthodox Church Outside Russia formally recognized John as a saint. Orthodox Christians often call this act “glorification.” The Church in Moscow recognized that decision for wider honor in 2008.

His main feast day is July 2. The San Francisco cathedral also holds its major celebration on a nearby Saturday so people can gather. His body rests in a shrine in the cathedral, where the faithful come to pray.

His titles name Shanghai and San Francisco. They do not name every place that helped shape him. Serbia gave him a new start. Tubabao brought him among refugees waiting for homes. Washington called him to speak for them. Western Europe gave him new churches and people to serve.

Across those places, his work often began with a need close at hand. A boy needed shelter. A patient could not come to church. A family could not enter another country. Years later, a letter let a young singer know that John still followed his progress.

John did not make all those lives easy. People did not always agree with his decisions. But those who told his story kept returning to the time and care he gave them. For many, the saint they came to honor was first the person who had remembered them when they most needed a friend.
