Padre Pio wrote no books. His teaching survives in letters, written mostly because his spiritual directors told him to write, and in short sayings that people remembered and that the Church quotes. The letters fill four volumes of his Epistolario: to his directors (1910–1922), to Raffaelina Cerase (1914–1915), to his spiritual daughters (1915–1923), and to others. They are still under copyright, so the passages below are short, quoted from the Italian given in Church documents, in articles of Voce di Padre Pio that cite the letters by page, and in the Italian Wikiquote, in our own English.

Prayer: where God is found

For Padre Pio prayer came before learning. Books can describe God, he said, but only prayer reaches him. He called prayer a weapon and a key, the one means everyone has.

“In books we look for God; in prayer we find him. Prayer is the key that opens God’s heart.”

“Nei libri cerchiamo Dio, nella preghiera Lo troviamo. La preghiera è la chiave che apre il cuore di Dio.”

— Padre Pio, as quoted by the Dicastery for the Causes of Saints (our translation)

He knew prayer from the inside. Writing about his prayer in the years after 1913, he said that as soon as he began to pray, his soul gathered into a peace and quiet no words could express, and his senses fell still except his hearing, so that even a great noise around him did not disturb him (Epistolario I, p. 420). John Paul II quoted a related saying at his canonization: prayer is the best weapon we have, a key that opens the heart of God. By one account, in 1942 he answered Pius XII’s appeal for prayer for peace by asking his spiritual children to form groups to pray together; other sources date the prayer groups to the 1950s. There were about seven hundred of them by his death.

The Mass: Calvary on the altar

For Padre Pio the Mass was the center of everything. As a young priest he wrote that he longed for Communion so much that he went to receive it even with a fever. In later years his Mass lasted about an hour and a half, and John Paul II, who had attended it, remembered him saying that in the Mass there is all of Calvary.

“The world could even do without the sun, but it cannot do without the Holy Mass.”

“Il mondo potrebbe stare anche senza sole, ma non può stare senza la Santa Messa.”

— Padre Pio, letter, Epistolario III, p. 59, as quoted in Voce di Padre Pio, December 2025 (our translation)

The popular version, “without the Mass the world could not stand,” is not his exact wording. In 1987 John Paul II asked the friars of San Giovanni Rotondo whether the altar and the confessional were not the two poles of his life.

The Cross: where love is learned

The center of his spirituality was the Cross. He wrote that Jesus had told him that love is learned beneath it, and he described Calvary as the mountain the saints climb. To Raffaelina Cerase, on the feast of the Assumption in 1914, he wrote that a chosen soul is led by no other road than the one Christ walked: self-denial and the Cross.

“Under the Cross one learns to love.”

“Sotto la Croce s’impara ad amare.”

— Padre Pio, recording words he heard from Jesus, Epistolario I, p. 339 (our translation)

He lived this literally: from 1918 until shortly before his death he bore wounds in his hands, feet, and side, which he covered and almost never spoke of.

Suffering offered as love

Padre Pio did not treat suffering as something merely to be endured. In his letters it is a gift he can give back to Christ: in love Christ delights him, and in sorrow he delights Christ. After the stigmata he could still write to God, “How sweet and bitter you are together! You wound and you heal” (Epistolario I, p. 1103). To his spiritual daughters he promised that a heart that holds the crucified Christ will find its crosses changed.

“Keep Jesus crucified in your heart, and all the crosses of the world will seem to you like roses.”

“Tieni nel tuo cuore Gesù crocifisso e tutte le croci del mondo ti sembreranno rose.”

— Padre Pio, letter to a spiritual daughter, Epistolario III, p. 827 (our translation)

He told McCaffery that the nights were the worst, because if he slept the pain of the wounds grew; and he went on saying Mass and hearing confessions.

Abandonment and trust

His letters also record long stretches of darkness, when he wrote, “I look for God, but where am I to find him?” His counsel to others was surrender, like a small child carried by its mother.

“Give yourselves up wholly to the divine heart of Jesus, like a child in its mother’s arms.”

“Abbandonatevi pienamente sul cuore divino di Gesù, come un bimbo tra le braccia della madre.”

— Padre Pio, a saying quoted by John Paul II at the beatification, 2 May 1999 (our translation)

To Raffaelina Cerase he wrote that it seemed Jesus had nothing else in hand but to sanctify her soul.

Souls and the priesthood

Padre Pio held priests to a high standard, beginning with himself. To Raffaelina Cerase he wrote that every minister of the Lord should work for souls without ever admitting weariness, and that he knew himself “too well” to think he did so; she, in turn, offered her Communion for priests who had gone wrong, and he approved. He signed letters by asking for prayers for his own conversion.

“Every minister of the Lord should always work for the health of souls and never admit weariness.”

“Ogni ministro del Signore dovrebbe mai sempre lavorare per la salute delle anime, non dovrebbe riconoscere mai stanchezza.”

— Padre Pio to Raffaelina Cerase, Epistolario II, pp. 537–540, as quoted in Voce di Padre Pio, December 2005 (our translation)

He lived it in the confessional, where he sat for hours a day for some fifty years. As early as July 1917, alone with one other friar and a house of seminary boys, he wrote to Father Agostino, “I do not know how to say no to anyone.”

Obedience

Padre Pio wrote his letters, kept silent, and accepted restrictions because his superiors asked it of him. He told one of them that he acted only to obey. When Bishop Rossi, sent by the Holy Office in 1921, asked whether he would always submit to the authority of the Church, he answered: “Yes, Excellency. In Holy Church it is God himself who speaks.”

“I act only to obey you.”

“Opero solamente per ubbidirvi.”

— Padre Pio to a superior, Epistolario I, p. 807, as quoted by John Paul II, 2002 (our translation)

In 1920 he would not show his wounds to Father Gemelli without written authorization, and from 1931 to 1933, when the Holy See allowed him only a private Mass, he said nothing in public in his own defense. He described the stigmata to Father Benedetto only when ordered to, and began that letter of 22 October 1918 with the confusion and humiliation it cost him.

Humility and the danger of vainglory

He knew the temptation that fame brings. In his letters he called the wish to be admired a martyrdom, and he described himself only as a poor friar who prays. When a Jesuit wondered how his humility survived the crowds, he laughed and said that a man who claimed God’s gifts as his own would have to be either mad or a thief, like a traveler who shows off as his own a watch he was asked to carry to Milan for repair.

“My God! What a martyrdom the temptation of vainglory is.”

“Dio mio! che martirio è la tentazione della vanagloria.”

— Padre Pio, letter, Epistolario (1995 ed.), 314 (our translation)

The watch story is told by John McCaffery in The Friar of San Giovanni (1978); the words above are our summary, not a quotation. Of the hospital he founded he said that the work was not his but Providence’s.
