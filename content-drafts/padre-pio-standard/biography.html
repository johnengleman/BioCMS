In the summer of 1923 the people of San Giovanni Rotondo heard that their friar was going to be sent away. The friary stood about a mile outside their town, high on the Gargano in southern Italy. They walked up to it in a crowd, and they would not go home until he came to a window and let them see him.1

That summer the Rome correspondent of the London Observer wrote about the man the town would not give up. He found no wonder-worker.2

“Padre Pio himself lays no claim to miraculous gifts; he is the most modest and simple of men.”3

— The Observer, London, summer 1923

The friar at the window was Saint Pio of Pietrelcina, whom the world still knows as Padre Pio, Italian for Father Pio. He was thirty-six. For five years he had carried open wounds in his hands, his feet, and his side, and by now much of Italy knew it. Doctors had examined them and newspapers had written about them. Rome had even sent a bishop in secret to question him. Yet twice he had asked God to make the marks disappear.4

He would stay on that mountain for fifty-two years. He spent his days at the altar and in the confessional of one small friary, and the world climbed up to find him. He left behind a hospital and a movement of prayer that reached across the world. One of the young priests who came to him for confession would one day be Pope. It began with a farm boy who wanted a beard.5

Saint Pio Grows Up on a Farm

Saint Pio was born Francesco Forgione on 25 May 1887 in Pietrelcina, a hill village near Benevento. His parents, Grazio and Maria Giuseppa, owned a little land outside the village at a place called Piana Romana, and they worked it themselves. They grew grapes and wheat, olives and figs, and kept a few sheep and goats. There was food every day and very little cash. Neither parent could read or write, but they knew the Bible stories by heart and told them to their children.6

The day in that house began with the dawn bell and ended with the Rosary. The family went to Mass every day and gave up meat three days a week in honour of Our Lady of Mount Carmel. The neighbours called them the God-is-everything people. The boy minded the sheep at Piana Romana and went to school when the farm could spare him. Long afterward he said that as a small child he saw Our Lady and the Sacred Heart of Jesus in the village church. He thought every child saw them.7

Then a friar came through the village. He was a young Capuchin named Fra Camillo. He walked the countryside asking for gifts for his friary at Morcone. He wore a beard. The boy was drawn to him. He told his parents that he wanted to be a friar like that one, a friar with a beard. He said much later that the beard stayed so fixed in his mind that nothing could turn him from the wish.8

His parents took him at his word. They went thirteen miles north to Morcone and asked whether the friars would take him. The friars were willing. But the boy had only three years of village school and needed much more. A tutor cost money that a small farm did not make. So Grazio Forgione went to America to find work, and the money he earned there paid for the boy’s lessons.9

The boy was fifteen when he rode to Morcone in an oxcart in January 1903. On 22 January he put on the brown robe of the Capuchins, the friars who follow Saint Francis of Assisi, and he received a new name. From then on he was Fra Pio, Brother Pio. The name came from Saint Pius V, the patron of his village.10

Saint Pio Becomes a Capuchin and Falls Ill

Saint Pio made his first vows in January 1904 and moved from friary to friary for his studies. Within a year or two his body began to fail. He could not keep food down. He fainted, and his head ached, and for a time he could digest only milk and cheese.11

In the school year of 1907 to 1908 he studied theology at the friary of Serracapriola. His teacher there was Padre Agostino da San Marco in Lamis, who would watch over his soul for the rest of his own life. Padre Agostino described his student in his diary: “He was good, obedient, studious, though sickly.” At Serracapriola Saint Pio meditated on the Passion of Christ and wept over it so much that his eyes grew sick.12

By Good Friday 1908 his chest was already weak. That day a migraine took hold of him, and it lasted the rest of his stay. He missed lessons. That summer he wrote home that the heat made it a little hard to be there, and he told his parents not to worry. The friars worried for him. His pains seemed to have no end, and he had lost his appetite. They sent a telegram to the parish priest of Pietrelcina, Don Salvatore Pannullo, who sent for Grazio. The father came, slept at the friary, listened to the doctor, and took his son home to rest.13

That became the pattern of his twenties: back to a friary, ill again, home again. Through it all his superiors kept him moving toward the priesthood. In those days a new priest had to be twenty-four. Saint Pio was twenty-three, and the archbishop made an exception for him. On 10 August 1910 he was ordained in the chapel of the cathedral of Benevento. Four days later he said his first Mass in his own village.14

He did not go back to a friary. He stayed at home on sick leave, in his robe, and every morning after Mass he walked out to Piana Romana. His brother Michele had built him a hut there. He prayed under the trees in the open air, which was good for his lungs.15 Within a month of his first Mass, something happened to his hands.

Saint Pio Asks God to Hide the Marks

It happened under an elm at Piana Romana, on the afternoon of 7 September 1910. In the middle of each palm a little red mark appeared, about the size of a cent coin. With it came a strong and sharp pain that was worse in the left hand. There was pain in the soles of his feet as well.16

He said nothing to his superiors. He wrote afterward that he kept silent for almost a year because of “that cursed shame.”17

The first person he told was Don Salvatore Pannullo, the parish priest of Pietrelcina. Everyone called him Zi’ Tore, Uncle Tore. Saint Pio asked him for a kindness.18

“Zi’ Tore, do me the kindness: let us ask Jesus to take this shame from me. I want to suffer, even to die of suffering, but all in hiding.”19

— Saint Pio to Don Salvatore Pannullo, as remembered in Pietrelcina

The priest promised to help him pray. But Don Salvatore told him that if this was God’s will, he must bend to it in everything and everywhere. Saint Pio prayed, and the marks went away. The pain stayed. A few years later Padre Agostino questioned him under obedience. Saint Pio answered that the marks had once been visible, “especially in one hand.” The very sharp pain had not gone, and he felt it most on certain days.20

Why would a young man ask to suffer, and then ask to hide it? His letters give his own answer. He wrote that Jesus had told him that “in love he delights me; in sorrows I delight him.” Another time Jesus said to him, “Under the Cross one learns to love.” Pain was a gift he could give back to Jesus, and hiding kept it between the two of them. The wish to be admired was a torment to him. “My God! What a martyrdom the temptation of vainglory is.”21

In the autumn of 1911 his superiors sent him to the friary at Venafro to recover, and he stayed about forty days. There Padre Agostino saw things he had not seen before. The young priest fell into long states of prayer and spoke aloud with Jesus and Mary, with Saint Francis, and with his guardian angel, as one talks with friends. Padre Agostino began to write down what he heard. On the morning of 28 November he wrote for more than an hour while Saint Pio spoke to Jesus: “It is so sweet to be with you! Oh, how good it is here!” Padre Agostino also saw the devil attack him.22

On 7 December he went home to Pietrelcina. For four more years he lived there, mostly in his brother Michele’s house. As obedience asked, he wrote to the two priests who guided his soul, Padre Agostino and Padre Benedetto, and those letters show what he lived on. Before Holy Communion he felt such hunger and thirst that he nearly died of longing. Sometimes he went to the altar “with fever on me,” because he could not stay away. When he began to pray, his soul gathered into a peace and quiet “no words can express,” and his senses were held still, all but his hearing.23

The letters show the dark too. In May 1913 he told Padre Agostino that he had dared to ask Our Lady again for a grace she had already refused him. “This holy Mother flew into a rage,” he wrote. From that day she and the other heavenly persons withdrew from him. In another dark time he wrote: “I seek God, but where can I find him?”24

Again and again he closed his letters with the same request: “Pray hard for my conversion.”25

Saint Pio Comes to San Giovanni Rotondo

Italy was at war, and in November 1915 the army called him up. For more than two years it sent him back and forth between military hospitals in Naples and long sick leaves. In March 1918 it let him go for good with a disease of both lungs.26

Between leaves, in February 1916, his superiors sent him to the friary at Foggia, where he suffered sweats and fevers. That July Padre Paolino da Casacalenda took him up to a small friary in the mountains for a week. Padre Paolino was its guardian, the friar in charge of the house. In the mountain air he felt better. On 4 September 1916 he was sent to San Giovanni Rotondo “provisionally.” He would stay for fifty-two years.27

The friary of Our Lady of Grace stood about a mile outside the town, with a small church and a boarding school for boys who hoped to become friars. A week after he arrived, Saint Pio wrote to Padre Agostino that the community there set him a fine example, and that the night of his spirit was deepening. Padre Paolino slipped a note of his own into the envelope. Apart from the inner pains with which the Lord was testing him, the guardian wrote, the young friar could be called really happy.28

Then the war emptied the house. In February 1917 the director of the boys’ school was called up, and later another priest followed, until only two priests were left, Padre Paolino and Saint Pio. The sick friar became the boys’ guide. He heard their confessions and gave them two talks a week. He watched over their studies and their weekly walk, joined their games, led them at meals, and prayed with them. People from the town began to climb the hill to him as well.29

In July 1917 he told Padre Agostino that he had no time left even to rest in the heart of the Father. The care of the boys took all of it, and so did the souls he helped by voice and by letter. Then he wrote a line that would hold true for the rest of his life: “I do not know how to say no to anyone.”30

In the spring of 1918 Pope Benedict XV asked for prayers for the end of the war. On 30 May, the feast of the Body of Christ, Saint Pio offered himself to God at Mass for that cause. He wrote that what followed was a lasting state that felt like imprisonment in hell. Between March and September Padre Paolino twice found him in ecstasy, deep in prayer and speaking with Jesus and Mary. He also saw that Saint Pio could read what was hidden in people’s hearts.31

On the evening of 5 August 1918 he was hearing the boys’ confessions. He wrote to Padre Benedetto that all at once he was filled with terror at the sight of a heavenly figure. In its hand it held something like a very long blade of iron with a sharp point, and fire seemed to come from the point. The figure pierced his soul with it. He told the boy in front of him to go. He could barely let out a cry. “I felt I was dying.”32

The pain lasted until the morning of 7 August. When it passed, it left something behind. He wrote that he now felt “a wound that is always open” in the depth of his soul. The light blinded him. He was tired, and only obedience to his director kept him from giving up altogether.33

Padre Benedetto wrote back. He reminded Saint Pio that the sixth of August had been the feast of the Transfiguration, the day the Church remembers Christ shining in glory on the mountain. He added that the wound was a sign of God’s love.34 Six weeks later, the wound could be seen.

Saint Pio Receives the Wounds of Christ

On Friday 20 September 1918 the friary was nearly empty. Padre Paolino was away at San Marco in Lamis, and the brother who begged for the house was out on his rounds. Saint Pio said Mass in the little church. Then he climbed to the choir to give thanks. The choir was the friars’ prayer loft above the church door, with wooden stalls and a crucifix on the rail. It was between nine and ten in the morning, and he was alone.35

He told no one what happened next: not his superior, not Padre Benedetto, not even Padre Agostino. A month later Padre Benedetto ordered him under obedience to write it all down. He obeyed on 22 October, and his letter opens with a cry: “My God, what confusion and humiliation I feel.” Then he went back to that morning in the choir.36

“It was the morning of the 20th of last month, in the choir, after Holy Mass, when a rest came over me, like a sweet sleep.”37

— Saint Pio to Padre Benedetto, 22 October 1918

All his senses went still. Before him stood a mysterious figure like the one of 5 August, and its hands and feet and side were dripping blood. The sight terrified him. He felt he was dying, he wrote, and he would have died if the Lord had not held up his heart. Then the figure withdrew, and he saw that his own hands and feet and side were pierced and bleeding.38

Later he told a friend what happened next. He woke on the floor, and on hands and knees he dragged himself from the choir to his cell. There he lay on his bed and prayed, looked at the wounds, and wept.39

He meant to keep it secret. The secret did not last a day. A woman named Filomena Ventrella saw red marks on his hands that same day, and he warned her and her sister that they had better tell no one. The next morning the boys of the school saw a wound in each of his palms. He walked with difficulty, and his face was redder than usual.40

That afternoon a woman of the town named Nina Campanile came up to the friary. She had kissed his hand on the 18th and seen nothing. Now he teased her for staying away three days while he waited for a Mary to bring him a word of comfort. She held out an offering for a Mass, and on the back of his right hand she saw a mark that looked like a burn. She chose to act as if she did not understand. “Oh, Father! You have burned your hand!” Saint Pio went pale and put both hands behind his back.41

As she left, she tried to kiss the wound. “If you knew what humiliation you give me!” he said. He told her he would pray to the Lord so hard that it would all disappear. He had prayed the marks away once before.42

For eight or ten days the guardian noticed nothing. Then Nina told Padre Paolino, smiling, and he laughed out loud. Could Saint Pio have received the wounds of Christ without his guardian noticing? Padre Paolino was with him all the time. The next day he walked into Saint Pio’s cell without knocking. Saint Pio was writing. The guardian saw the wound on the back and in the palm of the right hand, then the wound on the back of the left. He wrote to Padre Benedetto that same day and asked him to come. Padre Benedetto did not come. He asked for the strictest silence.43

His letters that autumn show what it cost him. On 17 October he asked whether he must say his yes to God while he looked at “that mysterious figure who has wounded me all over.” He wrote that he heard a constant roar inside himself, like a waterfall that always pours out blood. Later he told Padre Benedetto that the wound in his side bled from Thursday evening until Saturday. The pain was killing him, and so was the shame that came after it. “I fear I will bleed to death.” And yet in another letter he spoke to his torments face to face. He called them sweet and bitter together, and he asked why they were so unbearable and so dear.44

He hid what he could. He wore mittens over his hands, red or black, and said he was embarrassed.45 But at the altar the mittens had to come off.

Padre Benedetto finally came at the start of Lent in 1919. He found a friar who could barely stand at the altar. On Ash Wednesday he wrote to Padre Agostino what he had seen. He had found no marks or stains. He had found “true wounds” that pierced the hands and the feet, and on Fridays there was blood. Everyone could see the gift whenever Saint Pio said Mass, because he had to raise his bare hands.46

Doctors and a Bishop Examine Saint Pio

By May 1919 the newspapers of Rome were printing stories about the friar with the stigmata, the wounds of Christ. Then the doctors came. The friars’ regional superior sent the first, Dr Luigi Romanelli of the hospital at Barletta. He found the wounds in the hands covered by a thin reddish-brown layer, with no bleeding, no swelling, and no inflammation. When he pressed his thumb into the palm and a finger on the back of the hand, he could feel the empty space between them. In July a professor from Rome, Amico Bignami, saw the same wounds and judged them otherwise. He thought they began as a disease of the skin and were completed by suggestion or by a chemical such as tincture of iodine, perhaps without the friar’s knowing. In October Dr Giorgio Festa came, and he came back for years. In 1925 he reported to the Holy Office, the Vatican office that guarded the faith. He wrote that the wounds came from no outside injury and no strong chemical.47

In April 1920 the well-known Franciscan doctor Agostino Gemelli arrived and asked to see the wounds. Saint Pio asked for his written permission from the Holy Office. Gemelli had none. He left without seeing the wounds and called the friar a hysteric.48

The Holy Office wanted its own answer. In June 1921 it sent a bishop in secret, Raffaello Carlo Rossi of Volterra. He arrived on 14 June and stayed eight days. His questions drew 142 detailed answers. He meant to stay cool and fair, and he wrote that he felt an indifference that was almost coldness. At last he came to the question the Holy Office cared about most. Would Saint Pio always submit to the authority of the Church?49

“Yes, Excellency. In Holy Church it is God himself who speaks.”50

— Saint Pio to Bishop Raffaello Carlo Rossi, June 1921

Bishop Rossi wrote down what he found. Everyone spoke of a scent like violets around the friar, and the bishop smelled it himself. He could not confirm many of the healings that people told him about. On 4 October 1921 he finished his report. He wrote that Saint Pio was a good friar and a model, practised in the virtues. He saw in him a heartfelt humility and a rare simplicity. The wounds were a real fact. The bishop found in them neither fraud nor the work of the devil.51

The report went to Rome. Rome did not follow it.

Saint Pio Is Ordered to Leave His Friary

On 2 June 1922 the Holy Office sent its first orders. The friar was to be watched. He must say Mass at no fixed hour, early and in private. He must not bless the people. He must not show the wounds, or speak of them, or let anyone kiss them. And he must take a new spiritual director in place of Padre Benedetto, and break off all contact with him, even by letter.52

Padre Benedetto had guided his soul through the first marks, the dark letters, and the wound of 1918. Now Padre Agostino took his place, with leave to visit San Giovanni Rotondo several times a year. Saint Pio and Padre Benedetto never saw each other again.53

In May 1923 the Holy Office declared that it could not establish anything supernatural in the facts told about him. In June new orders came. He was to say Mass in the friary’s inner chapel with no one present, and he was to answer no letters. The town found out. On 26 June the people rose in protest, and the friars let him say Mass in the church again.54

Five weeks later the head of all the Capuchins signed an order moving Saint Pio to a friary in the Marche, far to the north. Padre Luigi Festa was sent to carry it out, and he read it to Saint Pio at midnight on 8 August. Saint Pio folded his arms and bowed his head. “Here I am, at your service,” he said. “Let us leave at once. When I am with my superior, I am with God.” He asked only to be sent to Montefusco, inside his own province. Padre Luigi told him that nothing would happen until Rome sent further word.55

The town gave its own answer. Two days later a mason named Donato Centra walked into the church with a pistol and told Saint Pio that, alive or dead, he would stay with them. The mayor, Francesco Morcaldi, arranged for the friary to be watched day and night. On the evening of 15 August eight local Fascists climbed the hill with clubs to ask him to stay. The prefect of the province said that the order would be carried out even over dead bodies.56

Saint Pio was terribly worried that people would die because of him. On 12 August he wrote to the mayor. He was upset at the thought that he might cause deaths without wanting to, and he asked the mayor to help carry out his superiors’ will. On 21 August the head of the order told the mayor that the move was put off. Saint Pio wrote again on 27 August, this time to Padre Luigi. He would obey any order, he said. But if he left without some special help from God, priests would die, churches would be damaged, and his own life would be in danger. At the end of the month a senior official said that the move could not be made without bloodshed, and in early September the order was frozen. Saint Pio stayed.57

In his letter to the mayor he had asked one thing more. When he died, he wanted his bones to rest “in a quiet corner of this land.”58

Saint Pio Loses His Mother and Is Silenced

Through the 1920s he said Mass and heard confessions with Rome’s orders still standing over him. On 5 December 1928 his mother came from Pietrelcina to spend Christmas near him. She came with Mary Pyle, an American who had become one of his spiritual daughters. Mary Pyle gave her a room in her house.59

They met in the square in front of the little church. His mother, Mamma Peppa, took her son’s hand before he could pull it away. She kissed it for one aunt, and then for another, and on through ten or more aunts and godmothers. At last she said, “And now, Padre Pio, I kiss your hand for me.” As she bent down, he lifted his hands out of her reach and held them up. “Never this! A son must kiss his mother’s hand, not the mother the son’s.”60

She obeyed him. But every morning, after he had given her Holy Communion at the high altar and moved on, she bent down and kissed the floor where his wounded foot had stood. She climbed up from Mary Pyle’s house each morning through snow and cold and a cutting wind, in a thin dress she had brought from home. She would not take a wool dress from anyone.61

On Christmas night she sat in the crowded church between the altar of the Immaculate Virgin and her son’s confessional, and she heard his three Masses. Then she went home and lay down. After three days of high fever the doctor said it was double pneumonia. Saint Pio went to see her many times. The guardian of the friary always came with him and brought her Holy Communion every morning.62

Saint Pio stayed by her bed, and it was he who gave her the last sacraments, the Church’s rites for the dying. When he saw that she was about to breathe her last, he could bear no more. He kissed her forehead and broke into a loud sob, and then he fainted. Two doctors carried him away from the bed. The guardian, Padre Raffaele, took his place. Mamma Peppa died in the guardian’s arms at a quarter past six in the morning on 3 January 1929.63

They laid Saint Pio on a bed in the next room, and he wept until handkerchief after handkerchief was soaked. “My mother! My beautiful mother!” he cried. A man tried to comfort him. He had taught them himself that sorrow should be offered to God as love, the man said, so why did he weep like this? Saint Pio answered, “But that is just what these are: tears of love, nothing but love.” The next day she was buried in the friars’ chapel in the town cemetery.64

Two years later Rome closed the last door. In the spring of 1931 the Holy Office took from him every work of a priest except Mass. Even that he could say only in private, in the friary’s inner chapel. The decree reached the friary on the evening of 9 June. After the evening prayer of Vespers, Saint Pio stayed on in the choir to pray as usual. Padre Raffaele, still the guardian, gathered his courage and called him to the little parlour, and Saint Pio came at once. The guardian read him the decree. No public Mass. No confessions, from the people or from the friars.65

Saint Pio raised his eyes to heaven. “May God’s will be done!” he said. Then he covered his eyes with his hands, bowed his head, and said nothing more. The guardian tried to comfort him. A little later Saint Pio went back to the choir, and he stayed there until after midnight.66

On the morning of 11 June he began to say Mass in the inner chapel, with only the friar who served it. The server shut the door behind them, so that no one could come in. For two years his world was the chapel, the library, and his cell. One morning in June 1932 Padre Agostino came and heard that Mass. It lasted an hour and thirty-five minutes, and Saint Pio wept through it. Afterward he told Padre Agostino that even at Mass he had to push forward by force, as if with pincers. “My God, what torment!”67

In November Padre Agostino found him calm. “Let happen what will,” Saint Pio said. “I feel calm.” He spent the days in prayer and study and stayed on in the choir for hours after the friars’ prayers. At noon he ate very little, and in the evening nothing. With his brothers and his friends he was cheerful and kind.68

He accepted his superiors’ measures without a word, and he stayed silent in the face of slander. On 16 July 1933 he came down to say Mass in the church again. On 25 March 1934 the men could confess to him once more, and on 12 May the women came back to his confessional.69

Saint Pio Builds a Hospital on the Mountain

The crowds came back up the mountain, and Saint Pio had a plan for them. At the end of 1939 he told one of his spiritual sons, a veterinarian from Perugia named Mario Sanvico, that he wanted a hospital near the friary.70

On the evening of 9 January 1940 a small group met in a cottage near the friary, where the Sanvico family lived with the family of a doctor, Guglielmo Sanguinetti. There were three men, their wives, and one other woman. All three men had put their souls in Saint Pio’s care. They formed a committee and signed a paper saying that everything would be done first on his advice. Then they went to the friary and told him. He gave his approval. He said that he too wanted to give his offering, and he handed them a small coin that a visitor had given him that morning. That evening he said that his great work on earth had begun.71

Two days later he showed them where the hospital would stand. On 14 January he gave it its name: Casa Sollievo della Sofferenza, the Home for the Relief of Suffering. Then, in June, Italy went to war, and everything stopped.72

The war years brought a fear and a loss. In June 1941 Padre Agostino came on his official visit and found Saint Pio upset. Someone had told him that Padre Agostino was leaving for the missions for good. “I have lost Padre Benedetto,” Saint Pio said, “and now I am losing you too!” Padre Agostino calmed him. They must do God’s will, he said, and not worry about the future. On 22 July 1942 Padre Benedetto died. For twenty years Saint Pio had not been allowed to write to him.73

That same year Pope Pius XII asked Catholics to pray for peace. Saint Pio urged the people who came to him to gather in groups and pray together, and the first prayer groups began.74

His father had been living near him since about 1937, as Mary Pyle’s guest. Grazio Forgione was a strong man with hard, cracked hands, a ready joke, and a cheerfulness that caught on. When people told him how lucky he was to have a priest for a son, he answered with tears in his eyes that he had not made him. Jesus Christ had made him, he said; God had made him. Visitors came back to his sickroom just to enjoy his company. As the old man grew weaker, Saint Pio came more and more often, and near the end he stayed in the house. Friends filled the room, but Grazio saw only his son. He kept his eyes on Saint Pio’s eyes to the last. He died on the night of 7 October 1946 with Saint Pio beside him. He was eighty-six. They laid him beside his wife.75

In May 1947 work began on the hospital, with no architect’s plan and four million lire in the bank. A poor widow gave a fifty-lira note. Saint Pio kept it in the pocket of his robe and took it out to show people, sometimes in tears.76

The Home for the Relief of Suffering opened on 5 May 1956, on a bright, sunny day. It had some 250 beds and two operating theatres, and its own farm for milk, eggs, poultry, and oil. Saint Pio spoke a few words to the crowd. He said that a seed had been sown on the earth, and that God would warm it with the rays of his love. It would be a place of prayer and science. When people praised him for it, he gave the praise away.77

“The Work is not mine,” he said, “but Providence’s.”78

A Morning at Saint Pio’s Confessional

After the war the crowds grew larger. Allied soldiers had found their way up the mountain from 1943, and the articles they wrote for Catholic papers made him known in America. In April 1947 the archbishop of Manfredonia told the friars what he had said to an official of the Holy Office. “How many sins you left in the world when you suspended Padre Pio from confessions for three years!” By 1950 so many people wanted to confess to Saint Pio that the friary had to set up a booking system.79

A summer morning there began in the dark. Saint Pio rose long before four to prepare for Mass. Some pilgrims were at the church door by two or three in the morning, waiting for the best places. A band of local women came a few minutes before the doors opened and pushed to the front. Once they even chained a stool to the altar rail the night before, to save a seat. One woman on her first visit took her place at the door at half past two and was knocked down and trampled.80

He said the Mass in Latin at the side altar of Saint Francis in the old church. It lasted about an hour and a half. The church was crowded. When it was over, the men followed him into the sacristy, the room where priests dress for Mass. They stood without a word while he took off his vestments and prayed with his head bowed. Years before, he had written to a spiritual daughter what that hour meant to him. “The world could do without the sun, but it cannot do without the Holy Mass.”81

The rest of the day belonged to the confessional. For fifty years he sat in it hour after hour, in the heat of summer and the cold of winter. The women confessed in an open confessional in the church, and the men in a screened booth in the sacristy. Pilgrims wrote their names on a numbered list and waited for days.82

He could seem severe to the pilgrims, yet almost all of them came back to him. Saint John Paul II was born Karol Wojtyła. As a young Polish priest studying in Rome, he came to San Giovanni Rotondo. He stayed nearly a week, heard Saint Pio’s Mass, and made his confession to him. He spoke of it many years later as Pope.83

“I also had the privilege, during my young years, of benefitting from his availability for penitents.”84

— Pope Saint John Paul II, 16 June 2002

One man who came to confess was Giovanni, a taxi driver from Tuscany and a fierce Communist. He drove the long road south in his own taxi. In the confessional he wept, and Saint Pio wept with him. At the end Giovanni took out his party card and asked Saint Pio to destroy it.85

The Scottish writer John McCaffery served Saint Pio’s Mass seven times. He said that his memories of the friar were filled with humour more than with anything else. To a husband with a talkative wife, Saint Pio offered to take fifty per cent of her and leave him the rest. When McCaffery told him that a Jesuit superior wondered how his humility survived the crowds, Saint Pio shook with laughter. If he took for himself what belonged to God alone, he said, he would have to be either mad or a thief. He wrote short notes on prayer cards for visitors and laughed at his own scrawls. As for the perfume that people noticed around him, he called it just sweets for the children.86

He did all this on one meal a day, which he ate under obedience: a spoonful of soup or a forkful of pasta or rice or vegetables. The wounds stayed open, and he almost never spoke of them. One day near the start of summer McCaffery saw him wince when the sun from a corridor window fell on his back. McCaffery said he was sorry for him, with the hot days so near. Saint Pio answered in a weary voice. The days were not so bad, he said. One thing carried him on to the next, and the day went by.87

“It is the nights.” If he let himself sleep, the pain grew beyond measure. As he said it, he held up his wounded hands.88

Rome Investigates Saint Pio a Second Time

In 1960 serious charges against him reached a new Pope, Saint John XXIII. Some said that Saint Pio had improper relations with the women around him. Some said that the hospital’s money was being misused, and that fanatics had gathered around the friar. Someone had hidden microphones at San Giovanni Rotondo. In the early summer of 1960 an official of the Holy Office, Pietro Parente, told the Pope about the recordings.89

On 25 June the Pope filled four private pages about the matter. He set down the charges with a condition: “if what is reported is true.” Of himself he wrote that, by the Lord’s grace, he felt calm and almost unmoved. Then he turned to the friar, whom he named only by his initials.90

“I am sorry for P. P., who has a soul to save all the same, and for whom I pray hard.”91

— Pope Saint John XXIII, private notes, 25 June 1960

The Pope sent Monsignor Carlo Maccari to visit the friary. Maccari stayed from 30 July to 17 September 1960 and questioned Saint Pio in nine meetings. He judged the friar harshly. In his diary he wrote of secretiveness, narrowness of mind, and lies, and he summed up his impression in one word: pitiful. He asked that the friars be moved out a few at a time and that a superior be brought in from outside. Each person who came to confess should be allowed one confession a month, and the hospital should have new rules.92

In February 1961 the Holy Office sent another man in secret, a Dominican named Paolo Philippe. He came to put the charges to Saint Pio face to face. “It is false, utterly false,” Saint Pio answered, again and again. When it came to the hardest charge of all, he raised his hand as if before God. He had never kissed a woman, he said. He had not even wanted to kiss his own mother. He had made her cry by not returning her kisses, because he thought it would be wrong.93

The guardian, Padre Rosario da Aliminusa, spoke up for him. Saint Pio was simple and not cunning, he told the visitor. He did not like being treated as a saint. People kissed his hand, but they did the same to every priest. Philippe went back to Rome sure that the friar would not change. He wrote that Saint Pio could not be a saint. He asked that the friar be barred from hearing confessions and kept from Mass until he left, and that he be moved far away.94

The great feasts had been taken from him too. For more than forty years he had led the celebrations of Christmas and Easter at the friary. At Christmas 1960, at Epiphany, and at Easter and Pentecost of 1961 he did not. On Easter Sunday he came into the church only to receive Holy Communion. In April the Holy Office ordered that his Mass last half an hour, or forty minutes at most. He was to say it at a different hour each day, so that the crowds could not gather.95

Padre Rosario wrote that Saint Pio never failed in obedience. Once a spiritual son printed fierce defences of Saint Pio’s innocence and attacked the Church. Saint Pio rebuked him sharply and wanted no part of it, “because you cannot love the Son by wounding the Mother.”96

Then a letter came from Saint John Paul II, now a bishop in Kraków. He was in Rome for the Second Vatican Council, the great meeting of the world’s bishops. A friend of his at home had cancer. She was Wanda Półtawska, a doctor and the mother of four girls. The surgeons had set a date to operate. On 17 November 1962 he wrote to Saint Pio in Latin.97

“Venerable Father, I ask you to pray for a mother of four girls in Kraków, Poland. In the last war she spent five years in a concentration camp in Germany, and now her health and even her life are in grave danger from cancer. May God show his mercy to her and her family, through the Blessed Virgin.”98

— Saint John Paul II, then a bishop in Kraków, 17 November 1962

A cardinal in Rome gave the letter to Angelo Battisti and told him to take it to Saint Pio at once. Battisti worked at the Vatican’s Secretariat of State and also ran the Home for the Relief of Suffering. He drove to San Giovanni Rotondo. To him it looked like one more letter among many. He found Saint Pio in his cell, at prayer. Saint Pio told him to open the letter and read it. Battisti read the Latin aloud, and Saint Pio listened without a word. Then he raised his head. “Angelo, to this one it is not possible to say no!” He bowed his head again and went back to his prayer.99

When the surgeons in Kraków examined Wanda Półtawska before the operation, they found nothing to operate on. On 28 November the bishop wrote to Saint Pio again to thank him. He said that his friend had been suddenly cured before the surgery.100

On 14 May 1963 Padre Agostino died in the friary at San Giovanni Rotondo. He had taught Saint Pio theology at Serracapriola and written down his prayers at Venafro, and in 1922 he had taken Padre Benedetto’s place. He was guardian of the friary from 1944 to 1952, and he came back to live there in 1959. In his cell the friars found a box he had always kept with him, full of his notes and Saint Pio’s letters.101

On 30 July 1964 Pope Saint Paul VI gave Saint Pio back his full freedom to say Mass and hear confessions. When the Church began to change the form of the Mass the next year, the Pope let the old friar go on saying it in Latin as he always had. From November 1966 he was allowed to say it sitting down.102

The Death of Saint Pio

In March 1968 Saint Pio began to use a wheelchair, because his legs would no longer carry him. That September the prayer groups marked fifty years since the wounds. On the Friday before the anniversary Mass, delegates came to the friary from Switzerland and Austria, England and America, India, Brazil, and France.103

The Mass was moved to Sunday, 22 September, and because of the crowds it was sung as a solemn Mass, at five in the morning. Television crews stood in the church. Saint Pio was eighty-one and so weak that he nearly fell on the altar steps. The friars held him up. At one point he grew confused. At the end he fainted and was carried to his room. At six he came down again to bless the crowd in the church, and he fainted a second time.104

That day a friar said to him, “Fifty years, Father!” Saint Pio answered, his mind half elsewhere. “Fifty-eight. It is fifty-eight.” Fifty-eight years had passed since the afternoon under the elm.105

For three years Padre Pellegrino Funicelli had slept in a room joined to Saint Pio’s by an intercom, so that the old man could call him in the night. That evening Saint Pio was in bed by about nine. Until midnight he called five or six times to ask the time. He was quieter than usual and deep in prayer, and his eyes were wet. On the wall hung a portrait of his mother. He said that he saw two mothers there.106

At midnight he anxiously asked Padre Pellegrino to stay with him. He held the friar’s hand tight, trembling like a child with a fever. At about one o’clock he asked whether Mass had been said yet. It was too early, Padre Pellegrino told him. Then Padre Pellegrino would say it for him that morning, Saint Pio said. He asked to make his confession, and after it he asked to renew his religious vows.107

He had one more request. He asked Padre Pellegrino to beg his brother friars to forgive him for all the trouble he had caused them, and to ask them to pray for his soul. Then he gave his blessing to the friars, to his spiritual children, and to the sick. He sent his love to his niece Pia, to his nephew, and to Sister Pia. He said that they knew how much he had loved them.108

Soon after one he said that he could not breathe in bed. He got up, washed his face, and combed his hair. Then he walked out to the verandah, straight and unaided and quick, like a young man. The two of them sat there under the stars for about ten minutes, and Saint Pio looked around him as if he were searching for something.109

Then he grew pale. Padre Pellegrino got him into a wheelchair and back inside, and from the wheelchair into an armchair. His lips were turning blue. Over and over, more and more faintly, he repeated two names: “Jesus, Mary.” Padre Pellegrino started to go for help. “No!” Saint Pio said. “Do not disturb anybody.”110

Padre Pellegrino went anyway. Another friar came, and Saint Pio’s doctor, and two doctors on night duty at the hospital Saint Pio had built. They gave him oxygen, and a priest anointed him. He held his rosary in his hands. At about half past two in the morning of 23 September 1968 he bowed his head gently toward his shoulder and died in peace.111

When the friars prepared his body, they looked for the wounds. There were none. The doctor found his hands, his feet, and his side healed, without a trace and without a scar.112

Saint Pio Is Buried in His Friary

Until the funeral his body lay in the church, and the pilgrims filed past it. The funeral was held on 26 September, and about a hundred thousand people came. The coffin was carried through the streets of the town with hymns and prayers and the Rosary, and on the way the procession stopped for a moment before the hospital. At the funeral Mass the guardian gave the crowd the last blessing that Saint Pio had asked to be passed on to everyone. At half past eight that evening the friars laid him in the crypt of the church of Our Lady of Grace. It was the quiet corner of this land that he had asked for in 1923.113

Pope Saint Paul VI spoke to the superiors of the Capuchin order on 20 February 1971 about the friar whom Rome had twice investigated.114

“Look what fame he had, what a worldwide following he gathered around him! But why? Because he was a philosopher? Because he was a scholar? Because he had means at his disposal? Because he said Mass humbly, heard confessions from morning to night, and was, it is hard to say it, a representative stamped with the stigmata of our Lord. He was a man of prayer and suffering.”115

— Pope Saint Paul VI, 20 February 1971

The hospital went on. Saint Pio had held its shares in his own name. In his will he left them to the Holy See, so that the hospital became the Pope’s own. The prayer groups went on too. When he died there were about seven hundred of them, with some sixty-eight thousand members in fifteen countries. By 2013 there were about 3,300 groups in sixty countries.116

The young priest who had once made his confession to him was now Pope. In 1999 Saint John Paul II declared Saint Pio Blessed, the last step before the Church names a saint. In 2002 the same Pope declared him a saint, after a boy of seven named Matteo Colella woke from a coma in the hospital Saint Pio had built.117

In 1975 Pope Saint Paul VI spoke of the prayer groups. Among all the great and good things Saint Pio had done, he said, he had brought into being “this host, this river of people who pray.”118

For himself Saint Pio had asked for one thing: “I want to be only a poor friar who prays.”119

A Note on the Sources

Saint Pio’s letters are still under copyright, and no free text of them exists. We quote them in short lines and in our own English. The Italian comes from the page numbers printed in Voce di Padre Pio, the Capuchin magazine of San Giovanni Rotondo, and from the Capuchin portal padrepio.it. The same portal prints the diaries of Padre Agostino and Nina Campanile and the memoirs of Padre Paolino and Padre Raffaele; we read them only there.

Some dates and details differ. Cardinal Bertone dates the first marks to 7 September 1910, and the first written report is dated 8 September 1911. Italian Wikipedia places the words to Don Pannullo on the morning after that report. A 2022 notice of Raffaele Iaria’s book says that Don Pannullo learned of the marks a month after the ordination. We give the words without a date. Saint Pio’s letter of 22 October 1918 says he was alone in the choir, while the Vatican newspaper’s obituary of 1968 says a friar found him unconscious behind the high altar. We follow the letter. His account of crawling to his cell comes from a talk with his friend Don Peppino Orlando and has no date. The boy’s wish to be a friar with a beard survives only in English, and the friar’s visit is dated both 1897 and 1901. The window march of 1923 has no date, and we quote the Observer from a 2018 article without having seen the original page. The decree of 1931 is dated 13 May in Padre Raffaele’s memoir and 23 May in the Capuchin chronology; it reached the friary on 9 June. Sources differ on the coin of 1940, on 250 or 300 beds in 1956, and on 2:20 or 2:30 as the hour of death. Padre Pellegrino’s written account of 1968 and his later interview differ on small points of order. The words of Pope Saint Paul VI about the river of people who pray appear in two forms.

Some stories are left out of the life because their sources are weak. Pius XI is said to have told an archbishop in 1933 that he had been badly informed about Saint Pio, but no named source gives the words. Writers differ on whether Agostino Gemelli came in 1920 on his own or was sent by Rome. Who ordered the recordings of 1960 is not settled. English Wikipedia gives Saint John XXIII a harsh private phrase, and we did not find the primary text. A Capuchin author tells that the archbishop of Manfredonia showed the Pope that the kisses on the tapes were his own sister’s kisses on the friar’s hand; this has not been confirmed. The account of Paolo Philippe’s visit of 1961 comes from the Capuchin portal. The historian Sergio Luzzatto published the papers of Saint John XXIII and Maccari in 2007 and added charges of his own: that eleven early letters copy passages from Saint Gemma Galgani, and that Saint Pio bought carbolic acid. His defenders answer that the acid was for sterilizing syringes, and that Bishop Rossi in 1921 found simplicity and a playful spirit in the man. We have not read Luzzatto’s book or the printed volumes of the letters.

How This Life Was Researched

Five sets of research notes and fifteen scene cards lie behind this life, drawn from more than sixty sources in Italian, English, Latin, and Spanish. For this version we also read fourteen articles on the Capuchin portal padrepio.it about his mother’s death, the silence of 1931 to 1933, his father, his directors, the inquiries of 1960 and 1961, and the funeral. The official texts include the pages of the Dicastery for the Causes of Saints, the homilies of Saint John Paul II in 1999 and 2002, Cardinal Bertone’s homily of 2008, and the decree on Saint Pio’s virtues. Saint Pio’s letters come through the page numbers quoted in Voce di Padre Pio. The eyewitnesses include John McCaffery’s book of 1978, Padre Pellegrino Funicelli’s account of the last night, and the Vatican newspaper of 1968. The old newspapers of 1920 and 1923 come through a 2018 article.
