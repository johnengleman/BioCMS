At Byrra, the place of execution at Myra, a crowd had gathered to watch three men die. The condemned stood with linen cloths over their faces. The executioner had already raised his sword when the bishop of Myra reached the place, running. He took the sword out of the executioner’s hands, threw it to the ground, and set the three men free.

That is the oldest known story of Nicholas of Myra. It comes from a Greek text called the Praxis de stratelatis, the “Story of the Generals.” An unknown author wrote it, and scholars date it anywhere from soon after Nicholas’s death to the late sixth century, when a priest of Constantinople was already quoting a piece of it. There is no Santa Claus in it, and no bag of gold. There is a bishop who runs toward a sword.

Today Nicholas is one of the most loved saints on earth. Orthodox writers rank him in veneration just after the Mother of God and John the Baptist, and call him simply the Wonderworker. He is patron of sailors and merchants, bakers and travelers and children, and of lands from Greece and Russia to Sicily and Lorraine. In the West he is Saint Nicholas of Bari, after the city that keeps his bones. Yet when the Catholic Encyclopedia came to write about him in 1911, it admitted how little lies under all that love.

“…scarcely anything historically certain about him except that he was Bishop of Myra in the fourth century.”

— Michael Ott, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1911

How does a man of whom almost nothing is certain become the saint that half of Christendom calls on in trouble? The stories of Nicholas came in layers, over a thousand years: an early account of a rescue, a ninth-century Life, medieval legends, and a bold voyage to carry off his bones. Some layers are old and sober, and some are late and fanciful. What is striking is that all of them show the same man. He runs toward people in danger. He gives in the dark and asks for silence. He calls himself a sinner. And when people call on him, he comes. To find him, we have to walk through the layers, beginning with the town where the oldest Life says he was born.

An Only Son in Patara

The first full Life of Nicholas was written in the early ninth century by a monk named Michael the Archimandrite, nearly five hundred years after Nicholas died. Michael tells us that Nicholas was born in Patara, a town of Lycia, the only son of Christian parents who were rich. Tradition puts his birth about the year 270. Michael gives no names for the parents. The Russian tradition, which comes from Dimitri of Rostov in the seventeenth century, calls them Theophanes and Nonna, and the Russian Church keeps a feast of his birth on 29 July.

Michael adds one story about the baby. On Wednesdays and Fridays, the weekly fast days, the infant Nicholas would take his mother’s milk only once in the day. Monks loved to tell such stories about the saints they admired, and the Bari scholar Gerardo Cioffari calls this one a monastic commonplace. It tells us more about the monks who prayed to Nicholas than about a child in Patara. But it tells us how they saw him: as a man who belonged to God from his first days.

Then his parents died. The only son was left alone, and he was left rich. What a man of faith should do with a fortune is a question as old as the Gospel, and the first story that Michael tells of the grown Nicholas is his answer.

Gold Through a Window at Night

Next door to Nicholas lived a man who had once been well-off and had fallen into such poverty that he could give none of his three daughters a dowry. Nicholas came to the house at night, threw a bag of gold through the window, and was gone before anyone saw him.

He came back, in the same way, with a second bag. When the third night came, the father stayed awake. He heard the gold land in the house. The sound gave Nicholas away, and the father caught him and fell at his feet.

Nicholas lifted him up. All he asked was silence: he made the father swear that he would tell no one what had happened. The father, as Michael tells it, called him the savior of himself and his three daughters, after God. That small phrase matters. In the story the gift is God’s, and Nicholas is only the hand that threw it through the window.

The oath of silence is the one wish of Nicholas’s that the world has refused to grant. The story of the gold has been told across Christendom ever since, and it explains why Nicholas became the saint of gift-giving, and, at some remove, a source of Santa Claus. So the man who wanted no one to know became one of the most famous givers in the world. Yet the next story Michael tells is a public one. The Church of Myra came looking for him.

The First Man Through the Door

The bishop of Myra died, and the bishops of the nearby cities gathered with the clergy of Myra to choose his successor. God revealed to one of them what to do. He was to stand at the door of the church during the night, and the first man to come in quietly would be the one, and his name was Nicholas. That man was to be their bishop.

The rest of the bishops and clergy stayed inside, praying. At dawn a man came to the church ahead of everyone else, and the bishop at the door took hold of him and asked, “How are you called, child?” Michael gives his answer.

“Nicholas, a sinner, a servant of your Holiness, O master.”

— Nicholas at the church door, in the Life by Michael the Archimandrite, early ninth century

The man who had thrown gold through a window to stay unseen was led into the middle of the church and shown to the crowd as the shepherd God had sent. They seated him on the bishop’s throne of the chief city of Lycia. Michael told it as a story of God’s choosing, and the frame of a message and a door is his. But the core of it has other support. Gratian’s Decretum, a medieval book of Church law, remembers Nicholas as a layman elected bishop, and Cioffari thinks that statement very likely rests on a document now lost.

Notice the word he chose for himself. “A sinner” is how Nicholas names himself here, at the moment of his election. He will use the same words again, much later, in a stranger setting: in the dreams of an emperor.

A Bishop in a City of Artemis

Myra, today the town of Demre in Turkey, was an ancient city, and its great temple belonged to Artemis. Michael says it surpassed every other building in height and ornament, and Cioffari points out that ancient inscriptions from Myra bear him out. According to Michael, Nicholas tore it down, and the demons that had lived in it fled. Michael also remembers him as a bishop who fought the teachings of Arius and Sabellius, two teachers whose ideas about the Trinity the Church rejected.

Michael had heard one more thing, from a monk who passed on what was still remembered of Nicholas. People said the bishop was venerable to look at, that a sweet fragrance hung about him, and that heretics who met him went away healed of their sickness of unbelief. It is a hearsay portrait, written down centuries later. It is also the closest thing we have to a face.

Later Greek writers added a time of suffering. In their accounts Nicholas was imprisoned in the persecution under the emperor Diocletian and confessed his faith there. The story first appears around the year 900, and Alban Butler, in his Lives of the Saints, weighed it honestly.

“The silence of other authors make many justly suspect these circumstances.”

— Alban Butler, The Lives of the Saints, 6 December

The same care applies to another scene in the later Lives: Nicholas among the bishops at the Council of Nicaea.

The Council He May Have Attended

In 325 the bishops met in council at Nicaea over the teaching of Arius. Michael, who wrote the first full Life and knew that Nicholas had opposed Arius, never mentions the council at all. The first of his biographers to put Nicholas at Nicaea is Nicetas of Paphlagonia, before 890. The Synaxarion, the Church’s book of short lives for each day, repeats it around 900. By the late tenth century Symeon Metaphrastes could describe him as a member of the council who helped bring its work about.

Nor do the lists of bishops at Nicaea settle the question. By one modern count, the shorter lists, with about two hundred names, leave him out. The Catholic Encyclopedia of 1911 put it more bluntly: “his name is not mentioned in any of the old lists of bishops that attended this council.” Yet longer lists, with about three hundred names, do include him, among them the list of Theodore the Lector, written about 515, the earliest source to name him at the council. The scholars disagree about that entry. Anrich, who edited the Greek texts about Nicholas, thought another hand added it later. Cioffari thinks it is genuine, and guesses that Theodore found the name in the ancient Life that Eustratios of Constantinople quoted in 583.

So whether Nicholas sat among the bishops at Nicaea is still an open question. The Church’s memory has long placed him there, and history cannot confirm it. One well-loved story says that, burning with zeal for the faith, he struck Arius at the council. He was stripped of his office and jailed for it, and he was restored when Christ and the Mother of God appeared to him with a Gospel book and a bishop’s omophorion. Cioffari counts it among the popular developments of the legend, and one modern account dates its first appearance to the fourteenth century.

But the oldest story of Nicholas has nothing to do with councils. It is about three men, a governor, and a sword.

Silver and Gold at Byrra

Three imperial officers, Nepotianus, Ursus, and Eupoleonis, had put in at Andriake, the harbor of Myra, on their way to put down a revolt in Phrygia. A riot had broken out over their soldiers, and Nicholas had gone down to quiet it. There word reached him that the governor, Eustathius, had condemned three innocent citizens of Myra to be beheaded. Nicholas crossed the city with the officers behind him, and at the end he was running. He reached Byrra just as the sword was raised over men whose faces were already covered for death. He took the sword, threw it down, and freed them.

Then he went to the governor. He pushed open the door of the praetorium, the governor’s hall. Eustathius came forward to honor him, but Nicholas turned away and told him to his face that he would report his crimes to the emperor. Eustathius fell to his knees and protested that he was not the guilty one. Two leading men of the province, Eudoxius and Simonides, were. Nicholas would not let him hide behind them. Those two had not done this, he said. Silver and gold had, for the bishop had learned that the governor was to receive more than two hundred pounds of silver for putting the men to death.

In the end the three officers spoke earnestly for Eustathius, and once the charges against the three men were cleared, Nicholas pardoned him. The fierce bishop who had thrown the executioner’s sword on the ground forgave the man who had condemned the innocent. Cioffari finds such forceful dealing with the powerful in keeping with Nicholas’s character.

The officers received his blessing and sailed on. They could not know that they would soon stand where the three men of Byrra had stood.

A Stranger in the Emperor’s Dream

In Constantinople the three officers came home to a hero’s welcome, and it made them an enemy. The master of the forces, eaten up with envy, promised the prefect Ablabius seventeen hundred pounds of gold to destroy them. Ablabius told the emperor Constantine that the officers were plotting against him, and in the end the emperor ordered them beheaded in the night. In prison Nepotianus remembered what the bishop of Myra had done for the three men in Lycia. All three prayed to the God of Saint Nicholas to save them as he had saved those others.

That night Nicholas appeared to the emperor Constantine, who demanded to know who he was and how he had got into the palace. The visitor gave his name as Bishop Nicholas, a sinner, who lived in the chief city of Lycia, and he ordered the emperor to free the three men he had thrown into prison. Before the night was out he came to the prefect Ablabius with the same command and named himself again: Nicholas, a sinner, a servant of God, the bishop of Myra. Then he was gone.

When the emperor woke, he sent for Ablabius, and the two found that they had seen the same thing. Constantine had the officers brought before him and asked what magic they used to trouble his sleep, and whether they knew anyone named Nicholas. A sermon in honor of Nicholas, attributed to Proclus, the patriarch of Constantinople, though scholars doubt that he wrote it, tells the emperor’s question in short, baffled words.

“How is it that you have sent a certain Nicholas to punish me?”

— Emperor Constantine to the three officers, in the Encomium on Saint Nicholas attributed to Proclus of Constantinople

Constantine set them free, and he told them plainly where their lives had come from: from the man they had called on, Saint Nicholas, and not from the emperor. He sent them away with vessels for the altar and a Gospel book of gold, and they went to Myra to give thanks.

Money runs through both halves of the story. At Myra it was silver, more than two hundred pounds of it, promised to a governor. In Constantinople it was gold, seventeen hundred pounds, promised to a prefect. Many popular retellings leave out both sums. The story also touches known history. Nepotianus and Ablabius appear in the pagan historians Zosimus and Eunapius. Cioffari accepts the frame of the story as historical, though he reads the dream visits as the storyteller’s way of showing a saint’s power. The older Roman Martyrology singles out this half of the story in its entry for his feast. Nicholas, far away, appeared to Constantine in a vision and turned him from anger to mercy toward men who had called on him.

Twice in one night the bishop of Myra appeared in dreams to two of the most powerful men in the empire. Each time he gave his name as he had at the church door: a sinner. Later stories widened the circle of his care, from three condemned men to a whole hungry city.

Grain Ships at Andriake

Once grain ran short in Lycia. At Andriake, the harbor of Myra, grain ships from Alexandria lay at anchor, bound for Constantinople, and the people of the harbor sent word to Nicholas. He came down from the city and asked the captains to unload a little from each ship. The grain belonged to the state, the captains answered, and was not theirs to give. Nicholas asked only a hundred measures from each ship, and promised to answer for the loss to the treasury officials at Constantinople.

They unloaded the grain. When the ships reached Constantinople, Michael says, their cargo was found to be the same as when it had left Alexandria. And the grain left at Andriake fed the people who received it for two years.

By the sixth century, Cioffari notes, the story was already in circulation. In the early eighth, a sermon attributed to Andrew of Crete told it with a touch of its own. In that version Nicholas stood among the sailors in the manner of a sailor, and, as the story went, left three gold coins on the ship. The bishop who gave in secret gave here too, quietly, with his own hands. And his care was turning more and more toward the harbor, and toward the men who went to sea.

The Sailors Who Called His Name

Michael tells of a ship caught in a storm. The sailors called out to Nicholas, the bishop of Myra, to save them. Then, by God’s order, he was there among them, and he spoke.

“Behold, you have called me, and I am here to help you.”

— Nicholas to the sailors in the storm, in the Life by Michael the Archimandrite

The sailors were saved. When they came ashore they asked after the man who had saved them and learned that he was in the church. They went there to find him. He had come in plainly dressed, like one of the many clergy. Yet the sailors, who had never seen him before, knew him at once as the man who had stood by them in the storm. They fell at his feet.

He did not let them leave with only a miracle. He told them, Michael says, that their greed and their lust were the kind of sins that invite God’s correction. The rescuer was also a pastor, and he scolded the men he had saved. It is one of the most human moments in all the stories about him.

Cioffari sees in this story the beginning of Nicholas’s patronage of sailors. Myra was a stopover for pilgrims bound for the Holy Land, and the travelers who passed through it took him as their own. Generations of seamen would call his name in storms, and he would be remembered as the saint who comes when called. But first the bishop himself had to die.

Angels at the Last Hour

Michael says almost nothing about the end. Nicholas left his mortal life, and his body was buried in his church, where it gave off a sweet perfume. Symeon Metaphrastes, in the tenth century, adds a short illness, and has Nicholas die giving thanks to God, carried to his grave by bishops and clergy with torches. He died on 6 December. The year is not known.

The Golden Legend, the collection of saints’ lives compiled by Jacobus de Voragine in 1275, tells of his last hour.

“…he prayed our Lord that he would send him his angels; and inclining his head he saw the angels come to him, whereby he knew well that he should depart.”

— Jacobus de Voragine, The Golden Legend, 1275

He began the psalm In te, Domine, speravi, “In you, O Lord, I have hoped,” and prayed it as far as the words in manus tuas, “into your hands.” Then he died. What happened next, in this telling, is the beginning of a story that has never stopped.

“…a fountain of oil sprang out from the head unto his feet; and unto this day holy oil issueth out of his body…”

— Jacobus de Voragine, The Golden Legend, 1275

The fragrant liquid from his tomb drew pilgrims to Myra for centuries. Then, in the spring of 1087, it drew armed men from Italy.

Pilgrims at the Tomb in Myra

Long before then, the bishop of Myra was honored far beyond Lycia. His story was told in sermons, and an emperor built a church in his honor in Constantinople, by one account Justinian in the sixth century. By the seventh or eighth century his face was painted on an icon at Sinai, and in the eighth his image appeared in a fresco in the church of Santa Maria Antiqua in Rome. Early in the eighth century a sermon attributed to Andrew of Crete was preached in his honor at the tomb itself, and praised him as one who turned hurricanes into breezes.

As his fame grew, his story grew with it. In the sixth century there lived another Nicholas, the abbot of Holy Sion, who made a pilgrimage to Jerusalem and was remembered for calming a storm at sea and for many healings. Later writers merged the two lives into one, and by the tenth century the bishop of Myra had been given the abbot’s pilgrimage and the abbot’s miracles. In 1751 a scholar named Falconius even argued that the two were one man, and others, among them Assemani and Putignani, answered him. Here the abbot’s stories are left to the abbot.

For centuries the tomb at Myra was one of the great shrines of the Christian East. Then the world around it changed. In 1036, by one account, Saracens occupied Lycia and forbade the honoring of Nicholas at his tomb. Raiders ravaged the district, and the cities of the West began to look at the shrine with a different kind of interest.

Forty-Seven Men from Bari

In the spring of 1087 a fleet of merchant ships from the Italian city of Bari was sailing for Antioch with grain and other goods. The men of Bari had a plan that went beyond trade: they meant to take the bones of Saint Nicholas home with them. They had heard that the Venetians intended the same thing and had even brought iron tools for the work. The Bari ships got there first.

In April forty-seven armed men went up from the ships to the church, while others stayed behind to guard the ships. Raiders had ravaged the district, and the people of Myra had fled into the mountains. Only four keepers of the shrine were left.

“…forty-seven knights of Bari, and four monks showed to them the sepulchre of S. Nicholas.”

— Jacobus de Voragine, The Golden Legend, 1275

Showing was not quite how it went. At first the keepers, thinking the strangers had come to pray, led them only to the altar and to the place where the saint’s oil was drawn. When the men of Bari said what they had come for, the keepers wept and tore their garments, and the Bari men bound them and set guards at the doors. Then, in the Greek account, a priest named Lupus let a glass vial of the saint’s oil fall on the stones, and it did not break. The men of Bari took it as a sign that Nicholas consented to go with them. A Bari man named Matthew, sword in hand, demanded that a monk show him the tomb. The monk pointed to the place where the oil came out. Then he told them of a vision the saint had given three of his servants a year before. If the people who had fled did not return to Myra, he would leave for a foreign land.

Matthew put away his sword and took up a mallet. He broke through the marble pavement and then the lid of the tomb. Inside, the coffin was full of liquid up to the navel of the body, and a fragrance rose from it so strong that it reached the men waiting at the ships, nearly three miles away. So wrote Nicephorus of Bari, whose account is thought to have been set down within a week of the relics’ arrival at Bari. The sailors carried the bones down to the ships and left the monks of Myra to their grief.

Twenty Days to Bari

The voyage home did not go smoothly. A north wind and heavy tides pushed against the ships, and at last they put in at a place called Perdikca, where the sea lay calm but the men had no strength to go on. Soon the sailors were accusing one another of having taken something from the relics. They decided to bring out the Gospels so that every man could swear he had taken nothing, and five confessed that they had kept fragments for themselves. When these were given back, all swore on the Gospels that nothing more was held back, and the fleet went on. One of the sailors, a man named Disigius, dreamed that Saint Nicholas told him not to be afraid: in twenty days they would all arrive together at Bari.

They came into Bari on 9 May 1087. Crowds ran to the harbor, and the clergy came out in their vestments, singing. The citizens argued over where the relics should rest, and Elias, the abbot of the monastery of Saint Benedict, asked to keep them until the townspeople agreed. The sailors let him. Ursus, the archbishop, was away at Canosa. When he came back and wanted the relics for his own church, the quarrel turned to fighting in which men died. In the end the people carried the relics to the praetorium and laid them in the church of Saint Eustratius. It was torn down not long after to make room for a church of Saint Nicholas.

On the first night and the day that followed, Nicephorus and the Greek account agree, forty-seven people were cured. On the Thursday, Nicephorus writes, a young man who had been possessed for five years, deaf, mute, and blind, was made whole. A great basilica rose over the bones, and Pope Urban II laid them beneath its main altar. Russian tradition names Theodore, an envoy of Metropolitan John II of Kiev, as an eyewitness of the translation and the author of a narrative of it.

The sailors of Bari were given a privilege: the right to be buried along the walls of the basilica. In one Bari family, the Poveromo, family tradition says that a bone carved with the saint’s face has been handed down. A later Venetian account says that the Venetians came to Myra in 1100 and took what the men of Bari had left behind. The saint had left the East. He was about to belong to the whole Church.

The Saint Who Comes When Called

At Bari the faithful have long called the liquid from the tomb the manna of Saint Nicholas. The emperor Manuel, in one of his laws, mentioned a fragrant oily substance flowing from the saint’s relics there. The bones were examined between 1953 and 1957 and laid in a new tomb in the crypt, and the manna, one modern Orthodox account says, still flows.

East and West keep his feast on 6 December, the day of his death, and the Orthodox keep 9 May as the feast of the translation of his relics to Bari. Saint Nikodemos of Mount Athos wrote a service for the translation, to be chanted on 10 May, though in Greek lands the day is kept on 9, 10, or 20 May. The Russian Church revived its feast of his birth, on 29 July, in 2004. In 1888 the Russian painter Repin went back to the oldest known story of all and painted Nicholas saving the three innocent men at Myra.

The gold through the window made him the saint of secret gifts, and at some remove the ancestor of Santa Claus. It is a long way from Byrra. Yet even there the old pattern holds: a gift given in secret by someone who asked not to be thanked.

So who was he? Almost nothing about Nicholas of Myra can be proved, except that he was a bishop in the fourth century. But the Church’s memory of him is not a heap of loose tales. Across a thousand years, in Greek and Latin, Russian and Italian, it has kept a single face. He is the bishop running toward the sword, the heir throwing gold through a window and asking for silence, and the man at the church door who called himself a sinner. He is the stranger in the emperor’s dream who would not let three innocent men die. And he is the plain cleric in the church at Myra whom the sailors knew at once as the one who had come to them in the storm.

That is why sailors and travelers, merchants and children, whole nations and single families still call on him. They call because of what he told the sailors, the words that every layer of his story repeats in its own way: “Behold, you have called me, and I am here to help you.”

A Note on the Sources

The year of Nicholas’s death is given as 342 by Butler, from the history of the translation, as 343 by the Golden Legend, and as 345 or 352 by the Catholic Encyclopedia and a modern Orthodox account. No date is firm. Michael’s Life is dated between 814 and 843 by Anrich, but around 700 by Cioffari. The Praxis de stratelatis is dated between 460 and 580 by Anrich, and between the death of Nicholas and the middle of the fifth century by Cioffari. Eustratios of Constantinople, in the late sixth century, quotes a fragment of the story of the three generals from a lost Life. Theodore the Lector’s date is given as about 515 by Cioffari and about 500 in a summary by Roger Pearse. The counts of names in the shorter and longer lists of Nicaea, and the date of the slap of Arius, come from one modern summary, which is not a scholar’s edition.

Accounts of 1087 disagree in small ways. The first cures at Bari number forty-seven in Nicephorus and the Greek account, counted over the first night and the next day, and thirty on the first day in Butler. The raiders whom the people of Myra feared are Persians in the Greek account and Turks in Nicephorus. In the Greek account the priest Lupus drops the vial; in Nicephorus a priest sets it on a column and it falls. Butler says Justinian’s church in Constantinople was built about 430, which cannot be right, since Justinian reigned from 527 to 565. The story of the dowry survives in three very old and very different versions. In the one that seems oldest, there are two girls, and the boy Nicholas takes the gold for them from his own living parents; another version has four girls. The pilgrimage to Jerusalem, the calming of the storm on that voyage, the title of abbot of Holy Sion, and probably the story of a tax relief won in Constantinople belong to Nicholas of Sion. Cioffari thinks the story of the sailors also fits the abbot better, though it stands in Michael’s Life of the bishop. The Saracen occupation of Lycia in 1036 and the Venetian voyage of 1100 each rest on a single later source.

How This Life Was Researched

This life draws on twenty sources, read in English, with one in German. They include the Praxis de stratelatis, the oldest known account of Nicholas, and the ninth-century Life by Michael the Archimandrite. They also include the sermons of Proclus and Andrew of Crete, the Golden Legend, Butler’s Lives of the Saints, and two accounts of the translation to Bari, one written by Nicephorus of Bari in 1087. Every story was graded by how early and how independent its first witness is, from the Praxis to legends first written down a thousand years after his death. The original Greek and Latin texts were not read; every Greek and Latin source was read in an English translation or a scholar’s summary. The quotations from Michael’s Life come from the translation by Bryson Sewell, which Roger Pearse released into the public domain. Because the only English translation of the Praxis is under copyright, the story of the generals is told here in paraphrase. The research flagged the two bribes in that story, which many popular English retellings leave out.
