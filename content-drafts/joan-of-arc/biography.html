On the morning of 23 February 1431, in her prison at Rouen, a young woman woke because a voice had spoken to her. Nothing had touched her. She sat up on her bed, joined her hands, and begged the voice to ask God how she should answer the court that held her life in its hands. The voice said to her: “Answer boldly; God will help thee.”

The next day she told her judges about it, and then she did as the voice had said. She warned the bishop who presided over the court that he was putting himself in great danger. Later in the hearing she reminded them of “a saying among children, that ‘Sometimes one is hanged for speaking the truth.’” Then the court asked her whether she was in the grace of God. “If I am not, may God place me there; if I am, may God so keep me.”

The girl who gave that answer was about nineteen years old, and she could not read. Only a few years earlier she had been spinning wool and minding her father’s animals in a village called Domrémy. Since then she had ridden with an army, helped to break the English siege of Orléans, and stood beside the altar at Reims while her king was crowned. Now she was a prisoner in irons, facing a bishop and a bench of learned men, and before the end of May she would be burned in the Old Market of Rouen.

Everything in her story turns on the voice that woke her in her cell. It had first spoken to her when she was thirteen. It had sent her to the king, and it had told her when to attack and when to wait. She said she had never asked it for any reward but one, the salvation of her soul. Now it had brought her to this room, and the men in front of her wanted her to disown it. A Church court condemned her in 1431. Twenty-five years later another Church court overturned the verdict, and in 1920 the Catholic Church declared her a saint. What did she hear in her father’s garden, and would she keep faith with it once it stopped bringing victories?

The answer begins in a village where the neighbors teased her for going to church too often.

Teased for Going to Church

Domrémy, Joan told her judges, was really one village with its neighbor Greux, and the church where her neighbors remembered her was dedicated to Saint Rémi. Joan’s father, Jacques d’Arc, and her mother, Isabelle, worked the land, and the neighbors who spoke of them years later called them good Catholics with a good name. At home their daughter was Jeannette. Of her surname, she later told her judges, “I know nothing.”

She spun and sewed, did the housework, and sometimes took her father’s animals out to graze. A younger girl named Hauviette, three or four years her junior, was her friend and slept at the d’Arc house, out of affection, as the Latin record of her testimony puts it. Joan was proud of her needle. “In sewing and spinning I fear no woman in Rouen,” she told the learned men who were trying her.

What the neighbors remembered best was church. Joan went so often that people in the village teased her for it. When the bell rang while she was out in the fields, she knelt where she stood. Jean Waterin admitted long afterward that he and others used to mock her when she went off by herself to speak to God. The village sexton, Perrin Drapier, sometimes forgot to ring the bell for Compline, the night prayer. Joan scolded him for it, and then, to make him more careful, she promised him a gift of wool.

One witness remembered, the old French Lives say, that she often lent her own bed to the poor and slept on the floor herself. The same Lives describe her as cheerful, a girl who liked to see a happy face around her. Her friends had only one complaint about her, and it was her devotion.

The war came close. When armed men rode through the district, the people of Domrémy fled with their animals to the town of Neufchâteau, and Joan and her parents went with them. They stayed four or five days, and Joan spent them helping the woman who kept the inn, a woman called La Rousse.

Not far from the village stood a tree the villagers called the Ladies’ Tree. The old women said fairies haunted it. Every year, on the Sunday the village called the Sunday of the Fountains, the children went out to it with little loaves. They ate and danced under it and drank at the spring nearby, and Hauviette went there with Joan. Years later her judges asked her about that tree. She told them she had sung there more than she had danced, and that she had never seen a fairy there, that she knew of. None of the neighbors who described the tree afterward claimed to have seen one either.

When those neighbors were questioned a generation later, they described a girl who was good, simple, and sweet. None of these neighbors spoke of a vision or of any sign. The thing that changed her life happened when she was thirteen, at noon, one summer day, in her father’s garden.

A Voice in Her Father’s Garden

She described it to her judges in Rouen, with her life at stake, in the plainest words she had.

“I was thirteen when I had a Voice from God for my help and guidance. The first time that I heard this Voice, I was very much frightened; it was mid-day, in the summer, in my father’s garden.”

— Joan of Arc, to her judges at Rouen, 22 February 1431

She had not been fasting the day before, she said. The voice came from her right, from the direction of the church, and a light came with it. Ever after, she said, she rarely heard the voice without the light.

The first to come was Saint Michael, “surrounded by the Angels of Heaven.” Later Saint Catherine and Saint Margaret came to her, and she knew them, she said, “by the greeting they give me.” Asked whether she had really seen Michael and the angels, she answered plainly.

“I saw them with my bodily eyes as well as I see you; when they went from me, I wept. I should have liked to be taken away with them.”

— Joan of Arc, at Rouen, 27 February 1431

She had embraced Catherine and Margaret, and when the judges asked about it, she told them, “It is well to know, they smelled good.” Still, she thought it “more proper and respectful to kiss their feet.”

Then the voice told her what God wanted of her. “It said to me: ‘Go, raise the siege which is being made before the City of Orleans.’” The English were besieging Orléans, on the Loire, and the king she was to help, Charles, had not yet been crowned. Her first answer was honest. “I replied that I was but a poor girl, who knew nothing of riding or fighting.”

The voice did not let her go. In the countryside people already spoke of a prophecy that a maiden would come out of the Bois-Chenu. Joan said only that God had sent her, and later she put it in words that left no room for doubt. “I would rather have been torn asunder by four horses than have come into France without God’s leave.”

When she finally left home, she went with her uncle, Durand Laxart. A neighbor, Gérard Guillemette, saw them pass his father’s house. She called out to his father a goodbye that the Latin record gives as “Ad Deum,” to God: “Adieu, I am going to Vaucouleurs.” Jean Waterin heard her say goodbye as she went through Greux. Hauviette did not know she was going. When she found out, she wept a great deal, she said, because she loved Joan for her goodness.

Joan was going to see a man who would tell her uncle to take her home.

Take Her Home to Her Father

The man she needed at Vaucouleurs was named Baudricourt. She had never seen him, yet she told her judges that she recognized him. Her first visit came in May 1428, when her uncle brought her. Baudricourt gave her uncle his answer: “Take her home to her father and give her a good whipping.”

She came back. By her own account he refused her twice and received her only the third time. Before she left Vaucouleurs she announced that the French had just been beaten in a fight near Orléans, the skirmish later called the Battle of the Herrings. The news arrived days afterward, and it was true.

In February 1429 she rode out of Vaucouleurs in men’s clothes, with an escort that included two men of Baudricourt’s, Jean de Metz and Bertrand de Poulengey. The road to the king ran for eleven days through country held by his enemies, and it crossed many rivers. The bull of her canonization says that the king heard from Baudricourt that she had come through prodigiose, like a wonder, and he agreed to see her.

On the way she stopped at Fierbois, at the church of Saint Catherine, one of the saints of her voices, and heard three Masses there in a single morning. Within weeks, that church would matter again.

Early in March 1429 she came to the castle of Chinon, where the king was. He was not ready to see her.

The King Among His Courtiers

At first Charles would not receive her. Dunois, the commander at besieged Orléans, had sent two men to the king, and they told him later that Charles kept her waiting for two days. She was lodged in a tower of the castle of Coudray. A page of fourteen or fifteen named Louis de Coutes was lodged there with her by day, and at night women stayed with her. Louis often saw her on her knees in prayer, and sometimes she was weeping.

When at last she was brought into the king’s presence, he was standing among his courtiers. The old French Lives say she walked straight to him. He told her he was not the Dauphin, and she told him that he was. She herself said simply that she knew him among them all “by the counsel of my Voice.” The old Lives also give her first words to him: “I am called Joan the Maid, and I am sent by God to bring help to you, gentle lord, and to your kingdom.”

Then she told him something in private. What it was, no one knows. The bull of 1920 says only that she revealed to him certain secrets known to God alone. The Catholic Encyclopedia of 1910 reports the most common belief, that it touched a doubt about his own birth. Two years later her judges asked what revelations the king had received. She gave them a flat refusal: “You will not have them from me this year.”

Soon the king and his household were amazed to hear her talk about the war as well as the knights and squires who had been fighting it all along. Meanwhile the young duke of Alençon was hunting quail at Saint-Florent when a messenger came to tell him that a maiden had arrived at the king’s court. Next day he rode to Chinon and found her talking with Charles. She asked who he was. “The duke of Alençon,” the king said.

“You are most welcome,” Joan told him, and then she added, “The more of the royal blood of France are together, the better.”

The following morning she bowed to the king at Mass, and afterward he took her aside into a room with d’Alençon and La Trémoille. There she asked Charles to give his kingdom to the King of Heaven. After dinner she went out to the meadow and ran with a lance, and d’Alençon, who watched her, gave her a horse.

Charles still wanted more than a girl who could handle a lance. Before he would trust her with an army, he sent her to be examined by the theologians at Poitiers.

Not Come to Show Signs

For three weeks the doctors of Poitiers questioned her, and a quarter of a century later the witnesses still remembered her answers. One of the examiners told her that God would not wish her believed without a sign. “I am not come to Poitiers to shew signs,” she answered, “but send me to Orleans.” Another witness remembered the day two of the doctors came to her lodging. “I know neither A nor B,” she told them. Then she told them why she had come: “I am come from the King of Heaven to raise the siege of Orleans and to conduct the King to Rheims for his crowning and anointing.”

Months later, in Bourges, she told her hostess how she had answered the learned men: “There is more in our Lord’s books than in yours.”

The verdict went her way. One of the examiners said publicly that they saw nothing in her but what can be in a good Christian and a true Catholic. D’Alençon remembered that the clerics found nothing in her against the Catholic faith. The queen of Sicily and her ladies examined her too, and reported that she was a true virgin. The written record of Poitiers, which might have told us much, is lost.

In those weeks she dictated a letter to the English, since she could not write, and addressed it first of all to their king. It did not ask them for anything, but told them what they must do.

“… give up to the Maid, who is sent hither by God, the King of Heaven, the keys of all the good towns in France.”

— Joan of Arc, letter to the English, 22 March 1429

At her trial the judges read the letter back to her, and she corrected them. Where their copy said “give up to the Maid,” she said, it should read “give up to the King.” She said that two phrases, “Chieftain of war” and “body for body,” had not been in her letter at all. She also told them that she put “Jhesus Maria” on some of her letters, and sometimes a cross. The cross was a sign to her own side not to do what the letter said.

At Tours the king had armor made for her and gave her a household of her own, and Louis de Coutes became her page. She also sent for a sword. Her voices told her it lay buried at Fierbois, in the church of Saint Catherine where she had heard three Masses on her way to the king. She wrote to the churchmen there and sent an armorer of Tours, a man she had never seen, to look for it. It was found in the ground, rusty, with five crosses on the blade, and when the priests rubbed it the rust fell off at once. They sent it to her.

The sword was not what she cared about most. Her banner was white, sprinkled with lilies, and on it was painted the world with an angel on each side. Later she told her judges which of the two she loved more: “Better, forty times better, my banner than my sword!” In battle, she said, she carried it “to save killing any one, for I have never killed any one.”

At Blois the army gathered with a convoy of food for the besieged city. The old Lives say the priests sang the Veni Creator in front of her banner. Louis saw her receive Communion with the soldiers, and she told the men to put their trust in the Lord and to go to confession. “By my martin, they will be well led; have no doubt,” she told them, in a phrase that one chronicler puts in her mouth again and again. No one knows for certain what a “martin” was. It may have been a stick, or it may have been Saint Martin.

Late in April 1429 the convoy moved out of Blois, toward the river and the city that had been waiting for her.

The Wind Turned on the Loire

The man in command at Orléans was Dunois, whom everyone called the Bastard of Orléans. The army came up along the left bank of the Loire, and he met her on the bank of the river. A quarter of a century later, under oath, he still remembered her first words to him.

“Are you the Bastard of Orléans?”

“I am,” he said, “and I rejoice at your coming.”

She wanted to know whether it was he who had advised bringing the army by this bank of the river, instead of straight at Talbot and the English. She told him what she thought of such advice: “In God’s name, the counsel of God our Lord is surer and wiser than yours.” And she told him what she had brought him.

“I bring you better help than ever came to any soldier or any city, for it is help from the King of Heaven.”

— Joan of Arc to Dunois, April 1429, as he testified in 1456 (our translation)

At that moment the help did not look like much. The wind was blowing against them, and the boats loaded with food for the city could not sail up the river. Then, Dunois testified, the wind changed at once, “as if in a moment.” The sails were raised, and the boats went up past the English bastille of Saint-Loup. Dunois judged afterward that what she did came from God rather than from man. The wind was one of his reasons.

She entered Orléans at the end of April with La Hire, and her white banner went in with her. A week earlier a gentleman writing from Lyon, the Sire de Rotslaer, had set down in a letter what she was saying she would do. She would raise the siege. She would be wounded by a shaft before Orléans, and she would not die of it. The king would be crowned at Reims that summer. The letter was registered at Brussels before any of it happened.

Waiting did not suit her. One day she came back from Dunois angry because there would be no attack that day. She went out onto one of the French earthworks and called across to the English in the next one, telling them to leave in God’s name. An Englishman known as the Bastard of Granville shouted insults back at her. Then she went to lie down.

Ah, My Banner! My Banner!

On 4 May she was resting in the house where she lodged, with her hostess beside her. D’Aulon, who later testified about these days, was dozing on a bed in her room. Suddenly she got up and woke him. Her counsel had told her to go against the English, she said, but she did not know whether it meant their forts or Fastolf, the English captain who was said to be coming. She had already given Dunois an order about him: “Bastard, Bastard, in God’s name I order you to tell me the moment you learn that Fastolf is coming.” If he failed, she had added, she would have his head cut off.

Then a noise rose from the street. She came down the stairs and turned on her page. “Ah, bloody boy, you did not tell me French blood was being spilled!” Louis passed her banner out to her through the window. She ran into the street, found a page on a horse, made him get down, and rode for the Burgundy gate without waiting for d’Aulon. At the gate she met men carrying in a wounded Frenchman. D’Aulon said she never saw French blood without her hair standing on end. That day the English bastille of Saint-Loup was taken.

She ate almost nothing in those days. Louis de Coutes remembered how little she ate: only twice a day, and on many days nothing but a morsel of bread. Over the next days the French took the fort at Saint-Jean-le-Blanc and the bastille of the Augustins, and d’Aulon remembered the noise and bravado of it. Two knights who had quarreled with arrogant words raced each other to the palisade, and a gunner named Jean le Canonnier shot dead an English giant.

The hardest fight was for the earthwork at the end of the bridge, by the towers called Les Tourelles. Against the advice of the lords, she had the Burgundy gate opened, and the assault on 7 May went on from the first hour of the day until night. Some time that day an arrow struck her between the neck and the shoulder and went in half a foot deep. It was the wound the letter from Lyon had foretold. Dunois said she did not stop and took no medicine. The old Lives add that she pulled the arrow out with her own hands.

By evening the army was exhausted, and Dunois wanted to pull back to the city. Joan asked him to wait a little. Then she mounted her horse, rode off alone into a vineyard, and prayed there for a short while. When she came back she took up her banner and planted herself on the edge of the ditch. Dunois said the English trembled at the sight of her.

D’Aulon saw what happened next from closer up. He had handed the banner to a soldier called the Basque and gone down into the ditch himself, holding his shield over his head. Joan saw her banner in the Basque’s hands and seized it by the end. “Ah, my banner! my banner!” she cried, and shook it. D’Aulon shouted up at the Basque, “Ha, Basque! Is this what you promised me?” The Basque pulled the banner free, the troops rallied, and the earthwork fell that evening.

D’Aulon dressed her wound. “We will enter the city tonight by the bridge,” she said. The English commander, Glasdale, drowned in the river. Dunois remembered that she ate four or five sops of bread in wine mixed with a great deal of water, and nothing else all day.

The next morning, 8 May, the English came out of their camps in good order. Joan rose from her bed, put on a coat of mail and nothing else, and forbade anyone to attack. Let them go, she said. The English marched away, and the siege was over. Orléans gave thanks in procession, and the city has kept 8 May in her honor through the centuries.

She rode with Dunois to see the king at Loches. She believed she had very little time.

Forward, Gentle Duke, to the Assault

D’Alençon heard her tell the king, many times, that she would last “a year and not much more.” She had four things to do in that year, she said. She must drive out the English, bring the king to be crowned at Reims, set free the captive duke of Orléans, and raise the siege of Orléans. One of them was already done.

In June the army went on the attack. At Jargeau she turned to d’Alençon. “Forward, gentle duke, to the assault!” He thought it was too early. “Do not doubt,” she said; “the hour is ready when it pleases God.” And then, “Work, and God will work.” Then she said something only a friend would say.

“Ah, gentle duke, are you afraid? Do you not know I promised your wife to bring you home safe?”

— Joan of Arc to the duke of Alençon at Jargeau, June 1429, as he testified in 1456 (our translation)

The promise was real. D’Alençon had been a prisoner of the English, his ransom had been very great, and his wife had feared for him. During the assault Joan told him to move from the place where he was standing, or that machine would kill him. He moved. Soon afterward a lord named du Lude was killed on the very spot.

Joan climbed a ladder with her banner in her hand, and a stone struck her on the helmet and threw her to the ground. She got up again at once and shouted, “Friends, friends, up! up! Our Lord has condemned the English. This hour they are ours!” Jargeau fell. More than eleven hundred English were killed in the pursuit, and Suffolk was taken prisoner. Dunois heard later that a paper had reached Suffolk in his captivity with four lines of verse on it. They foretold that a Maid would come out of the Bois-Chenu and ride on the backs of the archers.

At Beaugency the constable Richemont arrived to join the army, and Joan greeted him with honesty and grace together. “Ah, fair constable, you did not come because of me; but since you came, you are welcome.” Before Patay she told the captains, “In God’s name, we must fight them; if they were hanging from the clouds we would have them.” On 18 June the English were routed at Patay, and Talbot was taken. D’Alençon told Talbot he had not expected this that morning. Talbot answered that it was the fortune of war.

The men who rode with her that summer remembered two things that seemed hard to fit together. D’Alençon said that in war she handled a lance, drew up an army, and placed the artillery so well that the men marveled, as if she had twenty or thirty years of fighting behind her. Yet she hated to see blood spilled. Louis de Coutes once saw a French soldier strike an English prisoner on the head so hard that the man was close to death. Joan got down from her horse, had the Englishman make his confession, and held his head, and comforted him.

With her own men she was stern. She rebuked the soldiers who swore, and d’Alençon admitted that she scolded him sharply when he did. She drove away the women who followed the camp. In the field she slept in her clothes, and a woman slept near her whenever one could be found. Jean de Metz, who slept at her side on the road to Chinon, swore that he never felt any carnal desire for her. Dunois said that neither he nor the others who were with her ever had an evil thought.

On 25 June, from Gien, she sent a letter to the loyal French of Tournai. “Keep yourselves good and loyal French, I pray you,” it said, and it asked them to be ready to come to the coronation of “the gentle king Charles” at Reims. Three weeks later the king was at Reims.

A Banner by the Altar

On 17 July 1429, in the cathedral of Reims, Charles was crowned and anointed king of France. Louis de Coutes was there. Joan stood near the altar with her standard in her hand. Two years later at Rouen her judges asked her about that too. She told them why it had been carried there: “That banner had been in the hardship, so it was only reasonable that it should be in the honor.”

On the same day she sent a letter to the duke of Burgundy, whose men fought alongside the English. It begins, as some of her letters did, with “Jhesus Maria” and a cross. If he wanted a war, she told him where to find one.

“If it please you to make war, go against the Saracens. … All those who make war against the said holy kingdom of France make war against King Jesus, King of Heaven and of all the world, my rightful and sovereign Lord.”

— Joan of Arc, letter to the duke of Burgundy, Reims, 17 July 1429 (our translation)

Her fame was spreading fast. Two weeks after the coronation, on 31 July, the poet Christine de Pizan finished a poem about her. Christine began it with her own sorrow, “I, Christine, who wept eleven years in a closed abbey,” and in the middle of it she broke out, “Ah, what honor to the female sex!”

The two things Joan had promised at Poitiers were done. The siege of Orléans was raised, and the king had been crowned at Reims. In August the army rode through the country toward Paris, and when the king came to La Ferté and to Crépy, the people ran out crying “Noël!” Joan was riding between the archbishop of Reims and Dunois. “Here is a good people,” she said; “I have seen none so glad at a noble king’s coming. I wish I could be so happy as to be buried in this land when I end my days.” “Joan,” the archbishop asked, “in what place do you hope to die?” “Where it shall please God,” she answered, “for I am no surer of the time or the place than you are.” Then, Dunois remembered, she said one thing more.

“I wish it pleased God, my creator, that I might now leave, laying down my arms, and go to serve my father and mother by keeping their sheep, with my sister and my brothers, who would be so glad to see me.”

— Joan of Arc, August 1429, as Dunois testified in 1456 (our translation)

She did not go home to the sheep. She went to Paris.

A Crossbow Bolt at Paris

On 8 September 1429 the French attacked Paris at the Saint-Honoré gate. Joan took her banner and was among the first down into the ditch near the pig market. A citizen inside the walls, who hated her and kept a journal, recorded what he saw from the other side. She stood on the edge of the ditch with her banner, he wrote, and cried out to the defenders, “Surrender to us quickly, in the name of Jesus.” A man on the walls shouted back, “Paillarde! ribaude!,” words for a loose woman, and shot her through the leg. Her standard-bearer was shot between the eyes and died.

On the French side, the chronicler Perceval de Cagny tells the same moment. The bolt struck her in the thigh after sunset, and she would not leave. She kept urging the men to go up to the walls, until Gaucourt and others carried her out of the fight against her will. “By my martin,” she said, “the place would have been taken.” The next morning she was up early, asking d’Alençon to sound the trumpets, and saying she would not leave until she had the city. She did not get it. The wound, she said later, healed in five days.

The failure was remembered against her. Nearly five centuries later, when the cardinals in Rome weighed her cause, one of the objections raised was that she had attacked Paris on the feast of the birth of the Virgin Mary.

Her temper could flare. At Saint-Denis, d’Alençon said, she chased one of the women who followed the camp with her sword drawn, and broke the sword.

In the months after the coronation she spent three weeks in Bourges, in the house of Marguerite La Touroulde, who was sixty-four when she testified about it. Almost every night the two of them shared a bed. Marguerite saw her go to confession often, and she asked to go to Matins. Joan gave alms freely and loved to give to the poor. She disliked dice. When the women of the town brought their rosaries for her to touch, she laughed at them. “Touch them yourselves,” she said; “they will be as good from your touch as from mine.” Marguerite’s verdict on her guest was that she was very simple and ignorant, except in matters of war.

The war went on. At Saint-Pierre-le-Moûtier the French fell back from the wall. D’Aulon had been wounded in the heel and was on crutches, and he saw Joan standing almost alone by the wall. She took off her helmet and told him she was not alone: she had fifty thousand of her people with her. D’Aulon could see four or five men. Then she shouted, “Bring brushwood and hurdles, everyone, to make the bridge!” The town fell soon after.

At the end of December the king ennobled her and her family, who took the name Du Lis. In March 1430, from Sully, she sent letters to the people of Reims, urging them always to be good and loyal. Of the enemy, she promised, “I will make them put on their spurs so fast they will not know how to catch them.” She had more good news to tell them, she wrote, but she was afraid the letters would be taken on the road.

That spring, at Lagny, a baby three days old was brought before the statue of the Virgin. The child had died without baptism, and he was, she told her judges later, as black as her coat. The young girls of the town were praying before the statue, and Joan went and prayed with them. At last life returned to the child, who yawned three times, and the color began to come back. He was baptized, and soon afterward he died and was buried in consecrated ground. Asked whether the village said it was done through her prayer, she answered only, “I did not enquire about it.” The bull of 1920 says she raised the child to life so that he could be baptized.

In April she was on the walls of Melun. There her voices told her that she would be taken prisoner before the feast of Saint John.

Taken in the Meadow at Compiègne

The town of Compiègne was holding out against the Burgundians. Late in May, about midnight, Joan rode out of Crépy with three or four hundred fighters. “By my martin, we are enough,” she said. “I will go and see my good friends of Compiègne.” They came into the town at sunrise.

That day there was a skirmish in the meadow outside the walls. The Burgundians had laid an ambush from the hill of Clairoy, and when it broke, the captain of Compiègne raised the bridge and closed the gate. Joan was left outside, and five or six men laid hands on her at once. They called on her to give them her word as their prisoner. “I have sworn and given my faith to another than you,” she said, “and I will keep my oath to him.”

It was 23 May 1430, a year and a few weeks after she had ridden into Orléans. The old French Lives did not forget what she had told the king: a year, and not much more.

She belonged now to Jean de Luxembourg. A steward of her captors told her that Compiègne would fall, and she told him it would not. Her voices, the bull of her canonization says, had promised that the town would be free before Martinmas, and it was.

She did not take captivity quietly. In the tower of Beaurevoir she heard two pieces of news: the people of Compiègne were to be put to fire and sword, and she herself had been sold to the English. “I had rather die than be in the hands of my enemies, the English,” she said later, and about Compiègne: “I would rather have died than live after such a destruction of good people.” Her saints told her not to jump. She jumped anyway.

She survived the fall. For days afterward she could neither eat nor drink. Saint Catherine came to her and told her to confess and to ask God’s pardon, and she did. Later, before her judges, she named the leap from Beaurevoir as the worst wrong she had done, and said she had asked pardon of God for it.

In the autumn the English paid ten thousand gold francs for her. That winter she was brought to Rouen, a city the English held, to be tried before a Church court under Cauchon, bishop of Beauvais.

Hanged for Speaking the Truth

Cauchon could have put her in a Church prison. Instead he kept her in a secular prison, guarded by the English, and two friars who served at the trial said so plainly years later. She was kept in irons. On 21 February 1431 she stood before the court for the first public hearing, in the chapel of the castle of Rouen. Forty-two assessors sat on the benches, and she complained to them that she was fastened with chains and fetters of iron.

They asked her to say the Our Father. She refused, unless the bishop would hear her confession. They told her to swear to tell the truth about everything. She would swear to tell the truth about the case, she said, “as much as I would to the Pope of Rome,” but not about everything. On the second day they tried to make her swear again, and she lost patience. “I made oath to you yesterday,” she said. “You burden me over-much!” She warned them plainly what she would and would not do.

“You may well ask me some things on which I shall tell you the truth and some on which I shall not tell it you. If you were well informed about me, you would wish to have me out of your hands. I have done nothing except by revelation.”

— Joan of Arc to her judges, Rouen, February 1431

They reminded her that she had tried to escape. “Is not this lawful for all prisoners?” she asked. “It is true I wished to escape; and so I wish still.” Her memory was better than the clerks’. One day a notary claimed she had not answered a question put to her eight days before. The record was read aloud, and she was right. The next time he made a mistake, she told him, she would pull his ears.

For weeks they came at her from every side. By one count she was questioned seventeen times between 21 February and 17 March. They asked about her voices and her saints, her visions and her men’s clothes. “What concerns this dress is a small thing — less than nothing,” she said. They asked about the Ladies’ Tree. Her brother, she said, had once told her that people in the neighborhood claimed, “Jeannette received her mission at the Fairies’ Tree.” “It is not the case,” she told them, “and I told him the contrary.” Since she had learned that she must go into France, she said, she had given herself up “as little as possible to these games.” They asked whether Saint Catherine and Saint Margaret hated the English. “They love what God loves: they hate what God hates.” She told them that she had never cursed any of the saints, “and it is not my habit to swear.”

On 1 March she told them that within seven years the English would lose a greater prize than Orléans.

Through it all they kept returning to the king’s sign at Chinon. At last, in the private hearings of March, she gave them an answer in the form of an allegory. An angel, she said, had brought the king a crown. The angel went first into the king’s chamber, and then she followed and said, “Sire, there is your sign; take it.” She had greeted the angel, she said, knelt down, and taken off her cap. The crown “was beautiful, honourable, and most credible; the best and richest in the world.” It smelled good, she told them, “and will smell good always, if it be well guarded, as it should be.” Then she told them what sign they needed. “No man in the world could devise so rich a thing as this sign; but the sign that you need is that God may deliver me from your hands; that is the most sure sign He could send you.”

The angel had not stayed, and she spoke of his leaving the way she had once spoken of her saints.

“He left me in that little Chapel. I was much vexed at his going; I wept; willingly would I have gone with him — that is to say, my soul.”

— Joan of Arc, at Rouen, March 1431

The great question of the trial was whether she would submit her voices to the judgment of the Church. A friar named Isambart de la Pierre tried to help her. When they pressed her to submit, she asked to be taken to the Pope. Isambart told her she might submit to the council of Basel. She asked what a council was. When he explained that it would include as many churchmen from her side as from the English side, she cried out that she would submit to it. Cauchon shouted at Isambart, “Hold your tongue in the devil’s name!” and told the notary not to write her answer down. Afterward some of the English threatened to throw Isambart into the Seine.

The questions, one of the friars said later, were too hard for a poor, simple woman who barely knew her Our Father and Hail Mary. Yet in all those weeks she did not give way. That came at the end of May, in the cemetery of Saint-Ouen, when they brought her out to a scaffold.

For Fear of the Fire

On 24 May 1431, on the scaffold at Saint-Ouen, her courage failed her for once, and she signed. The paper she signed was short, about eight lines by the account of the usher Massieu, and five witnesses at the later inquiry agreed that it was only a few lines long. The text entered in the trial record was much longer. According to the bull of 1920, she said afterward that she had made the abjuration out of force and fear, without understanding it, and that her voices rebuked her for giving in. She was taken back to her prison.

Within days she was wearing men’s clothes again. She said publicly that she had put them back on because the English had done her wrong and violence in her prison. Isambart saw her weeping, her face wet with tears and disfigured by them. She told Ladvenu, another friar, that she had been beaten and ill-used in her cell, and that an English lord had assaulted her.

On 28 May the judges questioned her about it, and she told them why.

“I would rather die than be in irons! but if I am allowed to go to Mass, and am taken out of irons and put into a gracious prison … I will be good, and do as the Church wills.”

— Joan of Arc to her judges, Rouen, 28 May 1431

Then she went further, and she knew where it would lead. Her voices had spoken to her since Saint-Ouen, she said, and they had told her of the great pity of the treason she had agreed to. “I have damned myself to save my life!” she told them. “All I said and revoked, I said for fear of the fire.” Against one of her answers that day, that she had never meant to deny her visions, the clerk wrote two Latin words in the margin: Responsio mortifera, “the deadly answer.”

To the churchmen she said, as Isambart remembered it, “If you, my lords of the Church, had led me and kept me in your prisons, perhaps it would not have gone so with me.”

As Cauchon left the prison, he spoke to Warwick and the English with him, and two of the friars reported his words. “Farowelle, farowelle,” he said, using their own word, “it is done; be of good cheer.”

On 29 May the judges declared her relapsed. On the morning of 30 May the friar Ladvenu went to her to tell her how she would die.

Where Shall I Be This Evening?

What we know of that last day comes from the men who were there, telling it under oath eighteen to twenty-five years later. Their memories agree on the shape of it.

When Ladvenu told her that she would be burned, she cried out and pulled at her hair. She would rather have her head cut off seven times, she said, than be burned. Toutmouillé, who was there, heard what she said to Cauchon: “Bishop, I die through you.” The friars remembered the rest of it. “Alas! I die through you, for had you given me over to be kept in the prisons of the Church, I should not have been here!”

The fear did not last. A churchman named Pierre Maurice came to see her that morning, and a witness at the later inquiry repeated what Maurice had told him. She had asked him, “Maitre Pierre, where shall I be this evening?” He asked whether she had good hope in the Lord. She said she had, and that God willing she would be in Paradise.

About nine o’clock they brought her to the Old Market of Rouen. Nicolas Midi preached a sermon. The witnesses agreed that the secular judges pronounced no sentence on her. Two sergeants simply made her step down and delivered her to the executioner. The pyre had been built high on a scaffold of plaster. The executioner said afterward that he had never feared an execution so much, for her fame and for the cruel way she had been tied up there.

She asked for a cross. An English soldier broke a stick and made a little one, and she kissed it and put it in her bosom. Then she asked for the cross from the nearby church, and it was brought and held up before her eyes, and it stayed there until the end. All who watched her, Isambart said, wept hot tears, and among them were the English cardinal and other Englishmen.

In the flames she called on the saints, and she called on Jesus. One witness heard her cry the name more than six times. At the very last, Isambart said, she bowed her head and said it once more.

The witnesses remembered what came after. An English soldier who hated her, and had sworn to bring a bundle of wood for her fire, was stunned when he heard her cry “Jesus.” His friends led him away to a tavern, and that afternoon he confessed to a Dominican friar that he had gravely erred. He had seen her spirit leave her, Isambart reported, like a white dove, going away from France. The executioner came to the friars in terror at what he had done. He was afraid he was damned. He told them that with all his oil, sulphur, and charcoal, he could not burn her heart and entrails to ashes, and he called it a most evident miracle.

On the orders of the English, her ashes and her heart were thrown into the Seine, so that no one could keep them as relics. Less than two years before, riding toward Paris among a glad people, she had wished she might be buried in that land. She had no grave at all.

In Domrémy her mother was still alive.

Her Mother at Notre-Dame

Within the seven years she had named to her judges, the English lost Paris. In 1449 the French took back Rouen itself. Charles VII ordered an inquiry into the trial, and witnesses, among them Isambart and Ladvenu, gave sworn testimony. In 1452 a second inquiry was held under Cardinal d’Estouteville, who also granted an indulgence to the people who kept the 8 May procession at Orléans.

The decisive step came from her family. Her mother, Isabelle Romée, and two of Joan’s brothers petitioned the Pope. On 11 June 1455 Calixtus III named three judges to hear the case: the archbishop of Reims and the bishops of Paris and Coutances. On 7 November 1455 the process opened in Notre-Dame in Paris, and Isabelle came forward and spoke for her daughter.

Then the witnesses came. In Domrémy, Hauviette, now about forty-five, told how they had slept in the same house and how she had wept when Joan left without saying goodbye. Perrin Drapier, about sixty, told about the bells and the wool. Dunois, about fifty-one, told about the wind on the Loire and the vineyard by the ditch. D’Alençon, about fifty, told about the quail hunt and the promise to his wife. D’Aulon testified at Lyon, and Louis de Coutes, now forty-two, remembered how she had scolded him for not telling her that French blood was being spilled. Marguerite La Touroulde, sixty-four, told about the rosaries. The friars told about the stake.

On 7 July 1456 the judges gave their sentence. They annulled the trial of 1431, the abjuration, and both judgments against her, and they ordered a cross raised at the place in Rouen where she had died.

Not long after, a Pope added his own words. Pius II wrote of her, and his sentence was quoted in the bull that made her a saint: Sic Ioanna obiit mirabilis et stupenda virgo, “So died Joan, a marvellous and astonishing maid.”

Orléans kept her memory. Every 8 May the bishop, the cathedral chapter, the clergy of twelve parishes, the mayor, and the commanders of the army went in procession, with a Mass and a sermon in her honor. In 1793, during the Revolution, the festival was stopped, the relics, sword, and banner kept in her memory were destroyed, and her statue was melted down into a cannon. Ten years later Napoleon allowed the festival to return.

In the nineteenth century people began to ask for more. Félix Dupanloup, bishop of Orléans, preached in her honor in 1849 and again in 1869, and in 1869 he and other bishops signed a petition to Pope Pius IX on her behalf.

Sixty Thousand in Saint Peter’s

On 27 January 1894 Pope Leo XIII formally introduced the cause of Joan of Arc. It did not go smoothly. In 1902 a consistory in Rome set out the objections against her: the attack on Paris on the Virgin’s birthday, her capture, her attempts to escape, her abjuration, and doubts about her purity. The cause went on. On the feast of the Epiphany, 6 January 1904, Pius X declared that she had lived the virtues to a heroic degree.

Among those who had prayed for her beatification was Thérèse of Lisieux, who had written a great part of her verses about Joan. At the end of 1908 Pius X approved three cures as miracles attributed to Joan’s prayers, all of them of nuns. Sister Thérèse of Saint Augustine was healed at once of a chronic ulceration of the stomach. Sister Julie Gauthier of Saint Norbert was healed of a painful ulcer of the left breast. Sister Jeanne Marie Sagnier was healed of a chronic tuberculous disease of the bone. On 18 April 1909 Joan was beatified in Saint Peter’s.

For her canonization the Church examined two more cures. Maria Antonia Mirandelle was healed of a perforating disease in the sole of her foot. Thérèse Belin was healed of tuberculosis of the lungs and the abdomen, and of damage to a valve of her heart. On 6 April 1918 the decree approving the two miracles was issued.

On 16 May 1920 Pope Benedict XV declared Joan of Arc a saint in the basilica of Saint Peter, with the bull Divina disponente. More than sixty thousand people were there, and among them were a hundred and forty descendants of her family. She was canonized as a virgin, and her feast was fixed on 30 May, the day of her death. Two years later, on 2 March 1922, Pius XI named her a secondary patron of France.

Of her body, no relic is known. In 1867 a jar was found in the attic of a Paris pharmacy, labeled as remains found under her pyre. It held a blackened human rib, bits of charred wood, a scrap of linen, and the thigh bone of a cat. It was kept for years at Chinon. In 2007 a team of scientists led by Philippe Charlier examined it and concluded that the contents came from an Egyptian mummy and had not been burned. The Diocese of Tours, which held the jar, said that the remains had never been considered relics by the Church and had never been venerated. The English had done their work in 1431. Joan has no tomb.

What remains is what she said, under oath, before the court that condemned her. Early in her trial they asked whether she was in the grace of God, and she left the answer to God. At the end she broke once under the fear of the fire, and then, knowing the cost, she took it back rather than deny her voices. The men who stood by her on the last morning did not all remember her words the same way. Pierre Maurice and Toutmouillé recalled that when churchmen told her that her voices had deceived her, since they had promised her deliverance, she answered, “they have deceived me.” Ladvenu, who stayed with her until her last breath, said that “up to the end of her life she maintained and asserted that her Voices came from God.”

The bull that made her a saint says that in her last hour she understood what her voices had foretold. Take everything willingly, they had said, and do not be anxious or troubled about your martyrdom, for you will enter the kingdom of Paradise. It was the one reward she had ever asked of them.

“There is not a day when I do not hear this Voice; and I have much need of it. But never have I asked of it any recompense but the salvation of my soul.”

— Joan of Arc, to her judges at Rouen, February 1431

A Note on the Sources

Almost everything we know about Joan comes from two sets of legal records. The first is the record of her trial in 1431, written down by her judges’ notaries; her answers are quoted here in T. Douglas Murray’s English translation of 1902. The second is the sworn testimony gathered between 1450 and 1456 for the inquiry that overturned the verdict, from people who had known her; nearly all the words of her last day come from these depositions. The sources disagree on some dates. Her birth is given as 6 January 1412 by the early writer Boulainvilliers and as 6 February 1412 in the bull of 1920. This life says only that she was about nineteen in 1431, as she said herself. Dunois’s deposition, as printed, dates her wound at Orléans to “the twenty-seventh of May.” That cannot be right, since the siege ended on 8 May; Murray’s translation corrects it to 7 May in a note, as do the Catholic Encyclopedia and Guérin. Her capture is dated 23 May in some sources and 24 May in others, and this life follows the earlier ones. The Catholic Encyclopedia dates the loss of Paris, which she foretold, to 12 November 1437 and Murray’s note to 1436; either date falls within the seven years she named. Witnesses disagree about her last morning: some said that she admitted her voices had deceived her, others that she held to them to the end, and this life gives both. Joan’s own list of godparents and the list in her neighbors’ depositions differ, so this life names none. In 2007 Nature reported that the Church had recognized the jar of supposed relics, and the Diocese of Tours said the opposite; this life follows the diocese. The chronicle of Perceval de Cagny is used here for its scenes, not for its dates, which are sometimes wrong. A letter to the Hussites under her name survives only in German and was composed by a clerk, so it is not quoted. The speeches given to her by the nineteenth-century French Lives are marked as theirs.

How This Life Was Researched

This life draws on the twelve sources listed below, in Latin, French, and English. The largest is Jules Quicherat’s five-volume edition of the trial of 1431 and the inquiry of 1450–1456. It prints the sworn depositions of her childhood neighbors in Domrémy, her commanders, her page, her hostess in Bourges, and the friars who stood by her at the stake. It also prints her own surviving letters, two fifteenth-century chronicles, and Christine de Pizan’s poem of 1429. The others include the papal bull of her canonization and, for the so-called Chinon relics, press reports and the abstract of a forensic study published in 2010. The researchers also flagged more than twenty details that they did not expect most popular accounts to include. Except where Murray’s 1902 translation is quoted, the translations from Latin and French are our own.
