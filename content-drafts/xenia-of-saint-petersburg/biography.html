Followed Into the Night Fields

Every night, the old woman who lived on the streets of the Petersburg Side went away, and nobody could say where. By day she was everywhere, at the market stalls and in the houses of people who knew her. After dark she was gone until morning. In the capital of the Russian Empire, a woman who vanished every night was a question, and questions were the business of the police. So they watched her, and at last they followed her out into the open fields.

They found her on her knees. She was bowing to the ground, first toward one quarter of the earth, then toward the next, until she had bowed to all four, and then she began again. She went on like that through the whole night, alone, in an empty field. The policemen watched for a while, and then they went away satisfied. The old woman was praying.

This story was printed in 1847, in the gazette of the Saint Petersburg city police, at a time when she had been dead for some forty years. Its writer still remembered how she looked. She had worn a man's linen, a man's waistcoat, and an officer's kaftan, and she had gone on wearing them until they rotted on her body. And she had answered to a man's name. Call out "Andrei Fedorovich!" on the Petersburg Side, and the old woman in the rotting coat would answer.

Andrei Fedorovich Petrov had been her husband, a singer in the court choir who held the rank of colonel. He had died suddenly, when she was twenty-six. Her own name was Xenia, or Aksinya Grigorievna in the plain Russian of her neighbours, and from the day of his funeral she refused to answer to it.

The Russian Orthodox Church venerates her today as Saint Xenia of Petersburg, the blessed Xenia, a fool for Christ. She left no letters and no teaching. No document from her lifetime is known to survive, and the stories about her were first printed some forty years after her death. What remains is what Petersburg remembered: a handful of scenes, a handful of sayings, and a grave to which people have been coming in crowds for two hundred years.

Those memories raise a question that none of them answers outright. Why would a young widow put on her dead husband's clothes, give away her house, and walk the streets under his name for forty-five years? And how did the woman whose own relatives tried to have her placed under guardianship become the one Petersburg runs to, still, when it needs a roof, a job, a husband, or a child? The answer begins with the little that is known about the woman she was before, and with the day on which, as she put it, that woman died.

The Wife of a Court Singer

Of Xenia's early life almost nothing is known. Her father was called Grigory, and her mother's name is lost. Even the year of her birth is a guess, somewhere between about 1719 and 1731. When a Petersburg priest, Dmitry Bulgakovsky, gathered her stories into a small book in 1891, he had to admit that "what station she came from is unknown." Later writers supposed that she came from a good family, because of the man she married. That is a deduction from the marriage, and nothing more.

The man was Andrei Fedorovich Petrov, who sang in the choir of the imperial court and held a colonel's rank. A modern Life adds a few details whose source it does not give. The couple, it says, had been married about three and a half years and had no children, and the house they lived in had been bought with her dowry. The same Life supposes that he died in the reign of Empress Elizabeth.

Legend later gave his death a grander setting. In one version he fell dead at a ball, and a young guardsman named Ivan Ushakov left the world too, and became the monk Feodor at the Alexander Nevsky Lavra. Even the modern Life that tells this story calls it "far from the truth," because the dates will not fit. At most, it suggests, the monk may have been the couple's confessor.

What Bulgakovsky's booklet does say is the bare fact: Xenia was twenty-six, and her husband died suddenly. The modern Life adds one more detail, and it is a heavy one for any Orthodox Christian. Andrei Fedorovich died without confession and without communion. He had no time to make his peace with God.

What his widow did at the funeral is the first story Petersburg tells about her.

A Funeral in His Coat

On the day of the funeral, as the modern Life tells it, the young widow came dressed in her husband's uniform: his waistcoat, his kaftan, his trousers, and his cap. In his clothes she walked behind his coffin. When the mourners spoke to her, she told them that Xenia had died. Andrei Fedorovich, she said, had not died at all. He lived on in her.

Bulgakovsky's booklet of 1891, the older account, tells it more simply and more strangely. From then on, she said, she was not Aksinya Grigorievna; she was Andrei Fedorovich. She held to it for the rest of her life. When people pressed her own name on her, she pushed it away as though they were speaking of someone else, someone they ought to leave in peace. "Leave Xenia alone!" she said in one telling. "What harm did she do you?" The booklet keeps an older and harsher form of the same thought, which ends in a prayer: "Leave the dead woman alone. What did she do to you? Lord forgive me!"

The police gazette of 1847, which is older still, remembered the clothes. She wore her husband's linen, his waistcoat, and his kaftan, and she went on wearing them until they rotted on her body.

She never explained herself, at least in any words that someone remembered. The facts simply stand side by side. A husband was taken without warning and, in the later telling, without the last sacraments. From that day, his wife lived under his name, wore his clothes, and called the woman she had been dead. A grieving widow might keep a husband's coat for years. Xenia put it on and went out in it, and she let the whole city see.

Her family soon found that the coat was only the beginning.

Examined and Found Sane

She began to give away everything she owned. The house went to Paraskeva Antonova, a friend who had been living there as a lodger, the widow of a non-commissioned officer. Her other goods went the same way. Her relatives watched the property disappear and did what relatives do: they petitioned her late husband's superiors to take her affairs under guardianship before there was nothing left.

The officers sent for the young widow and talked with her. Whatever they had expected, they found a woman of sound mind. They declared her sane, and because she was sane, what she owned was hers to give away as she chose. The petition failed. The only official judgment on Xenia's state of mind that anyone recorded found that she knew perfectly well what she was doing.

That verdict mattered more than it might seem. A fool for Christ, in the Russian phrase, is someone who becomes foolish in the world's eyes for God's sake. Peter the Great had no patience with such people. His Spiritual Regulation, the charter by which the Church was governed in Xenia's day, treated fools as frauds or as people possessed. For as long as that order lasted, no fool for Christ could be glorified as a saint. Xenia chose this life in a city and a century that officially distrusted it.

Her neighbours on the Petersburg Side, who saw her every day, did not need a rule book. As the police gazette reported decades later, they had their own way of putting it.

“Who does not belong to the world belongs to God.”

— Her neighbours, as reported in the gazette of the Saint Petersburg city police, 1847

With the house gone and the officers' verdict behind her, Xenia went out into the streets of Petersburg. She would live on them for forty-five years.

The Tsar on a Horse

When the officer's kaftan had rotted away at last, Petersburg remembered her in two colours: a red jacket with a green skirt, or a green jacket with a red skirt, all year round. Modern writers have wondered whether the colours came from a regiment's uniform, but that is only a guess. The rest of her outfit, in the stories and on the icons, was a stick in her hand.

People pressed money on her, and she would not take it. The one exception was an old copper kopeck of a particular kind, the coin she called the tsar on a horse. "Give me the tsar on a horse," she would say. And as soon as she had it, she gave it away.

The traders of the Sytny market learned to want her custom. When she passed, sellers opened their baskets and trays to her, hoping she would take something, because, they said, trade went well afterward. Mothers were glad when she stopped to rock a child. In the city's mind she had already become a kind of blessing on two legs, a widow in rags whose touch brought luck. The fool in the dead man's coat had become a neighbour.

She had her own habits, and some of them were odd. At the public bathhouse she went in wearing her shirt, climbed onto the shelf, wet her head, and walked out again in the same wet shirt. In all the stories she is gentle. People could remember only one time they had seen her angry. Some boys had been throwing mud at her, and the old woman went after them with her stick, her grey hair loose. The neighbourhood, for its part, saw to it that the boys were punished.

Gentle did not mean polite. One of the women who knew her was Evdokia Gaidukova, who lived until 1827 and died at ninety-one. Evdokia's husband Xenia could not abide, and she said so.

“Oh, that mare's head of his!”

— Xenia, of Evdokia Gaidukova's husband, as remembered by Evdokia's niece and printed by D. G. Bulgakovsky, 1891

We know this because Evdokia's niece, M. I. Belyaeva, remembered her aunt's stories, and at the age of eighty-three she told them to Bulgakovsky, who printed them in 1891. That is how short the chain of memory runs: from Xenia to a woman who knew her, from that woman to her niece, and from the niece to the priest. Through that chain we hear Xenia complain about a neighbour's husband, which is as close as the record ever comes to the sound of her voice on an ordinary day.

By day, then, the city knew where to find her. By night she disappeared. That, in the end, is what brought the police.

Bricks on the Scaffold

The policemen who followed her into the fields found nothing to report except a woman praying. She knelt in the open and bowed to the four quarters of the earth, again and again, all night long. No one saw her but the men who had come to catch her at something. They went away satisfied. Xenia, presumably, went on bowing.

Whatever else her life on the streets was, it was a life of prayer, and most of that prayer was hidden. The few glimpses that survive come from the night. The best known of them comes from the Smolensky cemetery.

Late in the eighteenth century the parish there was building a stone church to replace its wooden one, which had decayed. The builders began to notice something in the mornings. Bricks had been carried up onto the scaffolding during the night. Eventually somebody saw who it was. The widow in the red and green, who would take nothing from anyone, had been hauling bricks up the scaffold in the dark.

Like nearly everything about her, this story comes to us through tradition. It was printed a century after the church was built, in the booklet of 1891. But it fits everything else that was remembered: the night, the work, the refusal to be seen. It also fits the place. The cemetery where she carried those bricks is the one where she would be buried.

By then the city had noticed something else about Andrei Fedorovich. Things she said had a way of coming true.

All Russia Will Bake Pancakes

On 24 December 1761, the day before Christmas, Xenia was heard shouting. In Russia, pancakes were the food of memorial meals, baked to remember the dead.

“Bake pancakes, bake pancakes, all Russia will bake pancakes.”

— Xenia, 24 December 1761, as told by D. G. Bulgakovsky, 1891

The next day, 25 December, Empress Elizabeth died.

The second story is darker. For three weeks, Xenia wept every day. People passing her in the street stopped and asked her what was wrong, as they would ask a neighbour: "Why are you crying, Andrei Fedorovich? Did someone hurt you?" She answered with words about blood.

“There the rivers are full of blood, there the canals are bloody, there is blood, blood, blood.”

— Xenia, three weeks before the death of Ivan Antonovich, as told by D. G. Bulgakovsky, 1891

Three weeks later Ivan Antonovich died at Schlüsselburg, in 1764.

These two stories were printed in 1891, more than a century after the events. They are what the city told about her, not what anyone wrote down at the time, and that is how they are given here. What is striking is the manner they remember. She does not announce, explain, or take credit. She cries out a strange sentence in the street, or weeps for three weeks, and leaves the meaning for others to find.

The empress and Schlüsselburg were far away from the Petersburg Side. Most of what Xenia foretold happened much closer to home, in the houses of people who had let her in.

The Nettle Will Soon Wither

The merchant's wife Krapivina had a name that comes from the Russian word for nettle. Xenia said to her, in a sentence the police gazette of 1847 still remembered:

“The nettle is green, but it will soon wither.”

— Xenia, to the merchant's wife Krapivina, from the Saint Petersburg police gazette, 1847, as quoted by D. G. Bulgakovsky

Krapivina died soon after.

Not every saying was so grim. At one house, where she was told there was no fish pie, Xenia knew better: "No, you did bake one, and you do not want to give it to me." In the house of the merchant Razzhivin she remarked of a mirror, "This mirror is fine, but there is nothing to look at in it," and the mirror fell and broke. The humour is hers, and so is the sharpness. She did not flatter the people who fed her.

Sometimes the news was good. Xenia once came upon a young woman, still unmarried, sitting over a cup of coffee, and spoke to her as though she were already a wife.

“You sit drinking coffee, and your husband is burying his wife in Okhta.”

— Xenia, to a young woman, as told by D. G. Bulgakovsky, 1891

At that moment a widower was burying his wife at Okhta. In time, he married the young woman.

Evdokia Gaidukova, whose husband had a mare's head, had her own story. Xenia once gave her a copper five-kopeck coin and told her that the fire would go out. Later Evdokia's house caught fire, and by the time she reached it, the flames had been put out. Her niece told this story to Bulgakovsky some sixty years after Evdokia's death, and it comes closer than most to an eyewitness.

The last of these stories comes only from modern retellings, and it closes a loop that opened on the day Xenia gave her house away. She came to Paraskeva Antonova, the friend who now owned it, and found her mending stockings. "You sit here mending stockings," she said, "and you do not know that God has sent you a son!" Paraskeva went out and came upon a woman who had been killed by a carriage, and the newborn boy she had left behind. Paraskeva took the child and raised him. The widow who had received Xenia's house received a son as well.

Xenia herself was growing old. And now the merchants of Petersburg began to offer her the one thing she had given away first: a home.

No Worse Than a Bird

In her last years, families from the rich merchant houses of the city invited her to come and live with them. She would not go. "Why should I suffer when there is no need?" she asked them, as though the warm room were the hardship and the street the comfort. When someone offered her a sheepskin coat, she turned that down too: "Give it to the one who is unhappy without it; it will bring him joy." These sayings come from a modern Life. So does the one that best sums up the life she chose, an old woman's joke built on the Gospel: "The Lord feeds the birds of heaven, and I am no worse than a bird."

When and how she died, nobody wrote down. No record of her death or her burial survives. A later tradition says that the Mother of God came to her in a dream and told her that her time had come. The most likely date is around 1800, perhaps a year or two later. M. I. Belyaeva, speaking in 1891, put it at about ninety years before, and the police gazette of 1847 at forty years or more. She was buried in the Smolensky cemetery, where she had carried the bricks.

Her gravestone gave her age as seventy-one and said that she had wandered for forty-five years. The booklet of 1891 says she was twenty-six when her husband died, and the numbers agree: twenty-six years as Xenia, and forty-five as Andrei Fedorovich. The stone ended with a request, written as though she were speaking.

“Whoever knew me, let him remember my soul for the saving of his own.”

— Inscription on Xenia's gravestone, as given by D. G. Bulgakovsky, 1891

It was a modest thing to ask. Petersburg took her at her word.

Earth Carried From the Grave

By about 1820, a modern Life says, people were coming to her grave in crowds. They came to pray, to have requiems served, and to ask for help, and they took something home with them: a little earth from the mound over her grave. They took so much that the mound had to be built up again several times. When a stone slab was laid over it, they chipped pieces off the slab. They left money on the grave, until at last a box was set out to collect it.

Her name, or rather his, reached print in 1845, in a book of city sketches by Grebenka called The Physiology of Petersburg. It mentions her in passing, as the old woman who answered "Ась!", the Russian for "Eh?", when people called her Andrei Fedorovich. Two years later came the police gazette, with its night fields and its rotting kaftan. Then, in 1891, Bulgakovsky published his booklet, with the Gaidukov family's memories and a long list of favours that people said they had received at the grave. By 1904 it had gone through fourteen editions.

One of those favours begins on a bridge. A colonel's wife was weeping there when a woman in plain dress stopped her and told her to have a requiem served at Xenia's grave. She did as she was told, and her two sons were taken into the cadet corps. Another is about a bride whose bridegroom, a man who passed for a colonel, was exposed the day before the wedding as an escaped convict who had killed a real colonel and his coachman.

The rest are smaller and more practical, and that is their charm. A doctor named Bulakh had a requiem served at the grave and was given a post in Rzhev. A man known to us only as I. I. Is-ov received four offers of work on the same day. A captain's son was healed with earth from the grave stirred into water. An inspector at the mint, P. D. A-ev, was rubbed with the earth and was well the next day. A baby girl was named Xenia in thanksgiving. Nobody in these stories asks Xenia for anything grand. They ask for a school place, a job, a husband, a child's health.

Her help, in these stories, could reach well beyond the city. In 1873 the journal Russkaya Starina printed the account of a landowner in the Pskov province who dreamed that Xenia was sprinkling his house. A fire broke out, and a hay barn twenty sazhens away burned, but the house was saved. A later urban legend, found in a modern Life, says that an old woman was seen on the Petersburg Side after Xenia's death, warning people of danger. That one is a legend, and it is told as one. Still, it shows where Petersburg expected to meet her: on the same streets she had walked.

The grave got its first small chapel in the third quarter of the nineteenth century. In 1902 the architect Alexander Vseslavin built the larger stone chapel that stands over it now. The next year, a House of Industry, where the poor could be given work, was founded at the Smolensky church in her memory. Fifteen years later came the Revolution.

A Chapel Closed, Then Returned

Under Soviet rule the chapel was closed in 1940, with a decision to demolish it within a month. The demolition was never carried out, and people went on coming to the grave. The chapel opened again in 1946 or 1947, after the war, and was closed once more around 1960; one account gives the date as 18 November 1962. In 1983 it was returned to the Church. On 10 August 1987, Metropolitan Alexy (Ridiger) consecrated it again.

By then Xenia had already been glorified, far from Petersburg. The Russian Orthodox Church Outside of Russia had been gathering testimonies about her since 1971. On 24 September 1978, which was 11 September in the Church's own calendar, its bishops glorified her as a saint in the Synodal Cathedral of the Sign in New York. Their act named her "the homeless wanderer of Peter's city."

Ten years later the Church in her own country did the same. A Local Council of the Russian Orthodox Church met at the Trinity-Sergius Lavra under Patriarch Pimen from 6 to 9 June 1988, and on 6 June it numbered her among the saints. It acted, its document says, in full conviction of the truth and reliability of her miracles, and it described her in one long phrase.

“…who was a fool for Christ, was revered in her lifetime, and through the 19th and 20th centuries as a quick helper and wonderworker.”

— Act of the Local Council of the Russian Orthodox Church, June 1988

Two centuries earlier, the rules of Peter's Church had made such a glorification impossible. The widow the officers had examined and found sane was now numbered among the saints, as a fool for Christ.

Houses, Husbands, and Children

Her name day is kept on 24 January in the Church's Julian calendar, which falls on 6 February in the civil calendar. The Moscow Patriarchate also remembers her glorification on 24 May, which is 6 June. Monasteries bear her name, the first at Baran, in Belarus, in 2002, and the first in Russia at Dolbenkino, in the Oryol region, in 2010. On 6 June 2023 a monument to her was unveiled at the chapel, and the governor of Saint Petersburg called her the first woman among the saints glorified on Petersburg soil.

Her face is as uncertain as her dates. In 2017 the Hermitage showed a portrait, dated by analysis to the end of the eighteenth century or the start of the nineteenth, that was presented as possibly painted from her in life. A Hermitage curator later attributed it to another blessed woman of the city, Marfa Sonina. On the icons she stands with a stick, in a red skirt and a green jacket, with the Smolensky church and her chapel behind her.

What people bring to that chapel has changed little since the booklet of 1891. They ask for a place to live, for work, for a good marriage, for children, and for help with their studies. A modern Life calls her grave the city's "all-people's reception room." It is hard to miss the shape of that list. The woman who gave away her house is asked for houses. The widow who walked behind her husband's coffin is asked for husbands. The wife who, by the later account, never had a child is asked for children, and the wanderer who would take only a kopeck she meant to give away is asked for work.

Nobody recorded why she did what she did. What the record shows is a woman who, at twenty-six, lost her husband without warning, and then belonged to no one but God, as her neighbours said, for forty-five years. She knelt in the fields at night, she carried bricks in the dark, and she gave away every coin she took. She asked only to be remembered.

At her husband's funeral, the story goes, she told the mourners that Xenia had died, and for the rest of her life she answered to his name. The chapel over her grave bears hers. It is the name Petersburg has been calling out, at that grave, for two hundred years.

A Note on the Sources

No document from Xenia's lifetime is known, and every story about her is tradition. The oldest printed texts found are Grebenka's book of 1845 and two articles in the Saint Petersburg police gazette of 1847. Both are known here only as Bulgakovsky quotes them in his booklet of 1891, which adds the oral memories of M. I. Belyaeva. Several details come only from modern retellings that do not name their sources. Among them are the funeral in the uniform, the length of the marriage, and the dowry. So are the death without the sacraments, Paraskeva's son, and the date of about 1820 for the crowds. So do the sayings about the birds, the sheepskin, the merchants' homes, and "Leave Xenia alone." A modern Life also says she foretold that Emperor Paul would die at forty-seven; that claim was not checked and is left out of the story. Her birth is placed between about 1719 and 1731 and her death between about 1800 and 1803, with one scholarly view giving 1802. The stone Smolensky church is dated 1786 to 1790 by the Azbyka.ru calendar and the cemetery article, and Bulgakovsky notes its centenary in 1890. A memorial plate quoted by Russian Wikipedia gives 1794 to 1796 instead. The second closing of the chapel is given as 1960 in one article and 18 November 1962 in another, and the wording of the gravestone differs slightly between Bulgakovsky and Russian Wikipedia.

How This Life Was Researched

This life draws on eight texts in Russian and English. The only public-domain source is the booklet of the priest D. G. Bulgakovsky, printed in Saint Petersburg in 1891, which quotes the two oldest printed texts about Xenia, from 1845 and 1847, and records the oral testimony of an eighty-three-year-old woman whose aunt had known her. The other seven are modern and were used for facts only. Chief among them is a Life compiled by Anna Markova in 2010, which prints the Church acts of 1978 and 1988. The rest are the Azbyka.ru calendar, four Wikipedia articles (three in Russian, one in English), and an English article on the disputed portrait. Several sources were not read: the article in the Orthodox Encyclopedia, A. Rakhmanin's Life of 1909, Saitov's Petersburg Necropolis, the 1873 article in Russkaya Starina, the original texts of 1845 and 1847, and most of the miracle sections of the 2010 Life. All translations from Russian are our own.
