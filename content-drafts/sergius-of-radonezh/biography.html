A Prophet or an Orphan?

The plowman had come a long way to see the hegumen of the monastery in the forest of Radonezh. The man they showed him was working in the garden. Through a gap in the fence the visitor saw someone in a patched robe of coarse, undyed cloth, digging the earth like any laborer, and he refused to believe that this was the hegumen. When the hegumen came out of the garden and the monks pointed him out, the visitor turned away with a scornful laugh.

“I came to see a prophet,” he said, “and you show me an orphan.”

The brothers were ready to throw him out. Their hegumen stopped them: the man had come to see him, he told them, and not them. Then Sergius did something the peasant never expected. He bowed to the ground before him, kissed him, sat him at his own right hand, and fed him. Then a prince arrived at the monastery with his armed men. The prince’s guards pushed the peasant out of the way, and the prince bowed before the man in the patched robe. Only then did the visitor understand whom he had called an orphan, and he began to tremble.

Both of them were right. The man in the garden was the one Russians would come to call the Hegumen of the Russian land. A grand prince would ask his blessing before he marched against the Horde. A metropolitan would offer him the leadership of the Russian Church, and his disciples would carry his way of life into one new monastery after another. He was also, by his own choice, as poor as any orphan in the country. He wore cloth that other monks had refused, earned his bread with an axe when the bread ran out, and grew angry when people tried to put his name on a spring.

How did a boy who could not learn to read, who wanted nothing more than a hut in the forest and God, become the father whom princes bowed to? And how did he stay the man digging in the garden to the very end? His biographer, the monk Epiphanius, who spent twenty years gathering what people remembered of him, begins the answer before Sergius was even born, on a Sunday, at the Liturgy, in the Rostov country.

The Boy Who Could Not Read

Kirill and Maria belonged to a boyar family of the Rostov principality, the landed nobility of old Russia. One Sunday, while Maria was carrying her child, she stood in church for the Divine Liturgy, and three times the child in her womb cried out loud enough for the people around her to hear. The first cry came at the reading of the Gospel, the second at the Cherubic Hymn, and the third at the moment the priest proclaims, “Holy Things for the Holy.” The women around Maria turned to her and asked the question that runs under the rest of this story: “What will this child be?”

Epiphanius knew how such a story sounds. He set it beside the signs told before the births of Jeremiah and John the Baptist, of Ephrem the Syrian and Alypius the Stylite, as if to say that God had done this before. The boy was baptized Bartholomew. Even the year of his birth is uncertain: one line of the tradition places it in 1314, and another in 1322. The Life tells a stranger detail with more confidence. As a baby, Bartholomew would not take his mother’s milk on Wednesdays and Fridays, the fast days of the Church.

At seven he was sent to learn his letters, as his brothers Stefan and Petr had been, and there the child of the three cries became an ordinary failure. They learned quickly. He learned slowly and badly. His parents scolded him, his teacher punished him, and his schoolmates mocked him, and when he was alone the boy prayed with tears: “Lord, let me learn my letters.”

One day his father sent him out to look for some foals that had strayed. Epiphanius saw a biblical echo here: young Saul also went out after his father’s lost animals, and met the prophet Samuel on the way. In a field, under an oak, Bartholomew found a monk in a black robe, a priest, standing in prayer with tears. The boy did not interrupt. He waited until the old man had finished, and then he poured out his trouble: he could not learn to read, and he asked the elder to pray for him.

The elder prayed. Then he held out a small piece of white bread, a prosphora, the blessed loaf of the Liturgy. “Open your mouth, child,” he said, “take this and eat.” Bartholomew ate it, and it tasted sweet as honey.

The boy would not let him go. He begged the elder to come home with him, and there the old man told him to read from the Psalter in the chapel. The boy who could not read opened the book and read clearly. Before he left, the elder spoke to Kirill and Maria about their son.

“Your son will be a dwelling of the Holy Trinity and will lead many after him to understand the divine commandments.”

— The elder to Kirill and Maria, in the Life of Sergius by Epiphanius the Wise, chapter 3 (our translation)

At the gate, as he was leaving, the elder vanished. Kirill and Maria decided that their guest had been an angel. Neither of them could know what “a dwelling of the Holy Trinity” meant, and the words would wait many years for their answer.

A Boyar Hanged in Rostov

The boy who had learned to read so suddenly began to fast with a seriousness that alarmed his mother. Before he was twelve, he lived on bread and water, and on Wednesdays and Fridays he ate nothing at all. Maria begged him to stop. He answered her from the Scriptures, quoting Job and the fiftieth Psalm: no one is clean before God. His mother’s reply has the ring of any parent faced with a solemn child. “You are not twelve years old,” she said, “and you speak of your sins.”

He was a serious boy, and Epiphanius does not pretend otherwise. Bartholomew did not allow himself laughter, the biographer says, and when he wanted to smile, “he did it with great modesty and restraint.”

Around him, the family’s world was coming apart. Kirill had been a rich boyar, but the tribute owed to the Horde, the raids of the Tatars, and one raid in particular, the one led by Turalyk, stripped him of his wealth. Then Rostov itself passed under the power of Moscow. The Muscovite governors, Vasily Kocheva and Mina, came to the town and oppressed it, and the elder boyar of Rostov, a man named Averky, was hanged upside down.

Kirill gathered his family and left. They moved to Radonezh, a village that Grand Prince Ivan had given to his son Andrei, and settled near the Church of the Nativity of Christ.

Bartholomew already wanted to become a monk. His parents asked him to wait, and he did. He stayed and looked after them until they themselves took the monastic habit, and a few years later both of them died. Bartholomew buried them and kept the forty days of prayers for the dead. Then he gave everything he had inherited to his younger brother Petr. His elder brother Stefan had married, lost his wife, and become a monk at the monastery of Khotkovo. It was Stefan who went with Bartholomew to look for a place in the wilderness.

Logs on Their Shoulders

The brothers walked into the forests around Radonezh looking for a deserted place. They found it deep in the thicket of the forest. There they cut down trees and carried the logs on their own shoulders. They built a hut, then a cell, and then a small wooden church.

When they came to the question of whose name the church should bear, it was Stefan who answered. It should be dedicated to the Holy Trinity, he said, because of the three cries his brother had given before he was born. The elder’s words, spoken years before in the field, were beginning to come true. The boy who was to be a dwelling of the Holy Trinity now lived beside a church of the Holy Trinity, in a forest with no road to it. Priests sent by Metropolitan Theognostus came to consecrate it, early in the reign of Grand Prince Simeon.

Stefan did not last. The wilderness was too hard for him, and after a short time he left and went to Moscow, to the Epiphany monastery, where he came to know a monk who would later become Metropolitan Alexis. For now, Bartholomew was alone in the forest.

He was tonsured a monk by a hegumen named Mitrofan, on 7 October, the day the Church remembers the martyrs Sergius and Bacchus, and he received the name of the day’s saint. Bartholomew was twenty-three years old, the Life says. As the deer longs for the stream, he had told Mitrofan, so his soul longed for the monastic life in the desert. When the new monk Sergius received Communion, the little church filled with fragrance. He stayed in the church for seven days afterward, eating nothing but the blessed bread the hegumen had given him.

After those seven days, Sergius faced his nights alone.

A Bear at the Stump

They were not quiet nights. Once, while he was singing Matins in the empty church, the wall of the church opened, and demons rushed in dressed as Lithuanians, in Lithuanian clothes and tall caps, threatening him. Sergius answered with the Psalms and the sign of the cross, and they were gone. Some days later they came at him again, this time in his hut, and again he drove them off.

The real animals of the forest were nearly as frightening. Wolves howled around his hut, and bears passed by. Epiphanius does not make his hero fearless. Sergius “feared a little, like any man,” the Life says, and he prayed.

One bear came for a different reason. It did not want to hurt him; it was hungry. Sergius began to set out a piece of bread for it on a stump or a log, and the bear came back, again and again. When there was no bread on the stump, the animal would not leave. It stood there for a long time, Epiphanius writes, “like a harsh creditor wanting his debt.” Some days Sergius had only one piece of bread for himself. On those days he broke it in two and gave the bear half. He would go hungry himself, the Life says, rather than cheat the bear. This went on for more than a year.

The Life even keeps the old word for the animal, arkuda. The young hermit was sharing his last crust with a wild beast, and before long men would come to his forest who also needed to be fed.

A Knock on the Cell Window

How long Sergius lived alone, no one knew for certain. Two years, Epiphanius guesses, perhaps more or less, and then adds, with a biographer’s honesty, “I do not know, God knows.” Then the monks began to come, first one, then two, then three. Sergius did not make the life sound attractive. He warned them that they would find hunger, thirst, and poverty here, and that they should prepare their hearts for patience and many trials, and not for food, drink, or rest. They told him they could bear it. Each newcomer built his own cell, and the hermitage began to turn into a village of huts.

The brothers wanted Sergius to be their hegumen. He refused for a long time. They kept pressing him, and at last he went with two of the elders to Bishop Athanasius, who ordained him priest and made him hegumen of the little community. For two or three years there were twelve brothers and Sergius was the thirteenth, and the number stayed at twelve, as if the forest were keeping count. One of the new arrivals was Symon, an archimandrite from Smolensk. Stefan came from Moscow, bringing his young son Ivan, whom he gave to his brother to be made a monk. Sergius tonsured the boy and named him Theodore, and the boy would later be known as Theodore of Simonov.

The hegumen led by doing the hardest work first. He was the first into church for every service, and he stood through the long prayers without leaning on the wall. A man who wanted to join wore a long black coat for a long time and did every kind of labor before he was given the monastic habit.

At night Sergius walked past the cells. Where he heard a brother praying, he blessed him. Where he heard talking or laughter, he knocked on the door or the window, and then walked on. The next morning he would call the brothers who had been talking and speak to them quietly and gently, “from afar,” as Epiphanius puts it, as if telling parables, and never in anger. Soon the monastery would face worse trouble than late-night talk.

Moldy Bread and Fresh Loaves

The monastery ran out of bread and salt, and the brothers began to go hungry. Sergius had forbidden the monks to go out begging, and he did not lift the rule. He kept the rule himself and ate nothing for three days.

On the fourth morning he picked up an axe and went to the cell of an old monk named Daniil. He offered to build a porch onto Daniil’s cell, and asked for no pay but a little old, spoiled bread. All day the hegumen of the monastery worked on the old monk’s porch. In the evening Daniil paid him with a sieve full of bread, old bread, already moldy. Sergius sat down and ate it with water.

That was too much for some of the brothers. One monk, speaking for the rest, complained that their own bread had gone moldy, and that they would leave the next day. Sergius gathered them and urged them to trust in God, and he was still speaking when someone knocked at the gate. A cart stood outside, loaded with baskets of fresh, fragrant bread, still warm. The drivers would say only that a wealthy man far away, who loved Christ, had sent it. They would not stay to eat and soon disappeared from sight, and no one ever learned his name. Hungry as the brothers were, Sergius told them to feed the men who had carried the bread first.

He dressed the same way he ate. His robe was sermyaga, the coarse undyed cloth of peasants. Once a length of cloth came to the monastery that was so poor that seven monks refused it. Sergius alone took it, and he wore the robe made from it until it fell apart.

A robe like that one was what the plowman saw through the gap in the fence. The patched clothing that made Sergius look like an orphan was the clothing he had chosen. Poverty he could choose for himself. Water for a growing community was another matter.

Do Not Call It My Spring

As more monks arrived, the monastery outgrew its water, and the brothers grumbled at their hegumen again. Why had he settled them in a place with no water? Sergius went down into a ravine, where a little rainwater had collected in a puddle, and prayed there. A spring rose from the ground. Epiphanius says it was still in use when he wrote.

People soon began to call it Sergius’s spring. When he heard it, Sergius was angry. He forbade them ever to call it by his name, because he had not given them the water. The Lord had given it, he said, to all of them, unworthy as they were, himself included.

He was even more careful with a greater wonder. A man came to the monastery carrying his sick little son, and the child died in his arms. The father went away to get a coffin. While he was gone, Sergius knelt and prayed, and the child lived. When the father returned, Sergius would not let him think there had been a miracle. The boy had only been cold, he said, and had warmed up in the cell. No one rises from the dead before the general resurrection. And he forbade the father to tell anyone, warning him that it would only do him harm.

The father did not dare to spread the news, yet he could not keep from praising God for it, and the story became known through one of Sergius’s disciples. Some time later the monastery in the forest received a letter from much farther away.

One Rule Is Missing

Greek envoys arrived from Constantinople, from Patriarch Philotheus himself. They brought Sergius gifts: a cross, a paraman, the cloth a monk wears over his shoulders, and the schema, the habit of the highest monastic rank. They also brought a letter, and in it the Patriarch told the hegumen, “One rule is missing: you have no common life.”

Until then each monk had lived in his own cell. Sergius took the envoys’ letter to Metropolitan Alexis, the monk Stefan had known in Moscow, who had it read aloud and gave the same advice. So Sergius gave his monastery a common rule. There were now a cellarer, cooks, bakers, and an infirmarian for the sick, and no brother owned anything of his own. The Life does not say in what year this happened.

From then on, the forest sent out monks as well as receiving them. Sergius founded or helped to found monasteries at Kirzhach, at Simonov, at Dubenka, at Golutvin, and at Vysoky, and he had a part in the Andronikov monastery that Alexis founded. Several of the monks he trained are honored as saints themselves, among them Nikon, Savva, Andronikus, Micah, and Athanasius of Serpukhov. The elder under the oak had said the boy would lead many after him. Now he was doing it.

The brothers saw signs they were not meant to talk about. When Prince Vladimir visited the monastery, Sergius served the Liturgy, and two monks, Isaac the Silent and Makarii, saw a fourth priest serving at the altar behind their hegumen, shining with light. No priest had come with the prince. Sergius told them that it was an angel of the Lord who served with him whenever he served, and that they must tell no one about it while he lived.

The hardest test of his poverty came from his friend Metropolitan Alexis. As his death drew near, Alexis called Sergius to him and gave him a gold cross set with jewels. “Forgive me, master,” Sergius answered. From his youth, he said, he had not been a gold-bearer, and in his old age he wished to stay poor. Alexis put the cross on him anyway, and then told him why he had been sent for. He wanted Sergius to be made a bishop, and after his own death to take the metropolitan throne itself, the leadership of the Russian Church. Sergius answered that it was beyond his strength, and he would not be moved. Alexis died in 1378, and the grand princes asked again, but Sergius stayed as firm as diamond, the Life says. The same chapter records a prophecy of his about Archimandrite Michael, who put on a metropolitan’s robes and later died on the way to Constantinople.

Before long, a grand prince would come to the forest with a question of his own.

A Letter on the Road

Mamai had gathered the Horde for war. Grand Prince Dmitri came to the monastery in the forest to ask the hegumen one question. Should he march?

Sergius blessed him and, as the Life puts it, armed him with prayer. He told the prince to go out against the godless, and that he would win if God helped him. Dmitri made a vow: if he came back victorious, he would build a monastery in honor of the Mother of God.

On the road the Russians came within sight of the Tatar host. It was so huge that they stopped, in doubt. Then a messenger arrived with a letter from Sergius.

“Without any doubt, lord, go boldly against their fierceness.”

— Sergius’s letter to Grand Prince Dmitri, as quoted in A. P. Rogovich, Prepodobny Sergii Radonezhsky, 1922 (our translation)

The prince went forward, and the Russians won. In the Life as it survives, Sergius knew the outcome before any messenger could bring it. At the hour of the battle, many days’ walk away, he stood in prayer with his brothers and saw the fighting as if he were there. A little later, when the enemy was fully beaten, he told the brothers of Dmitri’s courage, named the Russian soldiers who had fallen, and prayed for them. When Dmitri came back, he visited the monastery, thanked the old man and the brothers, and gave the monastery a rich gift. Then he reminded Sergius of his vow. Sergius found a place on the river Dubenka and built the Dormition monastery there, and his disciple Savva became its first hegumen.

That is how the Life tells it. It never names the battlefield, gives no year, and does not name a single warrior from the monastery. A later work, the Tale of the Mamai Rout, places the blessing before the battle of Kulikovo Field in 1380 and brings in two monks, Peresvet and Oslyabya, who appear nowhere in the Life. The promise also grew firmer as the story was retold. In the earliest text Sergius says that if God helped him, Dmitri would win and come home unharmed. Pachomius’s revision of the Life has him say that with God’s help he would win and come home unharmed with great honor. By the time the historian Klyuchevsky told the story, it had become a plain prophecy:

“Go boldly against the godless, without wavering, and you will win.”

— Klyuchevsky’s retelling of Sergius’s blessing, quoted in A. P. Rogovich, Prepodobny Sergii Radonezhsky, 1922 (our translation)

The historian Kuchkin reads the Life differently. He argues that its story of the blessing belongs to an earlier battle, on the river Vozha in 1378, and was joined to Kulikovo only in later texts. In the Life itself the picture at the center is simple. A prince came to a monk in the forest and asked what he should do, and a letter reached the army just as its courage failed.

In his own monastery, though, Sergius was waiting for a visit of another kind.

Do Not Be Afraid, Chosen One

One night Sergius prayed the akathist hymn before an icon of the Mother of God, asking her to intercede with her Son for the monastery. When he finished, he turned to his disciple Micah. “Be vigilant,” he said. “We will have a wondrous visit.”

A voice was heard: “The Most Pure comes.” Sergius hurried out of the cell into the entry, and a light brighter than the sun shone around him. In it stood the Mother of God, with the apostles Peter and John. Sergius fell on his face. Micah lay on the floor, half dead from the light. Then the Virgin touched Sergius.

“Do not be afraid, my chosen one. I have come to visit you.”

— The Mother of God to Sergius, in the Life of Sergius, chapter 26 (our translation)

His prayer for his disciples and his monastery had been heard, she told him. The monastery would have abundance, in his lifetime and after his death, and she would not leave this place. Afterward Sergius was too shaken to speak. When he could, he called two of the brothers, Isaac and Simon, and told them what had happened. The monastery still keeps the memory of that visit every year, on 24 August (6 September on the civil calendar).

The monk who had once eaten moldy bread from a sieve had been promised abundance by the Mother of God. Other brothers saw signs too, and more than once he asked the witnesses to keep silent. Simon, who served as ecclesiarch, the monk in charge of the church, was at the Liturgy one day when he saw fire moving across the altar. As Sergius came to receive Communion, the fire gathered itself together like a veil and went into the chalice. Sergius forbade him to tell anyone until Sergius himself was dead.

The Life tells of one more sign, and Simon was near for that one too. One night, while Sergius prayed for the brothers, a voice called his name, and when he opened the window of his cell a light brighter than day filled the darkness. A great flock of beautiful birds flew about the monastery and around it. The voice told him that his disciples would be as many as the birds, and would not dwindle after him if they followed in his footsteps. Sergius called Simon, who saw only a gleam of the light, and told him everything.

He was an old man now, and the brothers he had gathered would soon have to go on without him.

A Face Not Like the Dead

Six months before he died, Sergius handed the monastery over to his disciple Nikon and made him hegumen in his place. In September he fell ill. He called the brothers together and gave them his last instructions. They were to keep the Orthodox faith, stay of one mind with one another, keep themselves pure, welcome strangers, and avoid quarrels. And they were not to seek honors, the one thing he himself had refused all his life.

Called by God, he told them, he was leaving them. He entrusted them to the Lord Almighty and to His Most Pure Mother, who would be their refuge and their wall against the snares of the enemy. His disciples held him up as he received Communion. Then he raised his hands and prayed, and in that prayer he died.

It was 25 September 1392. In the Life as Pachomius revised it, he was seventy-eight years old. A fragrance filled the cell, and his face was bright, “not like a dead man’s.” He was buried at the monastery he had begun with two pairs of shoulders and a load of logs.

Epiphanius, a monk of the same monastery, began taking notes within a year or two of Sergius’s death. He gathered them for twenty years, from elders who had known Sergius, from a man who had waited on him, from Sergius’s own brother Stefan, and from things he had heard Sergius say himself. He finished the Life twenty-six years after the death. His story ends at the grave. The grave had another story to tell.

Water Presses My Body

Thirty years after his death, Sergius appeared in a dream to a devout man. Why, he asked, did they leave him so long in the grave, covered with earth? “Water presses my body.”

Nikon, still hegumen, opened the grave before princes and a council. Among them was Prince Yury Dmitrievich, Sergius’s own godson. Water stood on both sides of the body, but it had not touched him. The body and even the robe were whole, and the grave was filled with fragrance. On 5 July, in the year 1422 according to modern scholarship, the monks held a joyful feast and placed his relics in a shrine. Nikon then built a new church of the Trinity, this time in stone, and had it painted by Daniil and Andrei. Five years later, in 1427, a monastery was founded at Varnitsy, the village in the Rostov country that is named as his birthplace.

Veneration of Sergius began before the Russian Church had formal rules for canonization, and no single act of canonization survives. The church historian Golubinsky pointed to a letter of Metropolitan Jona, from 1449 or 1450, that calls Sergius “prepodobny,” venerable, the title of a monk-saint, and sets him beside the other wonderworkers of Moscow. He took it as the moment of his glorification throughout the Church. Some encyclopedias give 1452. In the sixteenth century the learned monk Maxim the Greek openly doubted that Sergius was a saint, because Sergius and the hierarchs of Moscow, he said, held towns, villages, and wealth.

For nearly five centuries the shrine stood at the heart of the monastery, leaving it only when fire or war threatened. Then, in Soviet Russia, the shrine itself was opened.

A Lock of Hair, 1919

On 1 February 1919 the Soviet Commissariat of Justice decreed the organized opening of saints’ relics. On 11 April 1919 a commission, with representatives of the Church present, opened the shrine of Sergius. They found bones and the rotted fragments of a rough monk’s robe, and the foot bones had crumbled away entirely. In the skull lay a lock of fair, reddish hair, wrapped in waxed paper that looked recent. A protocol was drawn up and signed by those present, including Archimandrite Kronid, the vicar of the monastery. The filmmaker Lev Kuleshov filmed the proceedings for a newsreel.

The Church remembers it differently. According to church tradition, before the opening, Pavel Florensky and Count Yury Olsufiev secretly took Sergius’s head from the shrine and put the skull of a Prince Trubetskoy, buried at the Lavra, in its place. The tradition says that during the war the head was hidden in the altar of the Vladimirskaya church at Vinogradovo, and that it rejoined the relics in 1946.

The shrine became an exhibit in the Lavra museum. In the summer of 1941 it was sealed and sent to Solikamsk, where it stayed from 25 July 1941 until 19 November 1944, when it came back to the Trinity cathedral.

On Holy Saturday, 20 April 1946, the day before Easter, the relics of Sergius were handed back to the Church. They were carried from the Trinity cathedral, which still belonged to the museum, to the Dormition cathedral. Today they rest again in the Trinity cathedral.

Hegumen of the Russian Land

The Orthodox Church keeps two great days for Sergius. One is 25 September, the day of his death, by the Church’s Julian calendar. On the civil calendar it falls on 8 October. On 5 July, which is 18 July on the civil calendar, she remembers the day his relics were found in 1422. He is also remembered with the saints of Radonezh, of Moscow, of Rostov and Yaroslavl, and of Vladimir. He left no writings, and only his spiritual instructions are known.

The stories did not end with his death. The Life in its later form adds more than fifty chapters of miracles and tales from the years after it. Among them are healings at his tomb. Another is a warning in a dream during the siege of his monastery in 1608 to 1610, and another a vision of his monastery shown to Don Cossacks in battle at sea.

Russians honor him as the patron of their country, and also as the patron of students. The patron of students is the boy who could not learn his letters until an old monk under an oak gave him a piece of bread that tasted like honey.

The plowman had been right about the robe. But on a night in his cell the Mother of God had promised this orphan that she would not leave his monastery. Loaves came to its gate, and a spring rose in its ravine. The brothers who came one by one filled the forest and then went out to fill other forests, and the monastery still stands around the shrine where he lies.

A Note on the Sources

The Life of Sergius was written by Epiphanius the Wise in 1417–18, but it survives only as revised by Pachomius the Serb, who worked at the Trinity monastery between about 1440 and 1459. Pachomius changed the style and added the finding of the relics and the posthumous miracles, and Simon Azarin added more miracles in the seventeenth century. The stronger form of the promise to Dmitri belongs to Pachomius’s revision, and Dimitry of Rostov’s later Life makes it a firm prophecy. The earlier wording is known here from Russian Wikipedia’s quotation of Epiphanius. Whether the vision of the battle was already in Epiphanius’s own text, the sources read do not say. Almost no dates appear in the Life for Sergius’s own life, and the year of his death is the firm one. The Life as Pachomius revised it gives seventy-eight years at death and twenty-three at tonsure, which point to a birth in 1314, and the scholar Borisov keeps that year. The earlier line of the tradition gives about seventy years at death and a tonsure at twenty, which point to a birth in 1322, and Kuchkin, Kloss, and Averyanov accept it. Nowhere does the Life name the battle before which Dmitri was blessed. The Kulikovo link, and the names Peresvet and Oslyabya, are found in the later Tale of the Mamai Rout and in later retellings such as Rogovich’s, not in the Life. The story of the head hidden by Florensky is told by church tradition. As Russian Wikipedia describes the 1919 protocol, it records a skull with a lock of hair in recent waxed paper, other bones, and robe fragments. The protocol itself was not read. Our quotations from the Life are our own English from a modern Russian translation, and they are kept short.

How This Life Was Researched

This life draws on four sources, in Russian and English. The main one is the fifteenth-century Life by Epiphanius and Pachomius, read in a modern Russian translation. The others are Dimitry of Rostov’s Life in an English translation, the Russian Wikipedia article with its summary of modern scholarship, and a study published in emigration in 1922. Chapters 2–33 of the Life were checked against the Russian text. The posthumous miracles in chapters 34–88 were known mainly from their chapter titles, with the siege and Cossack accounts spot-checked, so they are summarized here only briefly. The research marked nine details as ones it did not find in the English text it consulted. All translations from Russian are our own.
