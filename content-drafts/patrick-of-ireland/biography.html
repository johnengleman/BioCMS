A Sin Confessed Thirty Years Before

The blow came from the one man he trusted most. Long before, when Patrick was a young man in Britain and not yet even a deacon, he had gone to this friend with a fault from his boyhood, something from the year he was about fifteen, and confessed it. He had entrusted his very soul to this man. It was the same friend who, years later, brought him the news that would shape the rest of his life: “Look, you are to be given the rank of bishop.”

Thirty years after that confession, Patrick was a bishop in Ireland, living among the people who had once kept him as a slave. Back in Britain, some of his elders had turned against his mission, and they had found something to use against him: the sin he had confessed three decades before. Then the friend himself shamed him in public, at a time when Patrick was across the sea in Ireland, with no way to answer.

We know all this only because Patrick told it himself. Near the end of his life he set out to answer his accusers in writing, and it cost him something to do it, because he wrote Latin roughly and knew it, and among his readers would be clever talkers, “rhetoricians,” as he called them with some bite. He made no attempt to sound like their equal. He began like this:

“I am Patrick, a sinner, the most unlettered and least of all the faithful, and a person many despise.”

— Patrick, Confessio 1 (our translation)

That sentence opens the Confessio, one of the two pieces of writing that survive from Patrick’s hand. Both are short, both are in his rough Latin, and together they are almost all we know of him for certain, since no one else in his own century wrote about him at all. Nearly everything else the world tells about Patrick was written down long after he died, some of it many centuries after.

In his own words he is a stranger and harder man than the legends: a former slave who went back, of his own free will, to the country where he had been a slave. He stayed there until he died, poor and in danger every day, looked down on at home for his Latin and now for his past. His parents had begged him not to go, and his elders had tried to stop him. Why would anyone do it?

His answer runs through every page he wrote. He had been a stone sunk in the mud, God had picked him up, and he could not keep quiet about it. The story begins on a small farm in Britain, on the day he was carried away.

Taken From His Father’s Farm

Patrick came from a family of some standing and of long Christian habit. His father, Calpornius, was a deacon of the Church and also a decurion, a member of the town council, and his grandfather Potitus had been a priest. They lived near a village that Patrick calls Bannavem Taberniae, a place no one has ever identified, and the family kept a small farm close by.

It was at that farm that he was seized, at about sixteen, and taken across the sea to Ireland as one captive among many thousands. When he came to tell the story, many years later, the first blame he gave went to his own people: they had turned away from God, he wrote, and had not listened to their priests, and what happened to them was deserved. Then, about himself, he set down two short sentences. “I was then about sixteen. I did not know the true God.”

It is a startling admission from the son of a deacon and the grandson of a priest. Patrick had grown up in a Christian house, among the Church’s own officials, and the faith had not reached him, and he never softened that fact. When he looked back on the boy he had been, he did not see an innocent who suffered. He saw a stone stuck fast in a bog, and he described what happened next with an image that stayed with him all his life:

“Before I was humbled, I was like a stone lying in deep mud; and He who is mighty came, and in His mercy raised me and lifted me up, and placed me on the top of the wall.”

— Patrick, Confessio 12 (tr. G. T. Stokes, 1887)

In the same passage he calls himself “a rustic, a fugitive, unlearned.” Each word was true, and each was a sore point his critics would press on, yet Patrick turned all three into his evidence. If God could lift a stone like that onto the top of a wall, then the lifting was God’s work, and there was nothing in it for the stone to boast of. The lifting began in Ireland, on the open hillsides, in the cold.

A Hundred Prayers a Day

In Ireland the boy became a herdsman. Day after day he went out with the animals in his care, and for six years he served the same master. About that master and the work he tells us almost nothing, because what he wanted to record was happening inside himself.

Out on the land, alone with the herd, he began to pray. He prayed in the woods and on the mountain, and he was up before dawn to pray in every kind of weather, until prayer filled his days and his nights:

“Up to a hundred prayers in one day, and nearly as many at night… through snow, frost and rain, and I felt no harm.”

— Patrick, Confessio 16 (our translation)

He offers no theory of what changed him. He simply sets it down: the boy who had not known the true God was praying through the night on an Irish hillside, and the snow did not hurt him. He was fasting as well, and we know this because of what came next.

One night, after six years of this, he heard a voice in a dream. “You fast well,” it said. “You will soon go to your own country.” A little later the voice came again, and this time it sounded less like a promise than a summons: “Look, your ship is ready.”

The ship was perhaps two hundred miles away, in a place he had never been, among people he did not know. To reach it he would have to leave the only master he had known in Ireland and cross the country as what he now was, a runaway slave. Patrick went. He turned his back on the man he had served for six years and set out, with nothing to guide him but the voice.

Your Ship Is Ready

He found the ship where the voice had said it would be, went to the shipmaster, and asked to sail with them. The answer was a flat refusal: “On no account seek to go with us.”

It is easy to miss how much rode on that moment. Patrick had come two hundred miles on the word of a dream, and now the dream seemed to have failed him at the water’s edge. He did not argue. He turned and walked away, praying as he went, and he was still praying when he heard one of the sailors shouting after him to come back. They would take him after all.

In his telling, the answer to the prayer arrived before the prayer was finished, and that was all he needed to say about it. Three days later the ship made land, and Patrick and the crew stepped off into a country where, it turned out, there was nothing to eat.

Twenty-Eight Days in a Wilderness

For twenty-eight days they travelled through a deserted land, and the food ran out. As hunger set in, the shipmaster, who had first refused Patrick and then taken him aboard, turned on him with a question that was half a taunt and half a plea:

“What is this, Christian? You say your God is great and almighty. Why can you not pray for us?”

Patrick took the challenge seriously and told them to turn to the Lord. Not long afterward a herd of pigs appeared on the road in front of them, and the hungry men had their meal. Patrick claims nothing for himself in the telling; his only comment is that “by the help of God, so it came to pass.” They also found wild honey, and when one of the crew remarked that it had been offered in sacrifice, Patrick would not eat it.

That same night something fell on him, which he describes as Satan coming down on him “like a huge rock.” Pinned beneath it, he began to shout the name Helias, Elijah, with all his strength, and as he shouted, he writes, the brightness of the sun fell upon him, and the weight was gone. It is one of the strangest passages in all his writing, and he tells it plainly, as a thing that happened to him. He was sure that he had been rescued, and just as sure that he had not rescued himself.

Even then the road home ran crooked. He was taken captive once more, and a voice told him that he would be with his captors for two months; on the sixtieth night he was free. Where this happened, and who held him, he never says.

And then, at last, he was home. After a few years he was with his family in Britain again, the son they had lost at sixteen, grown now into a man who prayed through the night. They had no intention of losing him a second time.
