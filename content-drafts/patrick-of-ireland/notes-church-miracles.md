## Dating the Lives (all from S1 unless noted)

- Muirchú: wrote at the command of bishop Aed of Sletty, who died 698 [S1 intro, Book of Armagh description]. So c. 690s, about 230 years after the traditional death.
- Tírechán: notes taken from his foster-father Ultán of Ardbraccan, who died 656 [S1 intro]. So mid to late 7th century.
- Tripartite Life: Stokes argues it "could not have been written before the middle of the tenth century, and that it was probably compiled in the eleventh" [S1 intro p. lxiii]. Baring-Gould says it was "put together in the loth [sic, 10th] century" [S4].
- Jocelin of Furness: flourished about 1185, wrote at the request of Thomas, archbishop of Armagh, and John de Courcy [S4]. Baring-Gould: "of little historical value" and often "contradicts on the authority of some idle legend" (paraphrase).
- Patrick's own writings are the only fifth-century witness. Other agents cover them.

## Timeline (miracles and cult, by date of the report)

1. 7th c. (Muirchú, Tírechán): Easter fire at the Graves of Fíacc's Men near the Boyne, the night of the Tara feast. Status: reported by the 7th-century Lives. See Miracles 1.
2. 7th c.: Patrick confronts the king's druids Lochru and Lucet Máel at Tara. Status: reported by 7th-century Lives. See Miracles 2 to 4.
3. c. 700 onward: Patrick's staff (Bachall Ísu) given by Jesus. First found in the Tripartite Life, 10th/11th c. Status: legendary. NEW detail below.
4. 10th/11th c. Tripartite Life: raisings of the dead, the cursed and blessed, the great grave of Cass. See Miracles 5 to 9.
5. 1094 to 1105: shrine of Patrick's bell made, per search summary (to check, see Other).
6. 1173: staff taken from Ballyboughal to Christ Church, Dublin (per Wikipedia summary; to check).
7. 1177: John de Courcy seizes Down. Church at Down refounded; Benedictines from Chester in 1183 [S5].
8. 1185: bodies of Patrick, Columba, Brigid found in a triple vault at Downpatrick. Butler: "His body was found there in a church of his name in 1185" [S3]. Baring-Gould: found "in a triple vault, together with [Brigid] and S. Columba, in the year 1185" [S4]. Cusack: same discovery, translation 9 June 1186 under Cardinal Vivian, sent by Pope Urban III [S5]. Status: recorded; the identification is contested (Cusack: "It has been doubted whether Downpatrick could lay claim to the honour").
9. 1315: Edward Bruce carries off the enshrined right hand of Patrick [S5].
10. c. 1360: Higden's *Polychronicon* mentions the Staff of Jesus kept in Dublin [S3].
11. 1538: Lord Grey defaces Downpatrick and burns the cathedral [S5]. Staff of Jesus burned in Dublin by the "Saxons" per the Annals of Loch Cé, tr. O'Curry [S5]. The annalist says he is "not certain whether it was not in the year preceding" (1537).
12. 1726: Caleb Threlkeld gives the first written account that Patrick used the three-leaved plant for the Trinity [S9; see Other for the 1600s question].
13. 1899: granite slab placed at the traditional grave at Downpatrick [S10].
14. 1961: John XXIII letter for the 1500th anniversary of Patrick's "pious death" [S8]. Irish bishops in Nigeria name Patrick patron of Nigeria in 1961 [S12].

## Miracles (distinct events, one line each)

Format: number, event, reporter, status.

1. Easter fire (Muirchú ch. 14 to 15; Tripartite). The night of the Tara feast, no fire may be lit before the king's fire on pain of death. Patrick pitches his tent at "the burial place of the men of Fíacc" and lights the Easter fire; it "shone in the night and was seen by almost all the people who lived in the plain" (Muirchú, Bieler: paraphrase). The druids tell the king that unless it is put out that night "it will never be extinguished at all" (paraphrase of Muirchú). Tripartite: "no fire should be kindled in Ireland before the fire of Tara"; the druids: "unless it is quenched in the night on which it was made, it will not be quenched till doomsday" [S1 p. 43]. Reporters: Muirchú (c. 690s), Tripartite (10th/11th c.). Status: reported by 7th-century Lives. NOTE: neither text names Slane as the place of the fire. Muirchú and the Tripartite give the Graves of Fíacc's Men (Ferta Fer Féicc). Muirchú names Slane only as the place where the relics of Ercc son of Daig are venerated [S2 ch. 17]. The modern Slane location is a later tradition: Stokes's introduction already says "proceeded on foot to Slane, where he lighted his paschal fire" [S1 intro p. cxli-cxlii], a modern gloss. Hill of Slane identification: to check.
2. The king's party refuses to rise for Patrick. Only Ercc son of Daig stands, believes, and becomes bishop of Slane (Muirchú; Tripartite). Reported by 7th-century Lives. Status: reported.
3. Druid Lochru insults the faith; Patrick prays; Lochru is lifted into the air and dashed on a stone and dies. Muirchú ch. 17; Tripartite p. 45. Exact Tripartite (Stokes): "the wizard was raised into the air and forthwith again cast down, and his brains were scattered on the stone, and he was broken in pieces, and died in their presence." Status: reported by 7th-century Lives.
4. Poisoned cup. Lucet Máel puts poison in Patrick's cup; Patrick blesses it, the liquor curdles, he tips the vessel and the poison falls out; then the liquor is made good again (Muirchú; Tripartite p. 55). Status: reported by 7th-century Lives.
5. Snow and darkness contest. Lucet Máel brings snow "till it reached men's girdles"; Patrick says "I have no desire to go against God's will"; Patrick blesses the plain and "at Patrick's word the snow vanished, without rain, without sun, without wind" (Tripartite pp. 55-58, Stokes). Muirchú has snow also. Then darkness over Tara (Tripartite). Status: reported by 7th-century Lives.
6. The house ordeal by fire. A house half green and half dry; the druid in Patrick's chasuble in the green half, Benen (Benignus) in the druid's tunic in the dry half. The green half and the druid burn; the chasuble does not burn; Benen is unharmed and "the tunic was burnt so that (the fire) made ashes thereof" (Tripartite p. 59). Muirchú: the chasuble untouched; Benen unharmed. Status: reported by 7th-century Lives. In the Tripartite, Patrick makes three gems from the tears of three hostage children, who swallow them; the gems will be born as Colum Cille, Comgall and Finnia (Tripartite p. 59). Status: legendary (late Life). NEW for English pages.
7. Panic at Tara and the king's conversion. Patrick calls out "Let God arise"; darkness and an earthquake; fifty men die; only Loegaire, his queen and one man remain (Tripartite pp. 45-46). The queen Angass begs Patrick; the king kneels and "gave him a false peace" [S1 p. 46: "dorat brecsith"]. Status: reported, the king's conversion is contested (Muirchú: Loíguire's sincerity is doubted in the text; to check).
8. The Deer's Cry (Faeth Fiada): Patrick and his eight companions pass as eight deer through an ambush; Patrick leads with a fawn on his back (Tripartite p. 46; Muirchú). Status: reported by 7th-century Lives; the hymn is attributed to Patrick (S6 says "universally accepted as genuine"; contested by some scholars; not checked).
9. Raising of the dead.
   a. Cass son of Glass, the giant swineherd in a grave 120 feet long: Patrick has the grave opened, "brought to life the dead man", and asks his tidings (Tripartite). Status: reported by later Lives, legendary.
   b. Echaid son of Crimthann: "Patrick raised Echaid son of Crimthann from death" (Tripartite). Status: later Life.
   c. Son of Ailill: swine had eaten the boy; Patrick tells the Culdee Malach to raise him; "'I will not tempt the Lord,' saith Malach"; Patrick curses Malach's cloister (Tripartite). Status: later Life.
   d. Macc-Cuill's faked corpse: robbers put a man on a bier and ask Patrick to heal him; the man is truly dead when Patrick prays (Tripartite pp. 221-222). Status: later Life.
   e. Patrick's sister Lupait: "he raised the sister up at once" after a fall; the scar remains (Tripartite p. 15 area; this is a healing, not a raising). Status: legendary.
   f. Tripartite on the general gift: "after having raised the dead, after having converted much people unto God" (S1 p. 254). Muirchú, in his index, lists "How a dead man spoke to him" (S2, Book II 2) [paraphrase].
10. Staff of Jesus. Tripartite: Patrick on Mount Hermon (or its neighbourhood) is told by the Lord to go and preach to the Gael; "gave him the staff of Jesus, and said that it would be a helper to him in every danger and in every unequal conflict" [S1 p. 31]. Used to lay low the idol Cenn Cruaich and to slay a druid (Stokes introduction). Muirchú does not tell this origin (not found in the chapters I read). Status: legendary (10th/11th c.). Staff itself: Church-related relic. St Bernard names it as an insignia of Armagh (Cusack; paraphrase). Burned 1538 per Annals of Loch Cé [S5].
11. The bell. Tripartite: the Wave-voice of the Tyrrhene Sea, left in Druim Ferta (a bell) [S1 notes]. The bell later called Clog an Édachta or the "bell of the will": 11th-c. shrine; now National Museum of Ireland (from search summary; to check). Croagh Patrick: Patrick rings his bell and flings it at the demons, birds scatter into the sea; CE (1911) retells this: "At length he rang his sweet-sounding bell... He flung his bell among them" [S6]. Status: legendary; bell relic recorded.
12. Croagh Patrick Lent: forty days; Moran lists his five petitions, including that Patrick will judge the Irish on the last day [S6]. Muirchú, Book II, lists "Of Patrick's four petitions" [S2]. Status: reported by 7th-century Lives (the petitions), later layers added.
13. Death: the sun and night. In Muirchú "How nightfall was suspended, and of the twelve nights without darkness" [S2 contents]. Tripartite: "there was no night in Mag Inis, but an angelic radiance therein" for the twelve nights; "oxen brought him as far as the stead wherein to-day standeth Downpatrick" [S1 p. 255]. Status: reported by 7th-century Lives.
14. Angel decides the burial place at Armagh/Saul; Muirchú: angel prevented him from choosing to die at Armagh [S2]. Status: reported.
15. Snakes. Jocelin of Furness (early 13th c.): Patrick "bore on his shoulder the staff of Jesus", gathered all poisonous creatures and drove them from a promontory into the sea [S9 quotes it]. Gerald of Wales calls it "a flattering fiction" [S9]. Solinus (3rd c.) and Bede already say Ireland has no snakes [S9, quotes]. Status: legendary. Orthodox Greek service (2005, Somalis) still says Patrick "delivered the isle of Ireland from the serpents" [S11].
16. Flesh-meat that changed into fishes: Sanidopoulos post exists (2015). Not read. See "not read".

## Cult: status table

- Canonization. No formal papal process; Patrick pre-dates the papal reservation of cases. Butler: Patrick's name "marked on the seventeenth of March, in the Martyrology of Bede" [S3] (paraphrase of the line "His festival is marked on the seventeenth of March, in the Martyrology of Bede, &c."). Status: Church-recognized by long cult. I did not find a Church document that states "canonized"; the "Pre-Congregation" label comes from web summaries only. Do not claim a canonization date.
- Roman Martyrology, 1846 English: "In Ireland, the birthday of St. Patrick, bishop and confessor, who was the first to preach Christ in that country, and became illustrious by great miracles and virtues." [S7, "The Seventeenth of March"]. The same page also lists "At Clermont, in Auvergne, the demise of St. Patrick, bishop" the day before (16 Mar). Status: Church-recognized. Current Martyrologium Romanum (2004) wording: not read.
- Roman Breviary (1908 English edition): "Patrick, Archbishop [of Armagh,] Confessor. Greater Double" on 17 March; also in the list of feasts of the English calendar [archive.org `romanbreviaryre01cath`]. Status: Church-recognized.
- Vatican: John XXIII, 1961, calls him "Hibernorum Apostoli S. Patricii" and speaks of the "quintum et decimum ... saeculum" since his "pio decessu" (death). Patrick was sent "a Decessore Nostro S. Caelestino I" [S8]. The letter calls him "patriae vestrae fulcimenti et decoris" (support and honour of your country). Status: Church-recognized (papal letter, a witness that the Church treats him as sent by Celestine; this follows later tradition; the historical claim is contested, since Patrick's own writings do not name Celestine).
- Relics: Downpatrick 1185 (above). Status: recorded, contested identification. Patrick's hand shrine and the Down relic history: S5. Muirchú says burial place unknown per the OCA (S10): "Muirchu says that no one knows the place where Saint Patrick is buried" (OCA's words; I did not find the line in the chapters I read; check Muirchú II 11). Columba's prophecy of burial under the flag "beneath which are Patrick and Brigid" is quoted by Cusack as an old prophecy [S5].
- Orthodox: feast 17 March [S10]. Title "Enlightener of Ireland." Greek service composed 2005 by Panagiotis Somalis; the English service in use is a translation of a Slavonic service from the 1970s by Valeria Hoecke, blessed by the ROCOR Synod [S11]. OCA: "Many people now regard the story of Saint Patrick driving all the snakes out of Ireland as having no historical basis" [S10]. OCA also states he "used the shamrock to illustrate the doctrine of the Holy Trinity", without calling it legend.
- Patronage: Ireland (S8 Vatican: "fulcimenti et decoris"). Nigeria: named by Irish bishops in Nigeria in 1961 [S12] (NPR says bishops named him, so this is not a papal act). Engineers: only a weak web claim; no source read. Snakes: legendary.

## Shamrock

- Catholic Encyclopedia 1911 says on the Tara scene: Patrick "is said to have plucked a shamrock from the sward, to explain by its triple leaf and single stem, in some rough way, to the assembled chieftains, the great doctrine of the Blessed Trinity" [S6]. The wording is "is said".
- Muirchú and the Tripartite (read) do not mention a shamrock in the Tara scenes. I searched the Tripartite text for "shamrock" and found none (not exhaustively; the search was by key terms only).
- Sanidopoulos [S9]: the association begins in the 17th c.; Threlkeld (1726) wrote: "This Plant is worn by the People in their Hats upon the 17th Day of March yearly, (which is called St. Patrick's Day.)" and "It being a Current Tradition, that by this Three Leafed Grass, he emblematically set forth to them the Mystery of the Holy Trinity." [S9, as copied]. So the Trinity story is attested in writing in 1726, called a "Current Tradition". Status: legendary.
- Lead (from the WebFetch summary of Wikipedia, NOT verified): Thomas Dineley, 1681, the Irish "vulgar superstitiously wear shamroges"; Campion 1571 and Spenser 1596 mention eating shamrock. This would put the Patrick's Day link in the 1600s and the Trinity story in 1726. Verify in Dineley's text before use.
- Check prompt claim: "first attested 1600s" is partly right (feast-day wearing, 1681 lead) but the Trinity teaching is first found in 1726 (Threlkeld).

## Character and context (cult-related)

- Muirchú and Tírechán write to build Armagh's claim. S1 intro: the lost "Commemoratio Laborum" lay behind Tírechán. Stokes is sharp: "credulous hagiographers such as Muirchu and Tirechan" [S1 line ~9255, intro].
- Muirchú's Patrick is an Elijah and Peter figure: before Lochru he echoes "as Peter had said concerning Simon" (Muirchú ch. 17, paraphrase).
- Tone: the angry saint. He curses the field of Mudebrod, which "has remained sandy and barren" (Muirchú I 26, paraphrase). Curses Malach's cloister (Tripartite).

## Scenes (for the writer)

1. The night before Easter. The Tara feast: every hearth dark in Ireland on pain of death. One fire burns on the plain of Brega. The druids tell the king it will never go out (S1 p. 43; S2 ch. 14-15).
2. The king's chariots stop outside the tent. The druids tell the king not to enter, so he does not honour the man. They call Patrick out. Only Ercc stands as Patrick comes (S2 ch. 17; S1 pp. 43-44).
3. Lochru lifted and thrown down on a stone (S1 p. 45).
4. The cup that curdles, then the poison that falls out when Patrick turns it over (S1 p. 55).
5. The snow up to men's girdles that melts without sun, rain or wind (S1 p. 56-58).
6. The house half green and half dry: Benen walks into the fire in the druid's tunic and comes out whole (S1 p. 59).
7. The hostage children weep to Patrick, who makes three gems from their tears (S1 p. 59).
8. Patrick on a Lent mountain; the birds black the sky; he rings the bell and flings it (S6; Orthodox hymns echo the serpent legend S11).
9. The oxen carry the body to Downpatrick; twelve nights with no darkness (S1 p. 255).
10. 1538: the staff burned in Skinners Row (S5 says "by the Saxons"; the street detail is from a search summary, to check).
