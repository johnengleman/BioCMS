# Patrick of Ireland — notes, focus: own-words

Researcher: own-words agent. Date: 2026-10-01. Tier A. Tool calls about 30. Plain note: the sources for Patrick are very thin. Only two short Latin texts are his. The entry must say this.

## 1. Identity

- Name in his own writings: only "Patricius" [S1 Conf. 1; S3 Letter 1]. Irish Pádraig; Old Irish Pátraic [S6].
- Calls himself "peccator" (sinner), "rusticissimus" (most unlettered), "indoctus" (unlearned) [S1 Conf. 1; S3 Letter 1].
- Father Calpornius, a deacon; grandfather Potitus, a priest; family village "Bannauem Taberniae" (place not identified in the sources I read) [S1 Conf. 1]. In the Letter he says his father was a "decurion" (town councillor) [S2 Letter 10]. He says he "sold" his noble rank for others.
- Dates: NOT fixed. Wikipedia: active in the 5th century; death "mid-fifth to early-sixth century" [S6]. Annals give 457 (maybe Palladius), 461/2, and 492/3 (17 March, age 120) [S6]. White 1920 uses 389 to 461 as "most recent calculations" of his day [S2 p.1]. Bury 1905 uses 461. Both are old. Do not print exact dates as fact.
- Feast: 17 March. Wikipedia: Catholic in Ireland, solemnity and holy day of obligation [S6]. A scribal colophon at the end of the Confessio in the Armagh text says "Septima decima Martii die translatus est Patricius ad caelos" (not Patrick's words; see Other) [S1].
- Tradition: shared (Catholic and Orthodox; also Anglican, Lutheran). Orthodox: "equal-to-the-apostles and Enlightener of Ireland" [S6]. Orthodox calendar date: to check (OCA page 17 March, not read by me).
- Patronage: Ireland, Nigeria, Montserrat, others [S6].
- Research tier: A, thin sources.

## 3. Timeline (from his own words only; section numbers from S2)

All of this rests on one text each. No outside witness exists for any item. None of the dates are given by him.

- Age about 16: seized from his father's small farm near the village; taken to Ireland with "many thousands" [S1/S2 Conf. 1]. He says he did not know the true God and that the captives deserved it, because they ignored their priests [S2 Conf. 1]. NEW? (popular pages skip the blame he gives his own people).
- Slavery: he herds animals; prays "a hundred" times a day and nearly as often at night, in woods and on the mountain, before dawn in snow, frost and rain, "and I felt no hurt" [S2 Conf. 16].
- After six years: a voice in a dream: "Thou fastest to good purpose..." then "Lo, thy ship is ready." The ship is perhaps 200 miles away; he knows no one there [S2 Conf. 17]. He runs away from the man he had served for six years.
- At the port: the shipmaster says "On no account seek to go with us." He walks away praying; a sailor shouts him back [S2 Conf. 18].
- After three days by sea, 28 days through "a desert"; food fails; the shipmaster mocks his God; Patrick tells them to turn to the Lord; a herd of pigs appears (Conf. 19). They find wild honey; a man says it was offered in sacrifice; Patrick does not eat it [S2 Conf. 19]. (Not his claim that this was a miracle: he says "by the help of God, so it came to pass".)
- That night: Satan "like a huge rock" falls on him; he cries "Helias"; sunlight lifts the weight [S2 Conf. 20].
- Later he is captured "once more" and held 60 nights; then freed [S2 Conf. 21-22]. Where and by whom is not stated.
- Back in Britain "after a few years", with family who beg him not to leave again [S2 Conf. 23].
- Dream of Victoricus bringing countless letters; "The Voice of the Irish" from those by the Wood of Foclut near the western sea: "We beseech thee, holy youth, to come and walk among us once more." [S2 Conf. 23]
- Years of formation: not told in the Confessio. Conf. 43 says he longed to visit Gaul "to behold the face of the saints of my Lord" [S2]. The Armagh "Dicta" has: journey through Gaul, Italy and the Tyrrhene islands [S2 p.67]. Muirchu and Tirechan add Auxerre and Lérins stories [S2 intro p.11]. Legend or later tradition.
- Ordained a deacon, then a bishop; some elders oppose him and many "gifts" are offered to keep him from going [S2 Conf. 26, 32, 37, 46].
- "Thirty years" after a confession he made before he was a deacon, elders use it against him; a close friend, to whom he had entrusted his soul, shames him in public in his absence. Patrick was not in Britain [S2 Conf. 27, 32]. He dreams of a dishonoured writing and hears "We have seen with anger the face of the person" [S2 Conf. 29].
- In Ireland he baptizes "many thousands", ordains clergy, travels to far regions "where never had any one come to baptize" [S2 Conf. 50-51].
- Seized by kings with his companions; bound in irons; freed on the 14th day; goods returned [S2 Conf. 52]. He says he gave gifts to kings and judges, "not less than the price of fifteen men" [S2 Conf. 53].
- Coroticus raid: the day after newly baptized people in white were anointed, they are killed or taken [S2 Letter 3]. Patrick sends a letter by a holy presbyter; the soldiers "jeered" [S2 Letter 3]. Then he writes the Letter with his own hand [S2 Letter 2]. Order of the Letter and the Confessio: not settled in the sources I read. White says only that the Confessio was written late, "almost certainly after a.d. 441" [S2 introduction].
- End: the Confessio closes "this is my confession before I die" [S2 Conf. 62].

## 4. Character (from what he says about himself)

- Self-description: unlearned, rough Latin, ashamed of it; says his Latin is "translated into a tongue not my own" [S2 Conf. 9]. Shy of writing for years [S2 Conf. 9].
- Embarrassed by clever critics yet defiant: "ye clever sirs, ye rhetoricians, hear..." [S2 Conf. 13].
- Defensive: the Confessio answers accusations; he insists he never took payment: "if I demanded from one of them even the price of my shoe; tell it against me" [S2 Conf. 50].
- Fear: expects daily "slaughter, or to be defrauded, or reduced to slavery" [S2 Conf. 55].
- Wounded by betrayal: friend who shamed him publicly [S2 Conf. 32]. A human, hurt voice.
- Honest about faults: says he "was like a stone lying in the deep mire" before being lifted [S2 Conf. 12]; he did not quickly obey the signs [S2 Conf. 46].
- Pastoral anger: the Letter is hard: "patricide, fratricide", "ravening wolves" [S2 Letter 5]; he does not greet the soldiers as Romans: they are "fellow-citizens of demons" [S2 Letter 2].
- Pastoral tenderness: "I can see you" to the dead [S2 Letter 17].
- Class: father a decurion, he "sold" his noble rank [S2 Letter 10]. A critic sees: ignorance (his elders, Conf. 46, "did not say this out of malice").
- Habits: constant prayer; reading of Bible: both texts are full of biblical quotations, mixed Old Latin and Vulgate [S2 intro p.1-2].
- Humor: none found. A dry touch: "Let who will laugh and insult, I shall not be silent" [S2 Conf. 45].

## 5. Quotations (our English; Latin from S1/S3; locator by chapter; rights public-domain text, own English)

Latin copied from CELT with footnote numerals removed. Our English is our own, checked against White's English [S2]. Verified in this session.

1. Conf. 1. "Ego Patricius, peccator rusticissimus et minimus omnium fidelium et contemptibilis sum apud plurimos" = I am Patrick, a sinner, the most unlettered and least of all the faithful, and a person many despise.
2. Conf. 1. "Annorum eram tunc fere sedecim. Deum uerum ignorabam" = I was then about sixteen. I did not know the true God.
3. Conf. 12. "eram uelut lapis qui iacet in luto profundo, et uenit qui potens est, et in sua missericordia sustulit me" = I was like a stone lying in deep mud, and the Mighty One came and lifted me in his mercy. (Latin "uelut", S1.)
4. Conf. 9. "sermo et loquela mea translata est in linguam alienam" = my speech has been carried over into a language not my own. (Wording from S2; Latin check: partial in S1, in the same section.)
5. Conf. 16. "in die una usque ad centum orationes, et in nocte prope similiter... per niuem, per gelu, per pluiam; et nihil mali sentiebam" = up to a hundred prayers in one day, and nearly as many at night... through snow, frost and rain, and I felt no harm.
6. Conf. 17. "Bene ieiunas, cito iturus ad patriam tuam" = You fast well; you will soon go to your own country. Then: "Ecce, nauis tua parata est" = Look, your ship is ready.
7. Conf. 18. Shipmaster: Latin not copied; English "On no account seek to go with us" (S2). Latin to check.
8. Conf. 19. "Quid est, Christiane? Tu dicis Deus tuus magnus et omnipotens est. Quare ergo pro nobis orare non potes?" = What is this, Christian? You say your God is great and almighty. Why can you not pray for us?
9. Conf. 19. "Ecco grex porcorum in uia ante oculos nostros apparuit" = See, a herd of pigs appeared on the road before our eyes. (S1 spelling "Ecco".)
10. Conf. 20. "dum clamarem Heliam uiribus meis, ecce splendor solis illius decidit super me" = while I was shouting "Helias" with all my strength, the brightness of that sun fell on me.
11. Conf. 23. "Vox Hyberionacum" ... "Rogamus te, sancte puer, ut uenias et adhuc ambules inter nos" = The Voice of the Irish... We ask you, holy boy, to come and walk among us again.
12. Conf. 27. "post annos triginta inuenerunt me, et aduersus uerbum, quod confessus fueram antequod essem diaconus" = after thirty years they found me (they used) a word I had confessed before I was a deacon. (Latin as in C text, S1; spelling unclean.)
13. Conf. 32. "Ecce dandus es tu ad gradum episcopatus" = Look, you are to be given the rank of bishop (his friend's words; S1).
14. Conf. 50 (English S2; Latin to check). "I demanded from one of them even the price of my shoe; tell it against me and I shall restore you more." Latin has "calciamenti mei" (S1, in "pretium calciamenti" form).
15. Conf. 55. English: "poverty and affliction become me better than delights and riches" (S2). Latin not copied.
16. Conf. 59. "peto illi, ut det mihi ut cum illis proselitis et captiuis pro nomine suo effundam sanguinem meum, etsi ipsum etiam caream sepultura..." = I ask him to let me shed my blood with these strangers and captives for his name, even if I have no grave.
17. Conf. 60. "sol iste quem uidemus, illo iubente, propter nos cotidie oritur, sed numquam regnabit" = this sun we see rises daily at his command for our sake, but it will never reign.
18. Conf. 62. "Et haec est Confessio mea antequam morior" = And this is my confession before I die.
19. Letter 1. "Patricius peccator indoctus, scilicet Hiberione constitutus episcopum me esse fateor" = Patrick, a sinner, unlearned, I say that I am a bishop, established in Ireland.
20. Letter 1. "inter barbaras itaque gentes habito proselitus et profuga, ob amorem Dei" = so I live among barbarous peoples, a stranger and a refugee, for the love of God.
21. Letter 2. "Manu mea scripsi atque condidi uerba ista" = I wrote and composed these words with my own hand.
22. Letter 3. "Postera die qua crismati neophiti in ueste candida... flagrabat in fronte ipsorum, dum crudeliter trucidati" = the day after the newly baptized, in white, were anointed with chrism, while it still gleamed on their foreheads, they were cruelly slaughtered.
23. Letter 10. "uendidi enim nobilitatem meam (non erubesco neque me penitet) pro utilitate aliorum" = I sold my noble rank (I am not ashamed, and I do not regret it) for the good of others.
24. Letter 14. "Consuetudo Romanorum Gallorum que Christianorum est, mittunt uiros sanctos et idoneos ad Francos et ceteras gentes cum tot millia solidorum ad redimendos captiuos baptizatos: tu totius interficis et uendis illos" = It is the custom of the Christian Roman Gauls to send holy men with many thousands of solidi to the Franks and other peoples to buy back baptized captives. You kill and sell them.
25. Letter 17 (English S2; Latin not copied). "I can see you. Ye have begun to remove to where there shall be no night nor sorrow nor death any more." Note: this is the Armagh "Dictum" 2 also: "Ye departed from the world to Paradise. Thanks be to God." [S2 p.67].

Note: Letter 20 says the words are not his but of God, "quod ego Latinum exposui" = which I put into Latin [S3]. Useful for the writer: Latin is his second tongue.

## 6. Scenes (all from his own words; keep to them)

1. The farm raid: sixteen, taken from his father's small farm with "many thousands" [Conf. 1].
2. The herdsman at dawn: prayers in snow, frost and rain [Conf. 16].
3. The ship at the port: turned away, called back while praying [Conf. 18].
4. The desert: 28 days, starving crew, their taunt, the pigs and honey [Conf. 19].
5. The night weight: Satan like a rock; "Helias"; the sun [Conf. 20].
6. The letters dream: Victoricus; "The Voice of the Irish" [Conf. 23].
7. The betrayal: the friend, the dishonoured writing, "We have seen with anger" [Conf. 27, 29, 32].
8. The kings' irons: bound 14 days; freed [Conf. 52].
9. The white robes: the morning after baptisms in the Letter [Letter 3].
10. The writing desk: "Manu mea scripsi"; he asks that the Letter be read in front of Coroticus [Letter 2, 21].

## 7. Miracles

None in his own writings are claimed as miracles by him. Events he reads as God's help:
- Pigs in the desert, probably 5th c., on the road from the coast; Patrick; as told in the Confessio [S2 Conf. 19]; status: his own report, not investigated.
- Sun lifting the weight after "Helias" [S2 Conf. 20]; status: his own report.
- Escape after 60 nights [S2 Conf. 21] and after 14 days in irons [S2 Conf. 52]: his own report.
- Dreams (the ship, Victoricus, the praying Spirit within him, the dishonoured writing) [S2 Conf. 17, 23-25, 29]: his own report.
- Not in his writings: snakes, shamrock, resurrections, fire at Tara. See next section. Muirchu/Tirechan miracles belong to the witnesses agent.

## 8. Other

### Popular items NOT from Patrick (flag to the writer)

- "St Patrick's Breastplate" (Lorica, "Faeth Fiada", "I bind unto myself today"): Old Irish, in the 11th-century Liber Hymnorum; linguistic dating early 8th century [S7]. Not found in the Confessio or Letter. Bury 1905 called its attribution possible but unproven [S4 p.246]. White 1920 even said "no reason for denying" Patrick wrote it [S2 p.62]. That is an old, optimistic view; more recent: dated to early 8th century [S7]. Our rule: never attribute it to Patrick in the entry; say it is attached to his name.
  - John Colgan (1647) attributed it to Saint Evin and gave it the title Lorica Patricii [S7].
  - The Book of Armagh note says his "canticum Scotticum" should always be sung [S2 p.62; S4]. That is one hymn in Irish, not proven to be this text.
- "Be Thou My Vision": Old/Middle Irish "Rop tú mo baile", attributed to Dallán Forgaill (6th c.), but scholars date the text to the 10th or 11th century; English prose by Mary Byrne 1905, verse by Eleanor Hull 1912; tune "Slane" from 1919 [S8]. Not Patrick.
- Snakes: Wikipedia lists it as legend [S6]. The snake story comes first in Jocelin of Furness (12th c.) [per the web search summary, not read in raw; to check]. Bury notes that snakes at Lérins (Honoratus) appear in a different story [S4, line "Snakes at Lerinus"]. Possible link to the Lérins/Gaul legend: not in any source I read; do not claim.
- Shamrock: attribute added in Wikipedia: "no evidence the shamrock was sacred to the pagan Irish" (Patricia Monaghan) [S6]. The first image of Patrick with a shamrock on a Dublin halfpenny, 1674 [web search summary; raw not read; to check].
- "Prayers of Patrick" and short sayings (for example the "Christ with me, Christ before me" lines): these are verses inside the Lorica, not from Patrick's Latin writings [S2 pp.66-67]. The genuine "Dicta Patricii" in the Book of Armagh are three short sentences (see below).
- Other popular lines to check and probably drop ("may the road rise to meet you", "St Patrick's Day blessing"): not found in any source I read; I did not search. List as popular, no source.

### Genuine short sayings (Book of Armagh, "Dicta Patricii", fol. 9 r° a) [S2 p.67]

- "I had the fear of God as the guide of my journey through Gaul and Italy and, moreover, in the islands which are in the Tyrrhene Sea." (English, S2.)
- "Ye departed from the world to Paradise. Thanks be to God." (cf. Letter 17.)
- "Church of the Scots! nay, of the Romans! ... ye must chant ... Kyrie eleison, Christe eleison." (English, S2.) Latin and genuineness: White treats them as sayings with doubts. To check.

### Manuscripts

- Book of Armagh (Trinity College Dublin): date between 807 and 846 (White's Muirchu intro) [S2 p.68]; CELT says the scribe was Ferdomnach [S1]. It holds the Confessio with gaps, plus Muirchu and Tirechan. White: Confessio and Letter appear there under the title "Libri Sancti Patricii" [S2 p.30].
- Cotton MS Nero E.I. (British Library; CELT says it is the manuscript for the Letter and part of the Confessio). It is the source of the Letter text on CELT. Date of the Cotton MS: not read.
- Confessio colophon: "Huc usque uolumen quod Patricius manu conscripsit sua. Septima decima Martii die translatus est Patricius ad caelos." [S1]. It is a scribe's note, not Patrick's. Use it as a source for the 17 March tradition only.

### Facts the writer needs

- Name "Confessio": White says it is a word of thanks and not penance; it is "an open and thankful acknowledgment of the goodness of God" [S2 p.29]. NEW to many English readers.
- White also says that "Declaration" is the current English title in some books (Wikipedia: "Declaration"). [S6]
- Confessio c.43: he says he wishes to go back to Britain and Gaul but is "bound in the Spirit" [S2].
- Letter c.14 gives a rare outside fact: the Roman Gauls pay ransom to the Franks to redeem baptized captives [S2]. This shows the world he knew.
- White: the Coroticus of the Letter is the king Ceretic of Strathclyde, capital Alcluith (Dumbarton) [S2 p.52-53]. Old identification; White says "conclusively". Treat as the standard view; check in newer work.
- Tirechan's story of the Wood of Foclut (Tirechan, Book of Armagh fol. 10) quoted in S2 p.9-10: a legend growing out of Conf. 23. NEW.
- Conf. 41 in S2 reads "in Iceland": this is a printing error for Ireland in S2 (no source). Ignore the word; the Latin was not checked.
- "Palladius": annals record "the elder Patrick" in 457; may refer to Palladius, sent to Ireland in 431 [S6].
- Muirchu and Tirechan 7th century: earliest Lives [S6]. First Synod of Saint Patrick: 7th-century collection, not his [S6].
- No source I read has an independent witness for Patrick from his own century. Say so in the entry.
