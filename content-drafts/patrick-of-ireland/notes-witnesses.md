## Timeline

### What the Confessio says (S1 = Patrick's own words, 5th c.)
- Birth: father Calpornius, a deacon, son of Potitus, a priest; family village "Bannavem Tabernie" (Stokes's spelling), with a small farm nearby where he was taken. [S1 Conf.1 p.24-25]
- Captured "nearly sixteen", taken to Ireland "with so many thousand men". He says he did not know the true God, and that the captives deserved it for not obeying their priests. [S1 Conf.1 p.25-26]
- Slave years: he fed cattle daily, prayed about a hundred times by day and nearly as often by night, in woods and mountain, through snow, frost, rain. [S1 Conf.6 p.34-35] Six years with one master. [S1 Conf.7]
- Dream of escape: a voice "Thou fastest well... thou shalt soon go to thy country", then "Behold, thy ship is ready". The ship was about 200 miles away; he had never been there. [S1 Conf.6-7 p.35]
- Ship: the captain first refused him ("Do not by any means seek to go with us"); he walked off praying; a sailor called him back. [S1 p.36] NEW detail for most English pages.
- Later captivity: "not many years afterward" taken captive again; a voice said "Two months thou shalt be with them"; freed on the sixtieth night. Stokes notes that Ferguson reads this as a spiritual captivity; others as real. [S1 Conf.10 p.40 and note] Muirchú makes it 60 days of real capture [S3 I 3].
- Journey of 28 days through a desert; no food on arrival night. (Muirchú adds the herd of pigs; legend, S3 I 2.) [S1 Conf.10]
- Return to Britain; parents "besought me that, now at least... I would never leave them again". [S1 Conf.10 p.41]
- Call of the Voice of the Irish: in a night vision a man named Victoricus came "from Ireland", with innumerable letters; the first read "The Voice of the Irish". He heard those by "the wood Foclut... close by the Western Sea" cry "We entreat thee, holy youth, that thou come, and henceforth walk among us." He woke "deeply moved in heart". [S1 Conf.10 p.41-42] KEY SCENE.
- Spirit praying in him: "He who gave for thee His life, is He who speaks in thee." [S1 Conf.11 p.42]
- Opposition: "some of my seniors" urged his sins against his "laborious episcopate". "After thirty years they found me" and brought a word he confessed before he was a deacon. He told his dearest friend of a boyhood fault (about age fifteen). The friend then spoke against him in public. [S1 Conf.11-13 p.43-45]
- Vision of "a writing against me, without honour", and a voice: "We have seen with displeasure the face of the designate with his name stripped." He read "We", not "You", as God taking his side. [S1 Conf.12 p.44]
- Mission: gifts offered "with weeping and tears" to keep him home; he refused seniors "against my wish". He endures insult, "chains", twelve dangers to his life. [S1 Conf.15 p.47-48]
- Baptized "so many thousand"; clergy ordained; monks and virgins of Christ among chieftains' daughters. One noble Irish girl was baptized, then told him of a messenger of God who bade her be a virgin of Christ; six days later she did so. Parents persecute such girls. [S1 Conf.16-18 p.49-52]
- He wanted to see Britain, parents, Gaul and "the face of the saints" but felt "bound in the Spirit". [S1 Conf.19 p.52]
- Accounts: "half a screpall"; "the price of my shoe". He gave presents to kings, paid sons who escorted him; "they seized me together with my companions... bound myself with iron"; freed on the fourteenth day. [S1 Conf.22 p.56-57]
- Daily fear of "murder, or to be circumvented, or to be reduced to slavery". [S1 Conf.23 p.58-59]
- Close: "this is my Confession before I die." Cottonian/Armagh note: "Thus far is what Patrick wrote in his own hand: He was translated to heaven on the seventeenth of March." (A scribal note, not Patrick.) [S1 p.61; S2 Intro xciii quotes the Armagh note "Septima decima Martii die translates est Patricius ad caelos"]

### Coroticus letter (S1 Epistle)
- He calls himself "a sinner, unlearned", appointed bishop in Ireland; writes "with my own hand" to the soldiers of Coroticus, not to "citizens of the Roman saints". [S1 Ep.1 p.61]
- The massacre: the day after neophytes were anointed, "in their white robes, while it (the anointing) yet glistened on their foreheads", they were killed with the sword; others carried off. [S1 Ep.2]
- He sent a holy priest with a letter asking for some of the captives; the soldiers "laughed at them". [S1 Ep.2 p.64]
- He says Roman and Gallic Christians send men with "so many thousands of solidi" to redeem captives, but Coroticus kills them and sells them to a people who do not know God. [S1 Ep.7 p.65]
- Stokes: Coroticus was a Welsh prince (editor's view; others differ: to check). [S1 p.57-58 notes]

### Later witnesses
- Book of Armagh: written by the scribe Ferdomnach for Torbach, heir of Patrick, in 807 (Matthew gospel finished 21 Sept 807). [S2 Intro]
- Armagh contains Muirchú's memoir with its prologue, Tírechán's notes, the Confessio, the "Book of the Angel" and Dicta Patricii. [S2 Intro]
- Tírechán: bishop, foster-son of Bishop Ultán of Ardbraccan (d. 656), wrote from Ultán's dictation or book. He quotes the Confessio ("scriptio sua"). Stokes says he insists on a Roman mission; his two chronologies disagree. [S2 Intro, citing pp. 302-333]
- Muirchú (writing for Áed, bishop of Sleaty; "Áed bishop of the see of Sléibte") says earlier writers could not agree; he works "on uncertain authority, from an unreliable memory". (paraphrase of S3 prologue). He names Cogitosus as his predecessor in writing a Life (of Brigit).
- Muirchú's story: Patrick, born in Britain, father Cualfarnius, mother Concessa, home Bannavem Thaburniae "not far from our sea" (he places it at "Ventre"); captive at 16, six years' service; escaped at 23 (paraphrase). [S3 I 1]
- Muirchú: in Gaul he meets Germanus of Auxerre, stays "some say forty years, others thirty"; Victoricus visits him in visions; Germanus sends the priest Segitius with him. Palladius, archdeacon of Celestine, had been sent but gave up and died in Britain; Patrick was then consecrated by Bishop Amathorex. (paraphrase) [S3 I 6-9]
- Muirchú: Patrick lands at Inber Slaine; the swineherd of the pagan Díchu finds him; Díchu believes first; Patrick goes to buy himself free from his old master Miliucc, who, hearing his slave comes, burns himself and his goods in his house (paraphrase; LEGEND). Patrick sees it from the flank of Sliab Mis [S3 I 11-12; S2 Intro cxlii "an instance of... propitiatory self-sacrifice"]. SLEMISH = Sliab Mis. The angel Victor leaves a footprint on the rock of Scirit opposite Sliab Mis (LEGEND). [S3 I 12]
- Muirchú: First Easter (the Paschal fire): Patrick lights Easter fire on the plain of Brega on the night of a pagan feast at Tara where kings' decree forbade any fire to be lit before the royal fire at Tara. The druids tell the king that the fire "will never be extinguished at all" and will overpower his kingdom (paraphrase). Patrick and companions appear as eight deer and a fawn (legend). Easter Day: Patrick enters the banquet at Tara through closed doors; only Dubthach, a poet, rises; Erc son of Daig, whose relics are at Slane, stood up the night before. The druid Lochru is lifted and killed on a stone. (LEGEND). [S3 I 13-19] NOTE: Muirchú puts the fire on the plain of Brega, near Tara, not at the hill of Slane; the popular "fire on Slane" belongs to later retellings. To check which text first says Slane.
- Muirchú: Patrick wants to go to Armagh; Victor the angel stops him in a burning bush and sends him back to Saul. Four requests granted: (1) pre-eminence at Armagh; (2) judgment for those who say the hymn on his death; (3) mercy to Díchu's offspring; (4) the fourth request (to check). [S3 II 5-6]
- Death in Muirchú: he took the sacrament from bishop Tassach; angels kept vigil the first night and all human watchers slept; sweet scent as of honey; two untamed oxen carried the body and stopped at Dún Lethglaisse (Downpatrick), where he is buried; fire burst from the tomb when men dug; the Uí Néill and Airthir fought the Ulaid for the relics (paraphrase). [S3 II 9-13] So Muirchú puts the grave at Down, not Armagh. S2 Intro p. 357 gives 17 March as day. [S2]
- Tripartite Life: three Irish homilies, "a compilation of the ninth or tenth century" per Petrie; Stokes argues "not before the middle of the tenth century, probably the eleventh" (names a bishop slain 897, loseph d. 936). [S2 Intro lxiii]
- Tripartite: Patrick spent Lent (Shrove Saturday to Easter Saturday) on Cruachan Aigle (Croagh Patrick), "forty days and forty nights", in anger, without food; the mountain filled with black birds (not snakes); he rang his bell and flung it, breaking it; he wept; then angels brought white birds and he got concessions. Compared to Moses; "Six score years was the age of them both." (LEGEND). [S2 text, Cruachan passage; Stokes's intro cites fol. 74 b.2 dispute with the angel]
- The Tripartite sources: a marginal note says Patrick was a Briton on his father's side and a Frank on his mother's, sister of St Martin (a late note, LEGEND). [S2 Intro xvi]
- Snakes: Stokes prints a Rawlinson preface saying Ireland, like Paradise, has no snake or frog, "save only the wolf" (a natural-history claim, not by Patrick). [S2 Intro xxxi] The snake-banishing story is not yet found in Muirchú, Tripartite or Armagh: to check.
- Stokes's note: the Confessio says "oves/sheep" (his view), all Irish authorities say swine; he suspects Patrick wrote "sues". (S1 translates "cattle".) [S2 Intro cxxxiii note 4]

- Calls himself rustic, unlearned; worried about bad Latin (mentioned by S1 note on tongue; to be read). [S1 Conf.5]

- Defiant against slavery of his converts. [S1 Ep.]


## Dates, the Two Patricks, and the scholars
Extra sources: S6 Butler, Lives of the Saints, vol. 3, 17 March (PDF p.182ff; public-domain). S7 Wikipedia "Saint Patrick" (secondary; pointers only; check each claim in the books it cites). S8 T.M. Charles-Edwards, Kathleen Hughes Memorial Lecture 10, "Saint Patrick and the Landscape of Early Christian Ireland" (2012), https://www.asnc.cam.ac.uk/publications/Hughes/KH%20Vol%2010%202012%20Charles-Edwards.pdf (copyright: facts only). S9 History Press, "The solution to the 'Two St Patricks' theory" (popular; quotes Plunkett and Lehane). S10 St Patrick's Purgatory, Wikipedia (secondary).
- Traditional dates: Irish annals put Patrick's arrival at 432 and, in one entry, his death at 492/3 aged 120 on 17 March; 457 "Patrick the elder" and 461/2 are other entries. Annals compiled mid-6th c. at the earliest; 432 was probably chosen to cut Palladius's part (Palladius sent in 431 per Prosper). [S7 Dates, citing Dumville, Ó Cróinín, Byrne]. Stokes in 1887 listed "arrived in Ireland in 432... died 492 or 493, at about 120" among the traditions. [S2 Intro]
- Butler (1700s) printed 464 in his header, noting that Usher put Patrick's death at 493, Tillemont at about 455, and Nennius's count gives 464. [S6 p.174 footnote] Shows the old confusion.
- Confessio itself gives no year. Scholars: "general agreement" for fifth century; late-5th-c. death (c. 493) preferred by many Irish early historians; others accept c. 460 [S7]. The 553 annal says Colum Cille put Patrick's relics in a shrine "sixty years after his death". [S7]
- Dumville and others, *Saint Patrick, A.D. 493-1993* (Boydell, 1993): a volume that marks 493, not 461, as the death-year (a review notes it says 461 is "unsustainable"; the search snippet is secondary, check the review). Dumville also wrote on the date of the Tripartite Life, in that volume pp. 255-8 [S8 footnote]. 
- Charles-Edwards: as a search-result summary says, in *Early Christian Ireland* (2000) he joins scholars who place Patrick's mission in the later fifth century, and he reads Palladius's mission in its Roman setting from Prosper [secondary summary only; the book was not read; verify]. In S8 he says the Confessio was written in the fifth century and that Tírechán, writing in the late seventh century, "took serious liberties" but was right that Patrick was always on the move. [S8] Charles-Edwards also edited *The Chronicle of Ireland* (2006) [S8 refs].
- Binchy, "Patrick and his biographers: ancient and modern", *Studia Hibernica* 2 (1962) 7-173: a book-length critique; after it "those who wrote about Patrick were extremely careful in how they handled all sources later than the saint's own writings" [History Ireland review of Bury; see hi.txt]. Lehane's summary of Binchy: he held that a final answer was out of the question on the evidence. [S9]
- O'Rahilly, *The Two Patricks* (1942, Dublin Institute): Palladius was "Patrick" (the Patraic Sen, Patrick the elder, d. 457 in annals) with a mission 432-461; a second Patrick arrived 462 and died about 490. It caused "great scandal" (Plunkett). [S9] The Book of Armagh calls Palladius also Patrick. [S7]. Muirchú keeps Palladius and Patrick separate and puts Palladius's failure first. [S3 I 8]. Whitley Stokes notes a Patricius's obit on 16 March in the Roman martyrology and the Stowe Missal mentioning two Patricks. [S2 Intro cxiii note]. Most scholars today reject O'Rahilly's full theory but accept Palladius's earlier mission (S7: "not obviously a mission to convert").
- Columbanus (c. 613) wrote to Pope Boniface that the faith "was first handed to us by you", not naming Patrick. [S7, secondary]
- Tírechán c. late 7th c. (S8); Muirchú c. 700 (Ult/Sleaty patron Áed; date from CELT S3 not stated in text read; Tripartite dated by Stokes 10th-11th c., by Mulchrone, Mac Donncha, Jackson and Dumville each in studies on the date [S8 fn 26]; Charles-Edwards places Bethu Brigte in 9th c. and calls Tripartite later than it).
- Death: Muirchú ends with Patrick receiving the sacrament from Tassach, then burial at Dún Lethglaisse (Downpatrick) by the oxen; he does NOT say he died at Armagh. [S3 II 9-12] The trip to Saul is where the angel sends him back. The claim of death at Saul or burial at Down is later (S7 Places). Armagh claimed his primacy: angel says "your pre-eminence shall be in Armagh". [S3 II 6] Butler cites Nennius: "died and was buried at Down in Ulster". [S6]
- Armagh Book note: 17 March, "translated to heaven". [S1 p.61; S2 xciii]

## Legends: status check
- Slemish: Muirchú places Patrick's slavery near Sliab Mis and Miliucc (Dalaradia). The Confessio never names a hill. So "Slemish" is 7th c. tradition. Stokes's notes place him near Slemish and Broughshane (19th c. identification). Fochlut wood (Mayo) is where the Voice called from, so slavery may have been in the west (S7). [S1; S3; S7]
- Paschal fire on Slane: Muirchú has the fire on the plain of Brega with Tara the target; Erc's relics at Slane. Hill-of-Slane form appears in later retellings (S7 says so, no earlier text found by me). [S3 I 14-19; S7]
- Snakes: Ireland has none; Solinus (3rd c.) already noted this. First saint credited with banishing snakes is Columba in Adomnán's Life (late 7th/early 8th c.); Patrick and snakes first appear in Jocelin of Furness (late 12th c.); Gerald of Wales doubted it. No snakes in Muirchú, Tírechán, or Tripartite on Croagh Patrick (black birds). [S7, secondary; S2]. The Butler footnote (f) says the "popular tradition" credits his staff, the Staff of Jesus, in Dublin (mentioned 1360 by Higden). [S6]
- Shamrock: earliest written version in Caleb Threlkeld, 1726; earliest object is coins of the 1680s. [S7] Not in any early source.
- Croagh Patrick fast: Tírechán has 40 days on Cruachán Aigle like Moses. Tripartite (Shrove Saturday to Easter Saturday) has black birds, the broken bell, and Patrick's demands. The serpent Corra story is later. [S7; S2]
- St Patrick's Purgatory (Lough Derg): legend dated to 12th c.; earlier tradition is of Dabheog; the cave closed in 1497 and reopened (Butler says the pope closed it after "superstitious and false notions"). [S10; S6]
- Moving relics: found at Down in 1185 (Butler); Jocelin; S6 p.182.
- Patrick as Frank on his mother's side, nephew of Martin: marginal note, legend. [S2 xvi]. Butler repeats "Concessa niece to St Martin" as "some writers". [S6]
