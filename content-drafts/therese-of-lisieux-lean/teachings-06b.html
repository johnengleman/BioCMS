Thérèse of Lisieux taught that you do not have to become great to become holy. You have to let yourself be carried. She called it her little way, and she found it because the usual way was closed to her. The saints she read about stood on mountain peaks. She knew she was a grain of sand at the bottom.

Her teaching survives in three kinds of words, and they are not equally sure. The first is her own writing. The second is what she said aloud and others wrote down. The third is what was attached to her name later. Start with these four works:

  • Story of a Soul: her three manuscripts, written 1895–1897, printed in 1898 after her death.
  • Letters: 266 of them, from a child’s first lines to her last weeks.
  • Last Conversations: her sayings of 1897, kept by her sister Pauline in the Yellow Notebook.
  • Act of Offering to Merciful Love: the prayer she wrote on 9 June 1895.

Pauline edited the 1898 book and copied the notebook more than once, so the quotations below follow the manuscripts and the dated sayings. Be careful with lines that first appear long after her death. One priest placed her best-known saying about Heaven before she entered Carmel, and the editors of her sayings mark it with question marks.

The Lift to Heaven

What do you do when the staircase is too steep? Thérèse did not answer that you should train harder. She wanted to be a saint, and she meant it. But when she set herself beside the saints she had read about, she saw a mountain whose summit was lost in the sky, and at its foot an obscure grain of sand trodden underfoot. She could not grow bigger. God would not inspire desires that could never be fulfilled, she reasoned, so there had to be another way for a grain of sand: a little way, very straight and very short.

She put it on paper in June 1897, in a notebook her prioress had ordered her to fill:

“We live in an age of inventions… the lift that must raise me to Heaven is your arms, O Jesus!”

— Thérèse, Manuscript C 2v–3r, June 1897 (our translation)

The rich no longer climbed their stairs; they took a lift. In Scripture she had found God speaking of carrying His little ones as a mother does, and she knew what her lift was. To be carried she did not need to grow. She had to stay little, and become more so.

That was new against a holiness that seemed to belong to the strong. In 1923 Pope Pius XI called her way an easy way for every soul. Thérèse lived it to the end. On 30 September 1897, in pain in every muscle, she said to her prioress, “Mother, present me quickly to the Blessed Virgin.” Then she added, “I’m a baby who can’t go on.”

If you have given up on holiness because you are not strong enough, her answer is that strength was never the requirement.

My Vocation Is Love

In September 1896 Thérèse’s sister Marie asked her to write down her little way. Thérèse answered with a letter about desire. She wanted to be a warrior, a priest, an apostle, a martyr. She wanted to be a missionary on five continents, from the creation of the world. She was an enclosed nun of twenty-three, and she could do none of it.

Then, at prayer, she read St. Paul’s letter to the Corinthians, chapters twelve and thirteen. She found peace, and she wrote it down on 8 September, the sixth anniversary of her vows:

“My vocation, I’ve found it at last: my vocation is love!… In the heart of the Church, my Mother, I will be Love.”

— Thérèse, Manuscript B, 8 September 1896 (our translation)

Notice what she did not name. She named no task. She named a place, the heart of the Church, which no one sees from outside. It was a calling that a cloister could not shut in.

The Church later took the claim seriously. In 1927 Pope Pius XI made her patron of the missions, beside St. Francis Xavier, though she had lived inside a wall since she was fifteen.

Whatever your work is, she says, the part that no one can take from you is the love you put into it.

Fear Backs Away, Love Flies

At twelve Thérèse was ill with scruples, the torment of a conscience that sees sin in every thought. It lasted a year and a half. Every evening, while her sister Marie curled her hair, she wept and recited her faults. In Carmel the fear came back. One retreat preacher had called her wish to be a great saint rash, and sermons about how easily a soul could fall into mortal sin left her pale, unable to eat or sleep.

In October 1891 a Franciscan named Father Alexis came to preach the community retreat. He was meant for great sinners, and she was nearly the only sister who liked him. He told her that her faults did not grieve God, and she wrote that he launched her, full sail, on confidence and love.

“I’m made so that fear makes me back away; with love I don’t just go forward, I fly.”

— Thérèse, Manuscript A 80v, on the retreat of October 1891 (our translation)

She did not trust her own goodness. On 9 June 1895 she wrote the Offering of herself to God’s merciful love. In the evening of this life, it says, she would appear before God with empty hands, and she wanted to be clothed in His own justice. She carried the text on her heart for the rest of her life.

The sayings collected in Counsels and Reminiscences put the same idea in a line a child could remember: “We can never have too much confidence in the Good God, He is so mighty, so merciful.”

A Pin Picked Up for Love

Every evening at ten minutes to six, someone had to lead old Sister Saint-Pierre from the choir to the refectory, and Thérèse offered. The old nun was lame and hard to please. If Thérèse went too fast, she cried out that she would break. If she slowed down, the old woman said she could not feel her hand. Thérèse did it anyway, and in the end she won her with what she called her most beautiful smile.

She wanted martyrdom, and what Carmel gave her was an old woman’s hand to hold, ten minutes each evening. Her answer was that size does not decide the worth of an act. Love does.

“If you feel too lazy to pick up a bit of thread, and yet do so for love of Jesus, you acquire more merit than for a much nobler action done in a moment of fervour.”

— Thérèse, quoted in Counsels and Reminiscences, in Soeur Thérèse of Lisieux (tr. T. N. Taylor, 1912)

To her sister Léonie she wrote in May 1894, “Picking up a pin for love can convert a soul. What a mystery!…” (our translation). Her laundry gave her the proof. One day the sister working opposite her splashed her face with dirty water. Thérèse drew back and wiped it at first. Then it struck her that she was foolish to refuse such a gift, and she tried hard to want more of it.

Most of life is thread and pins. She says it counts.

The Little Ball

On 20 November 1887, in Rome, a fourteen-year-old girl knelt before Pope Leo XIII and begged him to let her enter Carmel at fifteen. He told her, “Come, come… You will enter if God wills it.” The guards lifted her away by the arms. She cried outside, and the letter she wrote to Pauline that night is plain and sad.

For some time before the pilgrimage she had been making a strange prayer to the Child Jesus. She had offered herself to Him as a toy:

“… to treat me like a little ball of no value, that could be thrown on the ground, kicked about, pierced, left in a corner, or pressed to His Heart just as it might please Him.”

— Thérèse, Story of a Soul, 1912 English translation, on her prayer before Rome

In that letter, LT 36, she told Pauline, “I am the Child Jesus’s little Ball; if he wants to break his toy, he is quite free.” (our translation) She was saying what many people find hardest to say when a plea goes unanswered: I am in God’s hands, and I do not need to see the plan.

The answer came from the bishop at the end of December, and then there was another delay until after Lent. On 9 April 1888 she walked through the door of the enclosure. The toy had been left in a corner for months. Then it was picked up.

Bitterness Turned to Joy

On 30 July 1897 Thérèse showed her sister Pauline a glass of foul medicine that looked like a fine currant liqueur. It was the image of her life, she said: people thought she drank exquisite liqueurs, and it was bitterness.

“This little glass is the image of my life… my life was not bitter, because I knew how to make my joy and my sweetness from all bitterness.”

— Thérèse, 30 July 1897, as recorded by Pauline in the Yellow Notebook (our translation)

She corrected herself, and the correction is the teaching. Suffering was real, and she did not dress it up. When the doctor praised her heroic patience, she protested that she moaned and cried out all the time. On 25 September she told Pauline, “it’s very easy to write fine things about suffering, but writing is nothing, nothing! You have to be in it to know!”

Some of her words are hard for a modern reader. On her profession day in 1890 she asked Jesus for martyrdom of the heart or the body, or both. In 1895 she offered herself as a victim to merciful love, grieved that His love was refused by so many. She wrote these words in an enclosed convent, in her own century.

Her father’s illness was the test. In 1889 he was taken to a mental hospital at Caen, and she wrote that his years of humiliation were among the most fruitful of their lives. Of his committal she wrote in her manuscript, “the most bitter, the most humiliating of all cups.” On her last evening, told that her agony might last a few hours more, she answered, “Oh, I would not want to suffer less.”

The Table of Sinners

In the Easter season of 1896, when others were glad, the thought of Heaven became a torment to her. She wrote that Jesus let her feel that there really are souls without faith. She described it for her prioress as a parable: someone born in a land of fog, who has only heard of a country full of sunlight. When she longed for that country, the darkness itself seemed to answer.

“You dream of light… Go on, go on, rejoice in death, which will give you not what you hope for but a night deeper still, the night of nothingness.”

— Thérèse, Manuscript C 6v, June 1897 (our translation)

It was no longer a veil, she wrote, but “a wall that reaches to the heavens and hides the starry sky.” When she wrote poems about Heaven, she felt no joy. She sang what she wanted to believe. She turned her back on the enemy without looking him in the face, and in that year she made more acts of faith than in all her life before. She carried the Creed on her heart.

What she understood in the end is the center of this teaching. She had been seated at the table of sinners, and she accepted to eat the bread of sorrow there for their sake. Almost no one knew. On 28 August 1897, looking at a black gap under the chestnut trees, she said, “that’s the kind of hole I’m in, body and soul. Ah yes, what darkness! But I’m at peace there.”

Doubt did not end her faith. It was where her faith was done.

A Heaven Spent on Earth

On 17 July 1897, after a night of coughing blood, Thérèse told Pauline what she felt was coming. She did not say she was going to rest.

“I feel I am going to enter into rest… But above all I feel that my mission is going to begin, my mission of making the good God loved as I love him, of giving my little way to souls.”

— Thérèse, 17 July 1897, as recorded by Pauline in the Yellow Notebook (our translation)

Then she gave her reason. “Yes, I want to spend my Heaven doing good on earth. It is not impossible, since even in the very bosom of the beatific vision the Angels watch over us.” The beatific vision is the sight of God face to face. Weeks before, when Marie said how sad the sisters would be, Thérèse had answered, “Oh no,” and then, “you’ll see, it will be like a shower of roses.”

The proof came early. In the summer of 1887 she set herself to save Henri Pranzini, a murderer who showed no sign of remorse. She had never met him. She gave her sister Céline a coin for a Mass and asked God for one sign. On the scaffold he kissed the crucifix three times, and she called him “my first child.” Long afterward she still asked for money on her feast days to have Masses said for him, whispering to Céline, “It is for my child.”

When Pauline painted the cross for her grave, she wrote her sister’s line on it: “I want to spend my Heaven doing good on earth.”

A Note on the Sources

Pauline edited Story of a Soul before it was printed in 1898. She recopied the Yellow Notebook in 1921–1923 from an earlier copy, and four versions exist. Where her manuscripts survive, the quotations here follow them in our own translation. A few come from the 1912 English translations of the edited book and of the sayings in Counsels and Reminiscences, as marked.
