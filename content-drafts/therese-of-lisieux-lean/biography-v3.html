In the summer of 1897, Sister Thérèse of the Child Jesus lay in her cell at the Carmel of Lisieux, resting after one of the painful cautery treatments the doctor had ordered for her lungs. It was the hour of recreation, and from the kitchen next door she could hear the sisters talking. One of them was talking about her. Sister Thérèse would soon die, the voice said, and what would the prioress find to write about her afterward? “This little Sister, amiable as she is, has certainly never done anything worth speaking about.”

By the world’s measure, it was true. She was twenty-four. Since entering the convent at fifteen, she had lived inside its walls. She had founded nothing and preached to no one, and her highest charge was helping with the novices. When the prioress wrote the customary letter about her after her death, there would seem to be very little to put in it.

Later that summer, at the worst of her illness, when all food had become unbearable to her, a lay sister brought her a cup of meat juice. Thérèse gently refused it, and the sister went back to the kitchen hurt. Sister Thérèse, she said, was not only no saint; she was not even a good nun. When the words reached the infirmary, Thérèse was delighted. “To hear on my deathbed that I am not a good religious,” she said, “what joy!” What she did object to was the charge that she had never known suffering. “And I, who have suffered so much since my earliest childhood!”

Twenty-eight years later, in St. Peter’s Basilica, Pope Pius XI declared her a saint. In 1997 the Church named her a Doctor of the Church, one of the small company of saints whose teaching it holds up to the whole world. The little sister who had done nothing worth speaking about became one of the most loved saints of modern times, and the story she wrote in cheap school notebooks has been translated into dozens of languages.

How did that happen? The answer is the whole of her life, and it begins with a contradiction. When she was about three, her sister offered the little ones a basket of doll clothes to choose from, and Thérèse took the whole basket. “I choose everything!” Years later she saw her soul in that moment. “My God, I choose everything,” she wrote. “I don’t want to be a saint by halves.” She wanted to be a great saint. Yet the longer she lived, the more clearly she saw how small she was: between the saints and herself, she wrote, lay the same distance as between a mountain whose summit is lost in the sky and an obscure grain of sand trodden underfoot.

Most people would have given up the dream. Thérèse gave up the climbing. God would not inspire desires that could never be fulfilled, she reasoned, and since she could not make herself bigger, she would look for another way to Him: a little way, very straight and very short. In the houses of the rich, she noticed, a lift now did the work of the stairs. “The lift that must raise me up to Heaven,” she wrote, “is your arms, O Jesus!”

That discovery, made in obscurity and lived out in a laundry, a refectory, and a sickroom, is why the sister in the kitchen was wrong, and why so many people have loved Thérèse ever since. It began in a watchmaker’s family in Normandy, with a father who had once climbed into the Alps and asked to leave the world.

Two Refused Vocations, One Marriage

Louis Martin was about twenty when he made his way up to the hospice of the Great St. Bernard, partly on foot and partly by coach, to ask the canons who kept it whether he might join them. The prior had one question for him. Had he finished his Latin? He had not, and he was sent home to finish it. He went back to Alençon and studied for more than a year under a parish priest, then fell ill and gave it up. In the end he settled in Alençon as a watchmaker, a young man who copied his favorite passages of literature into notebooks.

Zélie Guérin had knocked at a door of her own. Around 1850 she asked to join the Sisters of Charity who ran the hospital in Alençon, so that she could serve the sick and the poor, and the superior told her at once that God did not want her there. Zélie became a maker of the fine lace called point d’Alençon. She and Louis married in the church of Notre-Dame in Alençon at midnight on 13 July 1858, “without any parade,” with only the family and witnesses present.

Two people who had wanted the cloister for themselves now filled a house with children. There were nine of them, and four died young. One little boy, whose well-made hands made his mother dream of a future priest, died at eight months, and Zélie laid a crown of white roses on the small coffin. A baby girl was badly fed by a wet nurse, and ten-year-old Marie, the eldest, saw it, but the maid told her to keep quiet, and by the time she spoke up it was too late. People told Zélie it would have been better if the children had never been born. She would not accept it. Her pain, she wrote, did not compare with their happiness, and they would meet again in Heaven.

The last child was born in Alençon on 2 January 1873 and baptized two days later as Marie-Françoise-Thérèse. Within weeks she was dying. Zélie could not nurse her, and the baby showed the same alarming symptoms as the ones who had died. “Must I lose this one too?” her mother wrote in January. By 1 March she had no hope at all. The doctor said that the only chance was a healthy wet nurse. Louis was away. After a long night in which the baby would hardly drink, Zélie set out alone at daybreak for the village of Semallé, about eight kilometers off, to fetch a nurse named Rose Taillé. On a lonely stretch of road she met two men and thought, as she wrote afterward, that even if they killed her she did not care.

Rose would not leave her own children, but she took the baby home with her, and the baby lived. She spent her first year on a farm, riding on bundles of grass in a wheelbarrow. When she came home to the lace-maker’s house in Alençon, it was clear that the last of the Martin daughters had come back with a will of her own.

The Child Who Chose Everything

Zélie’s letters to Pauline, her second daughter, who was away at boarding school, are the best record of those years, and they are very frank. The little one, she wrote in December 1875, “flies into dreadful furies; when things don’t go her way she rolls on the floor in despair as if all were lost.” There were moments when it was stronger than she was. In the same letter the child, not yet three, caressed her mother and told her how much she wished she would die. When she was scolded, she explained that it was so her mother could go to Heaven, since Mama herself said you must die to go there.

She was clever and loving, and she was also immovable. When she said no, her mother wrote the next spring, nothing would make her give in: you could put her in the cellar for a whole day and she would sleep there rather than say yes. At night she kicked off the blankets and knocked her head on the wooden bed until she woke her parents crying that she was bumped, and in the end the family tied her into bed. Every evening little Céline, her playmate and nearest sister, came to knot the cords. Thérèse copied these letters into her own memoir twenty years later and added a remark to Pauline: “You see, Mother, how far I was from being a little girl without faults!”

This was the girl who took the whole basket of doll clothes. She meant it about everything. She counted her small sacrifices on a string of movable beads, and most of them were times she had given way to her sister, which cost her a great deal, because she was then very headstrong.

In December 1876 Zélie went to a doctor about a lump she had carried for years. He told her it was a fibrous tumor. She said that an operation would only shorten her life, and he agreed. When he offered a prescription, she asked what use it would be. “None,” he said. “It is to please the patients.” At home Louis could not be consoled. He gave up fishing, put his lines away in the attic, and stopped going to his circle of friends.

Zélie died on 28 August 1877, at half past midnight, aged forty-five. Before the body was taken away, Louis lifted his youngest daughter in his arms. “Come and kiss your poor little Mother one last time.” She put her lips to the cold forehead without a word. No one had time for her in those days, she wrote later, so she saw things they would have hidden from her, and she stood a long while looking at the lid of the coffin, which seemed very big.

After the funeral the five sisters stood together, and the maid said, “Poor little things, you have no mother any more!” Céline threw herself into Marie’s arms and said that Marie would be her mother now. Thérèse usually copied Céline, but this time she turned the other way. “Well, for me, Pauline will be Mama!” She had done it, she explained, because she thought Pauline might feel left out.

Something in her broke that year. From her mother’s death, she wrote, her happy character changed completely: “I, so lively, so outgoing, became timid and gentle, oversensitive. A look was enough to make me burst into tears.” That November Louis moved his daughters to Lisieux, to be near their uncle Isidore Guérin, the pharmacist, and settled them in a house with a garden called Les Buissonnets. The child who had chosen everything arrived there having lost, in her own words, the strength of mind she had been born with.

A Sword and a Smile

Pauline took the mother’s place, and she took it seriously. One summer day, when Thérèse came back very thirsty from a walk, Pauline suggested she offer her thirst to God for a sinner. That night Pauline brought her a drink in bed and told her that the sinner was surely saved by now, so she could drink. Thérèse hesitated, afraid of losing her sinner, and looked hard into Pauline’s eyes to see whether she was telling the truth.

Her temper had not gone, only gone underground. When the maid Victoire refused to hand down an inkwell and told her to climb on a chair for it, Thérèse climbed up, turned before jumping down, and said with great dignity, “Victoire, you’re a brat!” It was Victoire’s own word for her. Victoire shouted for Marie, and Thérèse was made to apologize, which she did, she admitted, “without contrition.”

At eight and a half she was sent as a day pupil to the school of the Benedictine abbey in Lisieux, and she hated it. The five years she spent there, she wrote, were the saddest of her life. She was put with girls of thirteen and fourteen and was often first in the class, and one of them, jealous, made her pay in a thousand ways for her little successes. She cried in silence and told no one. At recreation she leaned against a tree and watched, or buried dead birds under the chestnut trees, or told stories that went on for days until a teacher forbade what she called her trade of orator. At home in the evening she jumped onto her father’s knees, and his kiss made her forget.

Then, in the summer of 1882, she overheard Pauline telling Marie that she was going to enter the Carmelite convent in Lisieux. The Carmelites are enclosed nuns who live in silence and prayer, and a family sees a sister afterward only through a grille. It was, Thérèse wrote, as if a sword had been driven into her heart. In an instant she understood what life was: suffering and continual separation. Pauline explained Carmel to her gently, and something else happened in the child as she listened. She felt certain that Carmel was the desert where God wanted her to hide as well, and that she would go there for Jesus alone.

Pauline entered on 2 October 1882. On visiting days Thérèse had two or three minutes at the grille at the end of the family’s time, and she spent them crying. One day she said, “Look, Pauline, I have on the little petticoat you made me.” Nobody paid attention, and afterward Marie saw the tears in her eyes and later regretted that she had not guessed how much the child was suffering. Thérèse said to herself that Pauline was lost to her.

That winter she had headaches. At Easter 1883, while Louis was in Paris with Marie and Léonie, Thérèse and Céline stayed with the Guérins, and one evening her uncle spoke to her about her mother. She cried, and that night, while she was undressing, a strange trembling seized her and would not stop. For weeks she was gravely ill in a way no one could name. She had terrors. The nails on the wall looked to her like huge charred fingers. When her father came to her bed holding his hat, she cried out at the big black beast, and he left the room in tears, saying that his poor little girl was mad. The doctor, Dr. Notta, tried showers every day and admitted he understood nothing. “Call it what you like,” he said, as Marie later reported, “but for me it is not hysteria.” The family believed that the devil was at work, and the Church later recorded their belief.

Louis gave Marie gold coins to have a novena of Masses said at Notre-Dame des Victoires in Paris, a church much visited by people praying to the Virgin Mary. On a Sunday during the novena, Marie went out into the garden and left Thérèse with Léonie, who was reading by the window. Thérèse began to call, “Mama… Mama…”, louder and louder. Marie came back, and Thérèse did not know her. Marie, Léonie, and Céline knelt beside the bed and turned toward a statue of the Virgin that had stood in the family for years. Thérèse turned toward it too, and prayed.

“All at once the Blessed Virgin seemed beautiful to me, so beautiful that I had never seen anything so beautiful.”

— Thérèse, Manuscript A (her childhood memoir), written 1895, on 13 May 1883

What went to the depth of her soul was the Virgin’s smile. Two big tears ran silently down her cheeks. She looked down and saw Marie watching her, and Marie thought, “Thérèse is cured!” The healing came gently, she wrote, and not all at once. Later, Marie testified, she asked the child why she had cried, and after some hesitation Thérèse told her: it was because she could not see the Virgin any more.

She had meant to tell no one, for fear that her happiness would disappear. Marie guessed, and asked leave to tell the nuns at the Carmel, and in the parlour the sisters questioned the ten-year-old closely. Did the Virgin carry the Child Jesus? Was there a great deal of light? All she could say was that the Virgin had seemed very beautiful and had smiled at her. Seeing that they imagined something grander, she began to think she had lied. For four years the memory of the smile was a real pain to her soul, and long after that she believed she might have made herself ill on purpose. She told Marie and her confessor, and both tried to calm her, but the grace that should have been her joy had become a weight that neither of them could lift.

I Cried for Having Cried

She made her First Communion at the abbey on 8 May 1884. For three months she had prepared with a little book Pauline had made for her, and when the day came it was, she wrote, a kiss of love. She felt loved. “Thérèse had disappeared, like a drop of water lost in the ocean.” She cried, and the other girls thought she was crying for her dead mother or for her sister in the Carmel. They did not understand that all the joy of Heaven had come into one heart.

She wanted more of it. Around that time she asked Marie, who now ran the house, for half an hour of mental prayer every day, and then for a quarter of an hour. Marie later swore that she refused both, because the child was so devout that it frightened her and she feared God would take her too soon. Céline remembered that Marie allowed the quarter of an hour. Either way, on free afternoons Thérèse slipped behind the curtains of her bed, and when a teacher asked her what she did there alone, she answered, “I think about God, about life, about eternity. I just think!”

In May 1885, during a school retreat, she fell into what she called the terrible illness of scruples, and it lasted a year and a half. Every thought and every simple action troubled her. She found rest only by telling everything to Marie, and every evening, while Marie curled her hair so that she would look nice for their father, she cried and recited her sins. On the eve of confession Marie let her confess two or three of them and no more. Thérèse obeyed so exactly that her confessor never knew of her illness.

In October 1886 Marie entered the Carmel too. Only Céline and Thérèse were left at home with their father, and there was no one now to take her scruples. In desperation she prayed to the four little brothers and sisters who had died before she was born, and asked them to show that up there they still knew how to love. Peace came, and she understood, she wrote, that if she was loved on earth, she was loved in Heaven as well.

The tears did not stop. She cried over everything, and when she had stopped crying over the thing itself, she wrote, she cried for having cried. She was nearly fourteen, and she was still a child who had to be comforted out of her own sorrow.

In the Martin house, Christmas still ended the way it had when Thérèse was small. After midnight Mass the family came home, and the youngest daughter went to the chimney, where her shoes waited with presents inside, and the whole family enjoyed watching her open them. In 1886 that youngest daughter was nearly fourteen.

Her father was tired. As she climbed the stairs to take off her hat, she heard him below, looking at the shoes: “Enfin, heureusement que c’est la dernière année!” Well, luckily this is the last year.

It was a small, weary remark, the kind any father makes. For Thérèse it should have been a disaster. For years any cross word had reduced her to tears, and the tears to more tears, until someone had to comfort her out of them. Céline, following her up, saw her eyes fill and knew exactly what would happen next. “Oh, Thérèse, don’t go down,” she said. “It would hurt you too much to look in your shoes right away.”

Thérèse went down anyway. She went to the chimney and joyfully pulled out every present, “looking as happy as a queen,” she wrote years later. Her father laughed with her. Céline thought she must be dreaming. Only Thérèse knew what had happened. The work she had not managed in ten years, she wrote, Jesus did in one instant.

She called that night her complete conversion. The strength of mind she had lost at four and a half came back to her, and it did not leave again. For ten years she had been turned inward on her own hurts. Now she began to look outward, and within months she found someone to look at, in a newspaper her father had forbidden her to read.

A Fourteen-Year-Old Before the Pope

In the summer of 1887 everyone was talking about Henri Pranzini, who had murdered two women and a girl in Paris and showed no sign of remorse. Thérèse decided to save him. She did not dare to ask for a Mass herself, for fear of having to say whom it was for, so she gave Céline a coin and asked her to arrange it, blushing, without naming the man. Céline guessed, and joined her. Thérèse told God that she was sure He would forgive Pranzini even without a confession, but she asked for one sign, for her own consolation. Her father did not let his daughters read the papers. She told herself she was not disobeying, and she began to look for them.

Pranzini was guillotined on 31 August. The next day she opened La Croix. He had not confessed, the paper said, but on the scaffold he had turned, taken the crucifix the priest held out, and kissed the wounds three times. Thérèse had to hide to cry. The lips of “my first child,” as she called him, had pressed themselves to the sacred wounds. Years later a prison chaplain told the Church’s court, on the word of the priest at the scaffold, that Pranzini’s arms had been tied behind his back, so he could not have taken the crucifix. He had asked for it, insistently, and kissed it. Long afterward she still asked for money on her feast days to have Masses said for him, whispering to Céline, “It is for my child.”

She had already made up her mind about Carmel, and on Pentecost Sunday, 29 May 1887, after praying to the Apostles all day, she told her father. She found him after Vespers sitting in the garden and sat down beside him in tears. “What is it, my little queen?” he said, and drew her to his heart. “Tell me.” He wept too, and said she was very young. Then he listened, and said that God did him a great honor in asking for his children. He picked a tiny white flower from a low wall, roots and all, and explained how God had made it grow. She pressed it in her copy of the Imitation of Christ, and she still had it when she wrote her memoir eight years later.

Her uncle thought a miracle would be needed, and then gave way. The priest in charge of the Carmel, Canon Delatroëtte, would not hear of a girl of fourteen. On 31 October Louis took her to Bayeux to see the bishop. She put her hair up to look older. It poured, and in the bishop’s house she felt as small as an ant. She was told to sit in an armchair so huge that four girls like her could have fitted in it. When the vicar general saw tears starting in her eyes, he said, “Ah, I see diamonds. You must not show them to Monseigneur.” She did more than show them, she wrote: she gave them. The bishop suggested that she stay with her father a few more years, was surprised to find the father on her side, and said he must consult the Carmel’s superior. Louis told him they would be going to Rome.

They went in November, with a pilgrimage from the diocese. On the way, in Paris, she knelt in Notre-Dame des Victoires, where the Masses for her cure had been said, and there the fear that she had lied about the smile left her. Rome brought out her boldness. At the Colosseum the arena was closed off by a barrier. Thérèse found a place where the rubble reached the top, called to Céline, “Come quick, we can get through!”, and the two girls scrambled down to kiss the ground where the martyrs had died, while their astonished father called after them. He could not scold them afterward, she wrote; he was proud of them. At the church of Santa Croce she slipped her little finger through an opening in a reliquary to touch a nail of the Cross. “I was really far too bold!”

The audience with Pope Leo XIII took place on Sunday, 20 November 1887. In the account she wrote in 1895, the vicar general announced that no one was to speak to the Holy Father. Thérèse looked at Céline, who said, “Speak!” When her turn came, instead of kissing his hand she joined her own. “Most Holy Father, in honor of your jubilee, allow me to enter Carmel at fifteen!” The vicar general explained that the superiors were considering it. “Well, my child,” said the Pope, “do what the superiors tell you.” She rested her hands on his knees and tried once more. “Oh, Holy Father, if you said yes, everyone would agree!” He looked at her and said, stressing each syllable, “Come, come… You will enter if God wills it.” In that same account, the guards and the vicar general had to lift her by the arms and carry her almost to the door, and the Pope laid his hand on her lips and then blessed her.

The letter she wrote to Pauline that day is plainer and sadder. The good Pope was so old, she wrote, that you would think he was dead; the vicar general did the talking. She felt crushed, and abandoned, and she hid the letter from her father. For some time before the pilgrimage she had been making a strange prayer to the Child Jesus. She had offered herself to Him as a toy.

“… to treat me like a little ball of no value, that could be thrown on the ground, kicked about, pierced, left in a corner, or pressed to His Heart just as it might please Him.”

— Thérèse, Story of a Soul, 1912 English translation, on her prayer to the Child Jesus before Rome

In that letter she told Pauline that she was the little ball of the Child Jesus, and that if He wanted to break His toy, He was quite free to do it. Now the toy had been left in a corner. On the long road home she had half an answer, and no one knew whether the other half would ever come.

Now I Am Here Forever

It came from the bishop at the end of December, and then there was another delay, until after Lent. Pauline, already a nun inside, admitted years later that she herself had asked for it. On 9 April 1888, the feast of the Annunciation, moved that year from its usual day, the family went to Mass together for the last time. At Communion Thérèse heard nothing around her but the sound of weeping. She shed no tears. She walked ahead of them all to the door of the enclosure, her heart beating so violently that she wondered whether she was going to die. There she knelt for her father’s blessing, and he knelt too, and blessed her through his tears.

The door stood open and Louis was there when Canon Delatroëtte made his little speech to the nuns. Well, he said, they could now sing a Te Deum. As the bishop’s delegate he was presenting to them this child of fifteen whose entrance they had wanted. He hoped she would not disappoint them, but if she did, the responsibility would be theirs alone. The community was chilled. Thérèse was led into the dark choir, where she first met the eyes of the old foundress, Mother Geneviève, and then to her cell. “Now I am here forever,” she said to herself.

The prioress, Mother Marie de Gonzague, was used to being flattered, Marie said, and she did not flatter Thérèse. In her hours of direction the postulant was scolded almost the whole time. When Pauline complained that her little sister was being humiliated, the prioress answered, as Pauline later testified, that the girl was much prouder than Pauline thought and needed constant humbling. The novice mistress ordered Thérèse to report every stomach ache. She had one every day, and each report was torture to her. Soon a superior was saying that the child did nothing but complain, and that people who could not bear their ills should leave. Thérèse kept reporting, at the risk of being sent away, until someone thought to release her from the order. Several times she went to the Office in such pain that she thought she would faint, and told herself, “If I fall, they’ll see it!”

Outside the walls, her father was failing. He had already had a stroke, and on 23 June 1888 he left the house without a word to anyone. Four days later her uncle and Céline found him at the post office in Le Havre, with his beard shaved off, talking of solitude. Thérèse would not write about that June. The memories, she said, were too well engraved.

He was well enough to come to her clothing, the ceremony in which she received the habit, on 10 January 1889. At his wish she walked into the chapel on his arm in a dress of white velvet trimmed with Alençon lace, the lace of her mother’s trade, her long fair hair in natural curls. He met her at the enclosure door with tears in his eyes and pressed her to his heart. “Ah, here is my little Queen!” It was his last feast, she wrote. She had always wanted snow on the day she was clothed, and the morning was mild and grey with a fine rain. When she went back into the cloister, snow was falling, and the courtyard was white like her.

A month later, on 12 February 1889, Louis Martin was taken to the Bon-Sauveur, a mental hospital at Caen. In his mind, Céline wrote a few days later, he saw carnage and battles and heard the cannon and the drum. He had started carrying a revolver in case of danger, though he would not have harmed a cat, and the family had him committed. In June lawyers came to the hospital to have him sign the papers that took away his legal rights. They told him it was at his children’s request. He wept, said his children were abandoning him, and signed. In the town, people called him “le saint Patriarche.” At the Carmel, Pauline later said, the prioress talked at recreation, in front of his daughters, about what madmen did and about straitjackets.

Thérèse was sixteen. Pauline testified that she did not come to her older sisters for comfort; they came to her. In the list of the great graces of her life she wrote the date of her father’s committal and gave it a name: “Our great riches, 12 February 1889.” Of his three years of humiliation she wrote later that they were the most lovable and the most fruitful of their whole lives, and that she would not give them for all the ecstasies of the saints.

She made her vows, her profession, on 8 September 1890. On the eve her vocation suddenly seemed to her a dream, an illusion. On the day itself, in great peace, she carried a note on her heart. In it she asked Jesus to take her before she committed the smallest deliberate fault. She asked Him for love without limit other than Himself, and for martyrdom of the heart or of the body, or both. And she asked that no one bother about her, that she be “trodden underfoot, forgotten, like a little grain of sand of yours, Jesus.” When she lay face down on the floor, she was told to pray for her father’s cure. All she could manage was to ask God that it be His will that Papa be cured.

The public veiling followed on 24 September, when she received the black veil. The family had hoped Louis would be well enough to come to the grille, and at the last moment it was decided that the emotion would be too much for him. Even the bishop who was to preside was ill and did not come.

“On the day of my wedding I was truly an orphan, having no Father on earth any more but able to look to Heaven with confidence and say in all truth: ‘Our Father who art in Heaven.’” … “That day Jesus let me be unable to hold back my tears, and my tears were not understood.”

— Thérèse, Manuscript A, on her veiling, 24 September 1890

The girl who had chosen everything had now given everything she could think of. What was left was ordinary convent life, day after day, among women she had not chosen. It was there, and nowhere grander, that she would find out what her vows meant.

A Grain of Sand Lifted

An old lay sister named Sister Saint-Pierre could walk only with crutches, and she was hard to please. Every evening at ten minutes to six someone had to lead her from the choir to the refectory, and Thérèse offered. Sister Saint-Pierre would shake her hourglass to mean it was time. Her bench had to be moved in a certain way, and she had to be held by the belt in a certain way. “Ah, my God! You’re going too fast, I’ll break!” If Thérèse slowed down, it was worse: “But follow me! I can’t feel your hand, you’ve let go of me, I’m going to fall. Ah, I said you were too young to lead me.” At table Thérèse turned up the sleeves in the required way and, without being asked, cut the old nun’s bread, and she won her over in the end with what she called her most beautiful smile. One winter evening, in the brick cloister, she heard distant music and imagined a lighted drawing room full of elegant girls. Then she looked at the moaning old nun beside her and decided that she would not give those ten minutes for a thousand years of such parties.

One day in the laundry the sister working opposite her splashed her face with dirty water. Thérèse drew back and wiped it at first. Then it struck her that she was foolish to refuse such a gift, and she tried hard to want more of it. There was another sister, never named, who had a knack for getting on her nerves in everything. Thérèse prayed for her whenever they met and gave her the loveliest smile she had, and when the struggle became too hard, she simply fled. The sister once asked her what it was about her that attracted Thérèse so much. Céline, who entered later, was so taken in that she thought this sister was Thérèse’s best friend.

She did not always manage even that much. Once, while the prioress was ill, Thérèse went to return the keys of the grille, and a zealous sister senior to her tried to take them from her so as not to wake the prioress. Thérèse would not give them up. The door was pushed, the noise woke the prioress, and the other sister made a whole speech about how disagreeable Sister Thérèse was. Thérèse knew she did not have the virtue to be accused in silence, so she fled “without drum or trumpet” and sat on the stairs, her heart pounding, to enjoy the fruits of her victory. “It was not bravery, was it, Mother?”

When the old foundress, Mother Geneviève, died in December 1891, Thérèse arranged the wreaths around the coffin. A lay sister, Sister Saint-Vincent de Paul, watched her and remarked that she knew how to put her own family’s wreaths in front and the poor people’s bouquets behind. Thérèse thanked her and said she was right. Then she asked for the moss cross the workmen had sent and put it in front. Pauline heard the exchange herself. After Thérèse’s death, the sisters said later, Sister Saint-Vincent de Paul told the story to every nun in the house.

Inside, she was struggling with fear. She had once told a retreat preacher that she wanted to become a great saint, and he had found her very rash. Sermons about how easily a soul could fall into mortal sin left her pale, unable to eat or sleep. At the community retreat of October 1891 she went through trials so deep that she sometimes wondered, she wrote, whether there was a Heaven at all. The preacher that year, a Franciscan named Father Alexis, was meant for great sinners, and she was nearly the only one who liked him. He told her that her faults did not grieve God. He launched her, she wrote, full sail on confidence and love.

“I’m made so that fear makes me back away; with love I don’t just go forward, I fly.”

— Thérèse, Manuscript A, on the retreat of October 1891

In February 1893 Pauline, now Mother Agnès of Jesus, was elected prioress. Custom bound her to name Mother Marie de Gonzague novice mistress, but in reality, she said later, she was counting on her twenty-year-old sister. Thérèse was given the novices without the title. Even before this, she had shown what she would do with the task. On 8 December 1892 she took a fellow novice aside, laid the older woman’s head on her heart, and told her that her love for the prioress was too human, a kind of poison. She told the novice she would rather be sent away from the monastery than fail in her duty.

Her father had come home to Lisieux from Caen in May 1892, carried into the carriage, weeping the whole way. Two days later he was brought to the Carmel parlour. At the moment of parting he raised his eyes and pointed upward: “In Heaven!” He died on 29 July 1894 at La Musse, the country house where her uncle had taken him for the summer, with Céline beside him. “Papa is in Heaven,” she wrote to her sisters. “I received his last breath; I closed his eyes.” Six weeks later Céline entered the Carmel herself, and over the next three years she photographed Thérèse in the cloister.

It was in these years, around her father’s death, that Thérèse found what she was looking for. She still wanted to be a saint; she had never stopped. But when she compared herself with the saints, she saw a mountain whose summit was lost in the sky, and herself as an obscure grain of sand trodden underfoot. She did not conclude that she should give up. God would not inspire desires that could never be fulfilled, so there must be a way for a grain of sand. To grow bigger was impossible. She would have to bear with herself as she was and look for a little way, very straight and very short, a way that was completely new. The rich no longer climbed their stairs; they took a lift. In Scripture she found God speaking of carrying His little ones like a mother, and she knew what her lift was. It was the arms of Jesus, and to be carried she did not need to grow. On the contrary, she had to stay little, and become more so.

In her profession note she had asked to be forgotten like a grain of sand. Now she saw that the grain of sand could be lifted. Soon she would be ordered to write it all down.

Memories in Two-Sou Notebooks

One winter evening in 1894–1895, Thérèse told her sisters stories from their childhood. Marie urged Pauline, as prioress, to order her to write them down, and Pauline did. Thérèse wrote in her free time, in snatches, with no draft, in cheap two-sou school notebooks of the worst paper. The memoir she produced, Manuscript A, is the first of the three texts that later became Story of a Soul. On 20 January 1896, at evening prayer, she knelt as she passed Pauline in the choir and handed it to her. Pauline nodded, put the notebook down, and did not open it for two months. She could not remember Thérèse mentioning it once.

Months before, on Trinity Sunday, 9 June 1895, during Mass, she had felt urged to offer herself as a victim to God’s merciful love, grieved that His love was refused by so many. After Mass she drew Céline along with her, silent, to find the prioress. Pauline was in a hurry. She did not seem to understand what it was about, Céline said, and allowed everything; Pauline herself admitted that she agreed “without enthusiasm.” Two days later the two sisters knelt before the statue of the Virgin who had smiled in 1883, and Thérèse read the Offering she had written. In the evening of this life, it says, she would appear before God with empty hands, and she wanted to be clothed in His own justice. She carried the text on her heart for the rest of her life.

Her novices found her anything but solemn. When one novice began to cry, Thérèse took a mussel shell from her painting table. She held the girl’s hands so that she could not wipe her eyes and caught the tears in the shell, until the novice laughed. When another knelt in front of her and said that one day people would pray to “Saint Thérèse of the Child Jesus,” she answered, “What a child you are! That’s enough of making fun of me!” At Christmas 1896 she hid a spinning top in a novice’s cell with a letter from the Child Jesus, asking the girl to be His top, which needs whipping to spin. That novice later warned that it was a game made for one soul, not a rule for everyone.

By that Christmas she had a graver reason to feel small. Eight months earlier, on the night of Holy Thursday, her body had given her the first sign that she would not live long, and she had greeted it with joy.

Blood on Good Friday

She had kept the Lenten fast of 1896 in all its rigor and had felt strong. On the night of Holy Thursday, 2 April, after keeping watch before the altar of repose until midnight, she went back to her cell. As soon as she lay down she felt something like a stream rising, rising, bubbling up to her lips. Her lamp was out, and she decided not to light it to look. She thought that she might be dying, and her soul was flooded with joy. At dawn she went to the window and saw that she was right. It was blood, and it came on the anniversary of Christ’s death, like a first call from Jesus. At the chapter that morning she told the prioress, adding that she felt no pain and needed nothing. That Good Friday a novice found her washing windows between the long services, pale as death, living on bread and water, and could not persuade her to stop. The next night the blood came again.

Then something far worse happened. In the joyful days of the Easter season, she wrote, Jesus let her feel that there really are souls without faith. Her soul was invaded by the thickest darkness, and the thought of Heaven, which had been her joy since childhood, became nothing but a subject of combat and torment. She described it for her prioress as a parable. Imagine someone born in a land of fog, who has only heard of a country full of sunlight. Now, when she longed for that country, the darkness itself seemed to speak to her.

“You dream of light… Go on, go on, rejoice in death, which will give you not what you hope for but a night deeper still, the night of nothingness.”

— Thérèse, Manuscript C, written June 1897, on the trial of faith that began at Easter 1896

She did not want to write more, she said, for fear of blaspheming. It was no longer a veil, but a wall that rose to the heavens and hid the starry sky. When she wrote poems about Heaven, she felt no joy; she sang, she said, simply what she wanted to believe. She turned her back on the enemy without looking him in the face. In that year she made more acts of faith than in all her life before, and she told Jesus she was ready to shed her blood to confess that there is a Heaven. She carried the Creed on her heart, and her sister Pauline said she had written it in her own blood. In the end she understood the darkness as a place at a table. She had been seated, she wrote, at the table of sinners, and she accepted to eat the bread of sorrow there for their sake.

Almost no one knew. She told her confessors, and one of them told her that her state was very dangerous, which did not help. Marie learned of the trial only by reading the manuscript after Thérèse died. Early in 1897 a sister told her about a dream of a black door. Thérèse said the dark room was the image of her own darkness, and she told that sister of her trial. The sister later testified to words she heard: “I don’t believe in eternal life; it seems to me that after this mortal life there is nothing more.” Céline heard her ask, anxiously, “Is there a Heaven? Talk to me about Heaven.”

In September 1896, five months into the darkness, Marie asked her to write down her little way. Thérèse answered with a letter about desire. She wanted to be a warrior, a priest, an apostle, a martyr. She wanted to be a missionary on five continents, from the creation of the world. Every kind of martyrdom drew her, and like Joan of Arc she wanted to murmur the name of Jesus at the stake. Then at prayer she read St. Paul’s letter to the Corinthians, chapters twelve and thirteen, and found peace.

“My vocation, I’ve found it at last: my vocation is love!… In the heart of the Church, my Mother, I will be Love.”

— Thérèse, Manuscript B, 8 September 1896

In the note that went with it she warned Marie not to be misled. “Don’t think I’m swimming in consolations; oh no! My consolation is to have none on earth.” She pictured herself as a weak little bird covered only in down, with an eagle’s eyes and heart. It gets distracted, pecks at a seed, chases a worm, and falls asleep when the clouds hide the sun, still believing it is gazing at its star. The bird had only one year left to fly.

The Slow Last Summer

By the spring of 1897 the whole community could see that she was ill, and Thérèse joked about it. “I’m coughing! I’m coughing!” she said in May. “It’s like a railway engine pulling into the station. I’m arriving at a station too: the station of Heaven, and I’m announcing it!” When she found out that the syrup they were giving her was made from snails, she laughed: “What do I care about taking snail syrup, as long as I don’t see the horns!” Pauline had started writing down what she said, and the notes, later copied into a yellow notebook, are the source of most of her last words.

At the beginning of June the prioress, Mother Marie de Gonzague, who had been re-elected the year before, ordered her to finish the story of her religious life. Thérèse wrote in the garden, in a black notebook, telling the prioress that she would not mind if it were burned before her eyes unread. She was interrupted constantly. A sister passed with a pitchfork on her shoulder and stopped to chat about hay, ducks, hens, and the doctor’s visit. Another dropped flowers on her knees, perhaps to inspire poetic ideas. “My poor little sister,” said a third, “it must wear you out, writing like that all day long.”

On 7 June, walking in the garden on Pauline’s arm, she stopped to watch a little white hen gathering her chicks under her wings, and her eyes filled with tears. She could not say why at the time. That evening she explained. She had cried to think that God chose this image to make us believe in His tenderness. “All my life that’s what He has done for me! He has hidden me entirely under His wings!” Two days later Marie said how sad they would be when she left them. “Oh no,” Thérèse answered, “you’ll see, it will be like a shower of roses.”

On 8 July she was carried to the infirmary, and around the same days her notebook broke off, in pencil. The next day the superior of the Carmel, Canon Maupas, came to decide whether she should be anointed, and he found her so pleasant and smiling that he said she was not ill enough. Afterward she told the young sisters what she would do next time: she would drink a cup of milk before he came, because it always made her look worse, and she would hardly answer him. “I see that I don’t know the trade,” she said.

Something else was growing in her as she weakened. On 17 July, after coughing blood in the night, she told Pauline what she felt was coming.

“But above all I feel that my mission is going to begin, my mission of making the good God loved as I love Him, of giving my little way to souls.”

— Thérèse, 17 July 1897, as recorded by Pauline

If God granted her desires, she added, her Heaven would be spent on earth until the end of the world. “Yes, I want to spend my Heaven doing good on earth.” Two days later, when Pauline teased her at recreation and asked what she was, she said: “I am a little seed. No one knows yet what will come out of it.”

She kept her novices. One afternoon a novice saw her alone in her invalid carriage under the chestnut trees and, having no permission to speak to her, went off to cry in a hermitage nearby. When she looked up, Thérèse was sitting beside her on a tree stump, trembling with fever. “I’m not forbidden to come to you,” she said. “Even if I die of it, I want to console you.” She would not go back until the girl laughed. On 30 July she received the last sacraments. That day she showed Pauline a glass of foul medicine that looked like a fine currant liqueur. It was the image of her life, she said: people always thought she was drinking exquisite liqueurs, and it was bitterness. Then she corrected herself. Her life had not been bitter, because she had learned to make joy out of every bitterness.

In August the pain grew terrible. On the 28th, with her bed turned toward the window, she pointed out a spot under the chestnut trees near the cemetery where nothing could be seen.

“That’s the kind of hole I’m in, body and soul. Ah yes, what darkness! But I’m at peace there.”

— Thérèse, 28 August 1897, as recorded by Pauline

On the 30th they wheeled her on a folding bed to the door of the choir, where she threw rose petals at the grille, and she was photographed before she was brought back. When the doctor praised her heroic patience, she protested that it was a lie: she moaned and sighed and cried out all the time. On 22 September someone said to her, “What a terrible illness, and how much you’ve suffered!” She answered, “Yes! What a grace to have faith! If I had not had faith, I would have taken my own life without hesitating for a moment.” In those days she told Pauline that it was easy to write beautiful things about suffering, but writing was nothing, nothing; you had to be in it to know. On 25 September, as if she had been thinking it over, she said she felt now that everything she had said and written was true.

My God, I Love You

On 29 September the death rattle began. At noon she asked the prioress, “Mother, is this the agony? How shall I die? I’ll never know how to die!” When Pauline read the prayers for the dying and came to the mention of demons, Thérèse made a childish threatening gesture and smiled. After the doctor’s visit she asked, “Is it today, Mother?” “Yes, my little girl,” the prioress said. One of the sisters added that God was joyful. “Me too!” said Thérèse. Late that night she joined her hands: “Yes, my God, yes, my God, I accept everything!”

On the morning of 30 September, alone with Pauline during Mass, she looked at the statue of the Virgin who had smiled at her as a child. “Oh, I prayed to her with such fervor! But it is pure agony, without any mixture of consolation.” At three in the afternoon, in pain in every muscle, she put one arm on Pauline’s shoulder and the other on Céline’s, and stayed there with her arms stretched out like a cross while the clock struck three. The prioress laid a picture of Our Lady of Mount Carmel on her knees. “Mother, present me quickly to the Blessed Virgin,” Thérèse said. “I’m a baby who can’t go on.” Told that her humility was her preparation, she answered that it seemed to her she had never sought anything but the truth. “And I don’t repent of having given myself to Love.”

At about five the community was called. For more than two hours a terrible rattle tore at her chest. Her face and hands turned a reddish purple, sweat soaked the pillows, and outside the window, Pauline noted, the birds sang so loudly that she prayed they would stop. Céline put a piece of ice to her lips, and Thérèse smiled at her as if to say goodbye. A little after seven the prioress, thinking the end still some hours off, sent the sisters away. “Mother,” Thérèse asked, “isn’t this the agony yet? Am I not going to die?” “Yes, my poor little one, it is the agony, but perhaps God wants to prolong it a few hours.” “Well, come on, come on,” she answered. “Oh, I would not want to suffer less.” She looked at her crucifix.

“Oh! I love Him!… My God… I love You!”

— Thérèse’s last words, 30 September 1897, as recorded by Pauline

She fell back gently, her head leaning to the right. The prioress had the bell rung and said, “Open all the doors.” The sisters had hardly knelt around the bed when Thérèse raised her head and fixed her eyes a little above the statue of the Virgin, for about the time it takes to say the Creed. Her face, purple a moment before, had become pale as a lily. By Pauline’s account, her cousin Marie Guérin held a candle close to her eyes, and the lids did not move. Then she closed them, sighed several times, and died, at about twenty past seven. When the sisters laid her out, they thought she looked no more than twelve or thirteen years old.

A Book Instead of an Obituary

She was buried on 4 October 1897 in the town cemetery of Lisieux. Céline called the procession very small, while Marie and another sister remembered a large crowd. The dean of the parish of Saint-Pierre remarked that here was a burial where nobody wept. Pauline painted a wooden cross for the grave. When a workman carrying it smudged the wet paint, she replaced the line she had first written with one she had not dared to use: “I want to spend my Heaven doing good on earth.”

Inside the Carmel, opinions were still divided. Two pairs of her rope sandals were found, patched so often that no one else would have worn them. Marie wanted to keep them, and a lay sister snatched them and threw them in the fire. “You won’t keep that filth!” Yet Céline testified, on the sister’s own word, that Sister Saint-Vincent de Paul, the sister of the wreaths, came to the dead nun, laid her forehead on Thérèse’s feet, and begged her pardon. She said she was cured on the spot of an illness she had suffered for years.

The prioress still had to write the letter that the sister in the kitchen had worried about. Instead, the Carmel sent out Thérèse’s own words. The three manuscripts were edited, with Pauline’s cuts and changes, and printed in October 1898 as Story of a Soul. Her uncle paid for two thousand copies, and they went to other Carmels in place of the usual obituary letter. The Carmels lent it, and the requests poured in. By 1915 the Lisieux Carmel was receiving as many as five hundred letters on some days, and the book had been translated into thirty-five languages or dialects. In Scotland a Presbyterian minister named Alexander Grant carried a newspaper notice of the English edition in his pocket for nearly a year and finally read the book in bed with influenza. Later he hung her photograph over his fireplace, and when Protestant visitors were expected, he found he could not take it down. In 1911 he became a Catholic, and later he went to live in Alençon.

Some of those who had known her could not see what the fuss was about. In 1907 one of her fellow nuns wrote to the bishop, “I never saw anything that could get her put on the altars.” The Church did not ask that question lightly. The bishop’s court opened in 1910 and heard her sisters, her fellow nuns, and others who had known her, under oath. When the grave was opened that September, only her bones remained. In Rome the Promoter of the Faith, whose duty was to raise objections, argued that she was admired mostly through her own book and that her humility was not heroic. Then war came, and soldiers in the trenches sent petitions for her beatification. When the grave was opened again in 1917, a white ribbon lay in the coffin with her words lettered in gold: “I want to spend my Heaven doing good on earth. After my death I will let fall a shower of roses.”

In 1921 Pope Benedict XV declared that she had practiced the virtues to a heroic degree. In 1923 two cures were approved as miracles, among them that of a seminarian, Charles Anne, who had lain dying of tuberculosis in 1906 and cried out, as Taylor tells it, “I did not come here to die! I came to work for God!” The little way, said Pope Pius XI that year, was an easy way for every soul. On 26 March 1923 her remains were carried from the cemetery to the Carmel on a white hearse drawn by four white horses, past some fifty thousand people who had come to the town. That April Pius XI beatified her, and that evening, Laveille wrote, he came to St. Peter’s himself and knelt in silent prayer. On 17 May 1925 he declared her a saint, and when he finished the formula, witnesses reported, the crowd in St. Peter’s broke into applause.

In 1927 he made her patron of the missions, beside St. Francis Xavier, though she lived enclosed from the age of fifteen. In 1944 she became patron of France with Joan of Arc. In 1997, a hundred years after her death, Pope John Paul II named her a Doctor of the Church. The sister in the kitchen had been right about one thing. Thérèse never did anything the world would have called worth speaking about. She let herself be carried. “I am a little seed,” she had said that July. “No one knows yet what will come out of it.”

A Note on the Sources

Most of what Thérèse said in her last months comes from notes that Pauline kept and later copied into the Yellow Notebook; she recopied it in 1921–1923 from an earlier copy, and four versions exist. Story of a Soul was edited by Pauline and others before it was printed. The quotations here follow Thérèse’s manuscripts where they survive. One comes from the 1912 English translation of the edited book, and two from Taylor’s English lives. Witnesses disagree on some details. They differ on the date of the 1883 cure (10 or 13 May), the exact words of the audience with Leo XIII, the size of the funeral, and the time of her death (between 7:15 and 7:30). The sister in the kitchen is never named. The sisters also prepared their testimony for the Church’s court carefully, and Marie wrote in 1915 that the sisters meant to set right the Roman advocate’s portrait of her. Where the text depends on one witness, it says who.

How This Life Was Researched

This life rests on 3,661 numbered fact cards. Their sources include Thérèse’s three manuscripts, all 266 letters in the Carmel’s edition, and the notebooks of her last words kept by her sisters. They also include 33 sworn depositions from the Church’s inquiry of 1910–1911 and 26 from the inquiry of 1915–1917. Her mother’s letters, papal documents, and early English lives by Laveille and Taylor complete the list, in French, English, and Latin. The English translations of her French words are our own.
