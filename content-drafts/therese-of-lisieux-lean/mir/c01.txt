=== 001 | Abbé Charles Anne, a seminarian with tuberculosis, Bayeux, 1906
Charles Anne, a 23-year-old seminarian from Lisieux, took to his bed after a haemorrhage on 23 August 1906. His doctor, Francis La Néele, found cavities in the lungs and the tubercle bacillus in the sputum, and death was expected within days. A first novena failed. He made a second to Thérèse alone, and Taylor gives his prayer: "I did not come here to die! I came to work for God! You must cure me!" Laveille and Taylor describe an instant recovery. The Carmel's record shows a course over days, with the fever normal from 10 September and only a scar found about 18 September. Two doctors found him in excellent health in August 1917, and he later served as a priest. The three experts of the Congregation judged the lesion organic and not restorable by nature.

=== 002 | Sister Louise de Saint-Germain, Ustaritz, September 1916
Sister Louise, a teacher of the Daughters of the Cross at Ustaritz, had a bleeding stomach ulcer from 1913 and received the last sacraments in 1915. In early September 1916 another sister persuaded her to pray to Thérèse again. On the night of 10 September she reported that Thérèse said, "Be generous; you will soon be cured; I promise it," and the next morning rose petals lay round her bed. After a violent attack on 21 September she fell asleep, and she woke on the 22nd cured and asked to go to Mass. Taylor gives 25 September; Laveille and the Carmel give 22 September. The Church's experts found a most grave haemorrhagic ulcer of the stomach.

=== 003 | Maria Pellemans at the tomb, Lisieux, March 1923
Maria Pellemans, a young Belgian woman, had pulmonary tuberculosis by October 1909, followed by tubercular enteritis. She was treated at home and at the La Hulpe sanatorium, and a pilgrimage to Lourdes in August 1920 brought no change. In March 1923 she came with a pilgrimage to the Lisieux Carmel. Praying at the tomb, she reported that she at once recovered perfect health. Taylor puts this on 24 March, the day after her arrival. Three doctors appointed by the Congregation gave favourable opinions, and the diagnosis was chronic tuberculosis of the lungs and intestines.

=== 004 | Sister Gabriella Trimusi, Parma, June 1923
Sister Gabriella Trimusi, of the Poor Daughters of the Sacred Hearts at Parma, felt pain in her left knee from 1913. Heliotherapy, baths and injections did not help, and her spine was affected four years later; an X-ray showed periostitis of the head of the tibia. On 13 June 1923 she began a novena to Blessed Thérèse. On its last day, after walking painfully to the Carmelite church, she knelt without pain, and that evening she cried "Sanata sum!" (I am healed). The Congregation's diagnosis was chronic tubercular arthrosynovitis of the knee and chronic spondylitis of the spine. The bull gives no day for the cure.

=== 005 | Mrs Dorans, a tumour cured in Glasgow, 27 August 1909
Mrs Dorans, a widow with twelve children, had an abdominal tumour judged inoperable at the Western Infirmary in spring 1909. She could keep no food for ten weeks. She began a novena to the Little Flower on 22 August. About 5.30 on the morning of 27 August she reported a gentle pressure on her shoulders and a warm breath, and she felt no pain. A lump smaller than a marble remained and was gone the next day. Her Protestant doctor is quoted as saying that if the cure lasted it was nothing short of a miracle, and an X-ray in July 1910 was normal. Doctors for the Lisieux tribunal examined her in 1911 and found the abdomen normal. Taylor calls it the best-proven of the graces, and the diagnosis is given as "neoplasm" in some sources and "cancerous tumour" in others.

=== 006 | Gallipoli, the 500 francs left in a drawer, 16 January 1910
The Carmel of Gallipoli was in debt and sometimes went without dinner. After a triduum to the Trinity through Thérèse, the prioress, Mother Maria Carmela, reported that near dawn on 16 January 1910 she felt a hand on her bedclothes and a voice promised money for the debt. A young Carmelite shining with light led her to the parlour, said "I am the Servant of God, Sœur Thérèse of Lisieux," and added "my way is sure." In a drawer of the accounts desk the prioress found ten 50-franc notes of the Bank of Naples. Some sources give the sum in lire or livres. The prioress told Bishops Müller and Giannattasio, and the inquiry was reported to Rome. The prioress had been ill with pleurisy, and Giannattasio denied any nervous illness before or after.

=== 007 | Gallipoli, further sums of money, January to August 1910
After the first gift, the Gallipoli accounts showed unexplained surpluses at each month-end, from 25 lire in January onward. In May the prioress reported that Thérèse appeared and said a 50-franc note was in the box, and two sisters found three notes where there had been two. On the night of 15 to 16 July she promised 100 lire, and a 5-lire note was found at the foot of the Sacred Heart statue. On 6 August a 100-lire note, which the bishop had said he had lost from his accounts, was found and returned to him. After that alms came from outside. Bishop Müller checked the accounts carefully and found the origin of the sums inexplicable.

=== 008 | Gallipoli, "they will find only my bones," 5 September 1910
On 5 September 1910, the eve of the first exhumation, the prioress of Gallipoli reported that Thérèse appeared and said that only her bones would be found. She is said to have spoken of her relics as powerful weapons against the devil and of hidden crosses she had borne on earth. The prioress knew nothing of the exhumation. The account comes from Taylor, who drew on letters to the Carmel, and the case belongs to the same inquiry as the Gallipoli money.

=== 009 | Gallipoli, the bishop's sealed envelope, 16 January 1911
Bishop Nicola Giannattasio of Nardò sealed 300 lire inside a larger envelope, with a note, "My Way is a Sure one," dated 16 January 1910, to be opened at Gallipoli on 16 January 1911. The nuns had been making a novena for 300 lire to decorate the chapel. He reported that at the grille he saw the seal intact, with the gum folds as he had made them. Inside he found two 100-lire and two 50-lire notes, and one note smelled lightly of roses. He swore to this at the Ordinary Process and was moved to tears. The case belongs to the Church's inquiry at Gallipoli, and it is not an approved miracle.
