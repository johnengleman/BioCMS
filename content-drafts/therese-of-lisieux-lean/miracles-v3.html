This list gathers the accounts attributed to Thérèse of Lisieux, with their sources and their status. Each entry says who reported the event and how the report was treated. It makes no claim that any event was a miracle, and it does not dismiss any of them. Only the four healings in the first group were approved by the Church. Every other entry is a report, and the label under each one says only who made it. Where the sources disagree, the entry says so, and a key to the sources follows the last entry. The list has 322 entries, drawn from the Church decrees, the sworn testimony of the processes, Taylor, Laveille, de Teil, and Thérèse’s own writings.

Miracles approved by the Church

These four healings were examined by the Church and approved, two for the beatification in 1923 and two for the canonization in 1925. Each decree used the diagnoses of medical experts appointed by the Congregation of Rites.

Abbé Charles Anne, a seminarian with tuberculosis, Bayeux, 1906

Charles Anne, a 23-year-old seminarian from Lisieux, took to his bed after a haemorrhage on 23 August 1906. His doctor, Francis La Néele, found cavities in the lungs and the tubercle bacillus in the sputum, and death was expected within days. A first novena failed. He made a second to Thérèse alone, and Taylor gives his prayer: "I did not come here to die! I came to work for God! You must cure me!" Laveille and Taylor describe an instant recovery. The Carmel's record shows a course over days, with the fever normal from 10 September and only a scar found about 18 September. Two doctors found him in excellent health in August 1917, and he later served as a priest. The three experts of the Congregation judged the lesion organic and not restorable by nature.

Status: approved for beatification (decree of 11 Feb 1923).

Source: AAS 15 (1923) pp. 169–170; AAS 17 (1925) p. 341; Laveille 1928 scan pp. 409–411; Taylor 1927 scan p. 276; Taylor 1924 scan p. 21.

Sister Louise de Saint-Germain, Ustaritz, September 1916

Sister Louise, a teacher of the Daughters of the Cross at Ustaritz, had a bleeding stomach ulcer from 1913 and received the last sacraments in 1915. In early September 1916 another sister persuaded her to pray to Thérèse again. On the night of 10 September she reported that Thérèse said, "Be generous; you will soon be cured; I promise it," and the next morning rose petals lay round her bed. After a violent attack on 21 September she fell asleep, and she woke on the 22nd cured and asked to go to Mass. Taylor gives 25 September; Laveille and the Carmel give 22 September. The Church's experts found a most grave haemorrhagic ulcer of the stomach.

Status: approved for beatification (decree of 11 Feb 1923).

Source: AAS 15 (1923) pp. 169–170; AAS 17 (1925) pp. 340–341; Laveille 1928 scan pp. 425–426; Taylor 1927 scan pp. 276–277.

Maria Pellemans at the tomb, Lisieux, March 1923

Maria Pellemans, a young Belgian woman, had pulmonary tuberculosis by October 1909, followed by tubercular enteritis. She was treated at home and at the La Hulpe sanatorium, and a pilgrimage to Lourdes in August 1920 brought no change. In March 1923 she came with a pilgrimage to the Lisieux Carmel. Praying at the tomb, she reported that she at once recovered perfect health. Taylor puts this on 24 March, the day after her arrival. Three doctors appointed by the Congregation gave favourable opinions, and the diagnosis was chronic tuberculosis of the lungs and intestines.

Status: approved for canonization (decree of 19 Mar 1925).

Source: AAS 17 (1925) pp. 149–150 and 343–344; Taylor 1927 scan p. 279; Laveille 1928 scan p. 438.

Sister Gabriella Trimusi, Parma, June 1923

Sister Gabriella Trimusi, of the Poor Daughters of the Sacred Hearts at Parma, felt pain in her left knee from 1913. Heliotherapy, baths and injections did not help, and her spine was affected four years later; an X-ray showed periostitis of the head of the tibia. On 13 June 1923 she began a novena to Blessed Thérèse. On its last day, after walking painfully to the Carmelite church, she knelt without pain, and that evening she cried "Sanata sum!" (I am healed). The Congregation's diagnosis was chronic tubercular arthrosynovitis of the knee and chronic spondylitis of the spine. The bull gives no day for the cure.

Status: approved for canonization (decree of 19 Mar 1925).

Source: AAS 17 (1925) pp. 148–150 and 342–343; Taylor 1927 scan pp. 279–280; Laveille 1928 scan p. 438.

Cases examined by a Church inquiry

These cases were examined by a canonical inquiry or tribunal and were not approved as miracles. Four of the five concern one Carmel at Gallipoli.

Mrs Dorans, a tumour cured in Glasgow, 27 August 1909

Mrs Dorans, a widow with twelve children, had an abdominal tumour judged inoperable at the Western Infirmary in spring 1909. She could keep no food for ten weeks. She began a novena to the Little Flower on 22 August. About 5.30 on the morning of 27 August she reported a gentle pressure on her shoulders and a warm breath, and she felt no pain. A lump smaller than a marble remained and was gone the next day. Her Protestant doctor is quoted as saying that if the cure lasted it was nothing short of a miracle, and an X-ray in July 1910 was normal. Doctors for the Lisieux tribunal examined her in 1911 and found the abdomen normal. Taylor calls it the best-proven of the graces, and the diagnosis is given as "neoplasm" in some sources and "cancerous tumour" in others.

Status: examined by the diocesan tribunal (1911); not an approved miracle; Taylor calls it the best-proven grace.

Source: de Teil 1913 art. 142, pp. 180–185; Taylor 1924 scan pp. 398–401; Ordinary Process witness 2, f. 188r.

Gallipoli, the 500 francs left in a drawer, 16 January 1910

The Carmel of Gallipoli was in debt and sometimes went without dinner. After a triduum to the Trinity through Thérèse, the prioress, Mother Maria Carmela, reported that near dawn on 16 January 1910 she felt a hand on her bedclothes and a voice promised money for the debt. A young Carmelite shining with light led her to the parlour, said "I am the Servant of God, Sœur Thérèse of Lisieux," and added "my way is sure." In a drawer of the accounts desk the prioress found ten 50-franc notes of the Bank of Naples. Some sources give the sum in lire or livres. The prioress told Bishops Müller and Giannattasio, and the inquiry was reported to Rome. The prioress had been ill with pleurisy, and Giannattasio denied any nervous illness before or after.

Status: investigated by canonical inquiry (Bishop Müller of Gallipoli; reported to Rome); not an approved miracle.

Source: Taylor 1924 scan pp. 375–377; Taylor 1927 scan pp. 437–438; Laveille 1928 scan p. 413; Ordinary Process witness 33 (Giannattasio), ff. 1318r–1326v.

Gallipoli, further sums of money, January to August 1910

After the first gift, the Gallipoli accounts showed unexplained surpluses at each month-end, from 25 lire in January onward. In May the prioress reported that Thérèse appeared and said a 50-franc note was in the box, and two sisters found three notes where there had been two. On the night of 15 to 16 July she promised 100 lire, and a 5-lire note was found at the foot of the Sacred Heart statue. On 6 August a 100-lire note, which the bishop had said he had lost from his accounts, was found and returned to him. After that alms came from outside. Bishop Müller checked the accounts carefully and found the origin of the sums inexplicable.

Status: investigated by canonical inquiry (same inquiry as M-006); not an approved miracle.

Source: Taylor 1924 scan pp. 378–379; Taylor 1927 scan p. 437; Ordinary Process witness 33 (Giannattasio), f. 1318r–v.

Gallipoli, "they will find only my bones," 5 September 1910

On 5 September 1910, the eve of the first exhumation, the prioress of Gallipoli reported that Thérèse appeared and said that only her bones would be found. She is said to have spoken of her relics as powerful weapons against the devil and of hidden crosses she had borne on earth. The prioress knew nothing of the exhumation. The account comes from Taylor, who drew on letters to the Carmel, and the case belongs to the same inquiry as the Gallipoli money.

Status: investigated by canonical inquiry (same inquiry as M-006); not an approved miracle.

Source: Taylor 1924 scan p. 379; Taylor 1927 scan pp. 271 and 437.

Gallipoli, the bishop's sealed envelope, 16 January 1911

Bishop Nicola Giannattasio of Nardò sealed 300 lire inside a larger envelope, with a note, "My Way is a Sure one," dated 16 January 1910, to be opened at Gallipoli on 16 January 1911. The nuns had been making a novena for 300 lire to decorate the chapel. He reported that at the grille he saw the seal intact, with the gum folds as he had made them. Inside he found two 100-lire and two 50-lire notes, and one note smelled lightly of roses. He swore to this at the Ordinary Process and was moved to tears. The case belongs to the Church's inquiry at Gallipoli, and it is not an approved miracle.

Status: investigated by canonical inquiry (Bishop Müller); sworn by the sender, Bishop Giannattasio, at the Ordinary Process; not an approved miracle.

Source: Taylor 1924 scan pp. 380–381; Taylor 1927 scan pp. 437–438; Ordinary Process witness 33 (Giannattasio), ff. 1318v–1326v.

Cases presented to the Church process, 1901 to 1915

These cases were listed either in the vice-postulator’s Articles of the Cause (1910) or in Mère Agnès’s 1915 summary of 54 dossiers. They were presented to the process as cases and were not approved.

Mme Héloïse Debossu, a fibrous tumour, Marnes-la-Coquette, 1901

Mme Héloïse Debossu had a fibrous tumour for about ten years and refused an operation. She was bedridden from January 1901 and had peritonitis in September, and the doctors gave her up. Her priest, Abbé Petit, sent a lock of Thérèse's hair and asked the Carmel for a novena. On its last day she walked to church for Communion, and her health and weight returned. Taylor counts it among the first hundreds of cures reported by lay people.

Status: listed in the Articles of the Cause (1910); recorded by the Carmel or shrine.

Source: de Teil 1913 art. 121, pp. 134–135; Laveille 1928 scan p. 412, note.

Mme Jouanne, peritonitis after surgery, Marnes-la-Coquette, 1903

Mme Jouanne, a gardener's wife and mother of two, had appendicitis with general peritonitis in January 1903. A Paris surgeon opened the abdomen, found an abscess and diffuse pus, closed it, and gave her hours to live. The curé placed under her bolster a sachet of rose petals that Thérèse had used to caress her crucifix. The report says the illness stopped that day, she was out of danger on the third day, and she was seen in good health in 1907.

Status: listed in the Articles of the Cause (1910).

Source: de Teil 1913 art. 122, pp. 135–138.

Marthe Bourgon, appendicitis with gangrene, Nancy, 1905

Marthe Bourgon had appendicitis diagnosed late, and her operation was cut short because of gangrene. A sinus to the intestine formed, and she was dying. She received a lock of Thérèse's hair and made a novena. Two days later the sinus closed, and the chief surgeon is quoted as saying, "This cure is a phenomenon."

Status: listed in the Articles of the Cause (1910).

Source: de Teil 1913 art. 123, pp. 138–139.

Brother Ignatius Boron, liver stones, Cracow, 1906

Brother Ignatius Boron, a Jesuit lay brother at Cracow, had liver stones from Christmas 1905. Two professors called the illness incurable, and the surgeon said an operation was necessary. A novena was made through Thérèse, with a crisis on the second day. He was reported cured on 20 March 1906, and Fr Casimir Konopka reported it on 19 May.

Status: listed in the Articles of the Cause (1910); recorded by the Carmel or shrine.

Source: de Teil 1913 art. 124, p. 139; Laveille 1928 scan p. 412, note.

Sister Joséphine, double pneumonia, Carmel of Nîmes in exile, Florence, 1907

Sister Joséphine, a 41-year-old lay sister, developed double lobar pneumonia after influenza on 18 January 1907. Her temperature reached 43 degrees, her breathing became Cheyne-Stokes, her pulse passed 150, and she passed almost no urine. The prioress, woken at 2 a.m., found her black and glazed. The sister refused the prayers for the dying, said the Creed, and felt "as if touched." The crisis passed, and she said she had not prayed for a cure and had not wanted one.

Status: listed in the Articles of the Cause (1910).

Source: de Teil 1913 art. 126, pp. 143–145.

Louis Legot, a child with meningitis, Lisieux, 1908

Louis Legot, aged five, had meningitis in March 1908 and lay delirious and speechless for hours. The doctor declared it incurable. His mother asked, as a sign, that he would ask for a drink when she came home from Mass. He did, and he recovered. Abbé Lamy reported it on 16 April 1909, and Maupas repeated Lamy's account at the Ordinary Process.

Status: sworn at the Church process; listed in the Articles of the Cause (1910).

Source: de Teil 1913 arts. 131–132, pp. 155–156; Ordinary Process witness 25 (Maupas), f. 1231r–v.

Sister Sainte-Foy, loss of voice, Carmel of Rodez, 1908

Sister Sainte-Foy (Louise Chincholle) had six years of weakness, sixteen months of total loss of voice, and blood-spitting that raised fear of tubercular laryngitis. She began a novena to the Holy Face through Thérèse on 26 April 1908. On the second day she hummed two lines of "Vivre d'amour," and on the fourth day she was cured. The doctor's certificate of 27 July 1908 records signs of "neuropathy," and the source itself notes this as a natural explanation.

Status: listed in the Articles of the Cause (1910).

Source: de Teil 1913 art. 127, pp. 145–147.

Reine Fauquet, the "blind child of four," Lisieux, 1908

Reine Fauquet, aged four and a half, had phlyctenular keratitis from January 1906, and three doctors called her eyes hopeless. Her mother carried her to Thérèse's grave, because "Reine" was the name Louis Martin used for his daughter. On 26 May 1908, after a crisis, the child said she no longer needed her glasses. She told those around her that she had seen her "little Teresa," veiled and bright, who took her hand. Laveille and Taylor present it as an instant cure of a blind child. The source notes that keratitis tends to relapse and leave scars, and the certificate says she was cured completely.

Status: listed in the Articles of the Cause (1910); recorded by the Carmel or shrine.

Source: de Teil 1913 art. 128, pp. 147–150; Laveille 1928 scan p. 412, note.

Sister Marie-Michel, tuberculosis, Carmel of San Pol de Mar, 1908

Sister Marie-Michel of the Blessed Sacrament was diagnosed with tuberculosis on 19 April 1908 by Dr Joseph Marquis, who gave her two years at most. She made novenas ending on 30 September. She was certified well on 15 November 1908, although she had received the usual treatment. The cure was re-attested in March 1910.

Status: listed in the Articles of the Cause (1910).

Source: de Teil 1913 art. 129, pp. 150–152.

Marguerite Chabaud, a stomach ulcer, Issy-les-Moulineaux, 1909

Marguerite Chabaud, a 24-year-old nurse, had a stomach ulcer with vomiting of blood and relapsed on 8 February 1909. She was fed artificially, and a sachet with wool from Thérèse's pillow was laid on her. At the third Hail Mary after Mass on 21 February she felt a hand placed on the painful place. She then drank milk and ate chocolate and bread. Dr Tison confirmed the cure on 18 May 1909 and noted no nervous history.

Status: listed in the Articles of the Cause (1910).

Source: de Teil 1913 art. 130, pp. 152–155.

Sister Mary of Calvary, pneumonia, Carmel of Mangalore, 1909

Sister Mary of Calvary, aged 66, had pneumonia with liver and kidney disease and received Viaticum on 16 March 1909. She wanted to die, and she heard a voice telling her to abandon herself. A novena followed, with a suffocation crisis on 23 March. At 3.30 on the morning of 24 March she felt comfort, got up, and knelt half an hour at Communion. Dr Fernandez certified the cure on 31 July 1909, and Fr Élie cited the printed report at the Ordinary Process.

Status: sworn at the Church process; listed in the Articles of the Cause (1910); recorded by the Carmel or shrine.

Source: de Teil 1913 art. 132, pp. 156–162; Ordinary Process witness 5, f. 449r.

Brother Mary-Paul, a Trappist with a stomach ulcer, Tàrrega, 1909

Brother Mary-Paul (Philippe Tobzane), a Trappist lay brother, was thought to have a stomach ulcer and was given morphine and nutrient enemas. On 4 May 1909 he drank water with particles of a relic of Thérèse. He walked and ate eggs, potatoes, raisins and wine. Dr Alexandro Ubach attested the cure on 15 June 1909.

Status: listed in the Articles of the Cause (1910).

Source: de Teil 1913 art. 134, pp. 166–171.

Sister Mary of the Heart of Jesus, paralysis, Quimper, 1909

Sister Mary of the Heart of Jesus, aged 31, had typhoid or cerebro-spinal meningitis from December 1908, with phlebitis of both legs and paresis. The infirmarian held out Thérèse's picture and stepped back, saying "come and fetch the picture," and the sister walked. Dr Pilveu, writing on 28 July 1909, could not explain it, and slight swelling remained. The diagnosis is uncertain because Quimper had no laboratory.

Status: listed in the Articles of the Cause (1910).

Source: de Teil 1913 art. 133, pp. 162–166.

Adrien Henri, a stomach illness, seminary of Nice, 1909

Adrien Henri had a stomach illness for twenty years that grew worse in July 1909. He asked to sleep until 5 a.m., and he did. His appetite returned. Two weeks later he walked 40 kilometres in a day.

Status: listed in the Articles of the Cause (1910).

Source: de Teil 1913 art. 140, pp. 178–179.

Sister Marie-Bénigne Martin, a stomach ulcer, Visitation of Caen, 1909

Sister Marie-Bénigne Martin, a lay sister, had a stomach ulcer from December 1908 and vomited blood. On 2 July 1909, the last day of a novena, Léonie Martin (Sister Françoise-Thérèse) gave her water with a rose petal that Thérèse had used on her crucifix. The pain ended and she drank a litre of milk. The next day she ate an omelette, peas and salad, and the doctor's certificate is dated 29 July 1909.

Status: listed in the Articles of the Cause (1910).

Source: de Teil 1913 art. 135, pp. 171–173.

Mary Antes, trampled by a horse, New York, 1909

On 30 July 1909 Mary Antes was knocked down and trampled by a frightened horse in a New York street. Her ribs were broken and driven into the lung, her head bled, and the ambulance doctor gave her one chance in a thousand. A novena began on 3 August and a relic was applied. She mended and was out of danger on its last day. Her sister, a Dominican nun at Brooklyn, reported it on 12 August 1909.

Status: listed in the Articles of the Cause (1910); recorded by the Carmel or shrine.

Source: de Teil 1913 art. 136, pp. 173–174; Taylor 1924 scan pp. 402–403.

The Pichard boy, peritonitis after measles, Nogent-sur-Seine, 1909

A five-year-old boy had peritonitis after measles from 2 August 1909, and a specialist called it hopeless. The Carmel's prioress advised a novena. The father's letter of 2 November 1909 says the boy was cured on the eighth day.

Status: listed in the Articles of the Cause (1910).

Source: de Teil 1913 art. 139, pp. 177–178.

A mother and her unborn child, Smyrna, 1909

A woman five months pregnant was struck by her two-year-old child and feared a miscarriage or a deformity. A piece of Thérèse's clothing was applied, and the pains stopped. A healthy girl was born on 13 October 1909 and named Teresa of the Child Jesus. The chaplain reported it on 18 October 1909.

Status: listed in the Articles of the Cause (1910).

Source: de Teil 1913 art. 138, pp. 176–177.

Henriette Luchini, enteritis, and the lost parcel of relics, Piacenza, 1909

Henriette Luchini, aged eleven, had enteritis for two years. Relics sent to the Piacenza Carmel were misdelivered to another convent and given to the family, and a novena followed. A letter of 25 September 1909 reports that she was cured. The parcel's loss on the way was described as having "given rise to a fine miracle."

Status: listed in the Articles of the Cause (1910); recorded by the Carmel or shrine.

Source: de Teil 1913 art. 137, pp. 174–176; Ordinary Process witness 5, f. 443v.

Madagascar, a woman who walked and a mother's "beautiful lady," 1909

At Ambatolampy a woman who had been unable to move for ten years walked after novenas and was baptised Mary Teresa. A Malagasy mother said that "the beautiful lady," meaning Thérèse's picture, came in the night in a white robe and cured her dying baby. Sister Berchmans reported both accounts on 19 December 1909.

Status: listed in the Articles of the Cause (1910).

Source: de Teil 1913 art. 141, pp. 179–180.

Brother Paul, a knee wound, Trappist monastery of Rogersville, Canada, 1910

Brother Paul, a Trappist of Rogersville, was reported cured suddenly of a serious knee wound after an apparition of the Servant of God. Dr Bourret of Rogersville attested on 22 April 1910 that the healing of such a wound was so quick that he felt bound to ascribe it to a cause "quite supernatural." Mère Agnès lists it as item 6 of her summary.

Status: listed in Mère Agnès's 1915 summary of 54 dossiers.

Source: Apostolic Process witness 6 (Mère Agnès), [534], session 21, 19 July 1915.

Abbé Claude-Marcel Weber, a cataract, Saint-Jean-de-Luz, 1910

Abbé Weber, a priest aged about 75, had a beginning cataract confirmed in Paris in 1900, and by 1909 an optician called the right eye "completely extinguished." The exiled Carmel of Bordeaux at Zarautz made novenas to Thérèse without telling him. After the second novena, with a picture-relic laid on his eyes each evening, he reported that he could read the small print of his breviary and recognise people at over a hundred paces. During the third he saw bee eggs in a queen cell with the naked eye. He gave sworn testimony at the Ordinary Process on 22 and 23 March 1911, and Dr Baradat wrote on 7 September 1910.

Status: sworn at the Church process; listed in the Articles of the Cause (1910); recorded by the Carmel or shrine.

Source: de Teil 1913 art. 144, pp. 200–205; Ordinary Process witness 19, ff. 1130r–1138v; Taylor 1924 scan pp. 405–407.

Ferdinand Aubry, an ulcer of the tongue, Lisieux, 1910

Ferdinand Aubry, aged 60, lived at the home of the Little Sisters of the Poor in Lisieux and had a gangrenous ulcer of the tongue that was feared to be cancer. On 28 September 1910 he swallowed a rose petal from Thérèse's deathbed. On 2 October he said he was cured, and the missing part of the tongue is said to have grown back in three weeks. A photograph is attached to the doctor's certificate. He died peacefully on 18 December 1910. The sources differ on whether it was cancer or a gangrenous ulcer.

Status: sworn at the Church process; listed in the Articles of the Cause (1910); recorded by the Carmel or shrine.

Source: de Teil 1913 art. 147, pp. 213–218; Laveille 1928 scan p. 392; Apostolic Process witness 23 (Jeanne La Néele), [1360].

Sister Mary-Dominic, a fibroid tumour, Vertou hospital, 1910

Sister Mary-Dominic of St-Gildas-des-Bois, aged 52, had a fibroid in the left side of the abdomen "the size of a newborn's head." Three doctors advised an operation. After nine novenas she woke on 3 October 1910 lying on her left side with no swelling. Dr H. found no tumour and no scar.

Status: listed in the Articles of the Cause (1910).

Source: de Teil 1913 art. 146, pp. 208–213.

Abbé Jules Rossignol, an apparition before death, Beaupréau, 1911

Abbé Jules Rossignol, about 76, had taught theology at the Luçon seminary and lived at a retired priests' house at Beaupréau. For over forty years he had been secretly afraid of God's judgment. On the morning of 16 January 1911 he reported that he saw "little Sister Thérèse" at his bedside, smiling, and she made him understand that she was taking care of his affair. "It is not a dream, I was fully awake," he said. His fear gave way to joy, and he died almost suddenly on 18 January, with no sign of fear. He had a chronic intestinal disorder, but nothing foretold his death.

Status: sworn at the Church process; listed in the Articles of the Cause (1910); recorded by the Carmel or shrine.

Source: de Teil 1913 art. 145, pp. 205–208; Ordinary Process witnesses 21–22, ff. 1162r–1167v; Taylor 1924 scan pp. 434–436.

Clémentine Derenne, albuminuria and meningitis, Laval, 1911

Clémentine Derenne, aged 17, had albuminuria threatening consumption, then cerebral uraemia, and on 1 February 1911 the doctor said meningitis had set in. On 2 February she was given wool from Thérèse's pillow and could read its tiny print. She reported a young nun who said "You will be cured" and a perfume, and by 6 p.m. Dr Pivert found no trace of lung trouble or meningitis. A third visit on 4 February spoke of personal matters. Monsignor de Teil made an inquiry and confirmed the facts.

Status: listed in Mère Agnès's 1915 summary of 54 dossiers; recorded by the Carmel or shrine.

Source: Taylor 1924 scan pp. 409–411; Apostolic Process witness 6 (Mère Agnès), [536], item 12.

The Rev. Alexander Grant, a minister who became a Catholic, Edinburgh, 1911

Alexander Grant, a United Free Church minister, read the English Story of a Soul and, ill with influenza late in 1909, felt a sense that Thérèse was in the room. At the end of March 1911, during a novena in a convent chapel, he felt Christ present in the tabernacle. He was received into the Church on 20 April 1911. This is a conversion, not a healing, and he testified at both processes.

During Thérèse’s life, 1873 to 1897

These accounts concern events before her death. Many are her own reports of answered prayer, dreams and signs, and some are family reports. None is attributed to her as a miracle worker in her lifetime, and each keeps the label of who reported it.

A prayer to Saint Francis de Sales for the baby Thérèse, Le Mans, 1873

Zélie Martin's sister, Sister Marie-Dosithée, prayed to Saint Francis de Sales at the Visitation of Le Mans and vowed that if the baby lived she would bear his name, Françoise. She wrote that if Zélie refused, the saint was free to take the child back. Zélie refused, saying the child had improved before the prayers. This is a family report and not a miracle attributed to Thérèse herself.

Status: family report or tradition; popular report.

Source: Zélie Martin, letter CF 87 (1 March 1873), Carmel of Lisieux family correspondence.

The baby Thérèse revived in her wet nurse's lap, Alençon, 1873

At about nine weeks old Thérèse had a weak intestinal illness that Zélie feared was the one that had killed two of her sons. Dr Belloc said only the breast could save her, and the wet nurse Rose Taillé arrived at 10.30 a.m. At about one o'clock the baby brought up some mouthfuls and fell as if dead, while five people stood round and no breath could be seen. After a quarter of an hour she opened her eyes and smiled, and from then she was cured. Céline, who was three or four at the time, swore it came through Saint Joseph's intercession.

Status: sworn at the Church process; family report or tradition.

Source: Zélie Martin, letter CF 89 (March 1873); Laveille 1928 scan pp. 48–49; Apostolic Process witness 8 (Céline), [668] and [776].

The toddler found asleep on a chair, Alençon, 1874

Zélie forgot to push the cradle against her bed and went to the 5.30 Mass. When she came back the child's place was empty, and she found the toddler asleep sitting on a high chair by the bed, unhurt. She could not understand how the child got onto the chair. Zélie credited her guardian angel and the souls in purgatory. Marie said the child had fallen from her cradle and could not have climbed the chair herself.

Status: sworn at the Church process; family report or tradition.

Source: Laveille 1928 scan pp. 56–57; Apostolic Process witness 7 (Marie), [560]; Apostolic Process witness 8 (Céline), [776]–[777].

The veiled, bent man like her father, Les Buissonnets, about 1879

When Thérèse was about six or seven she stood alone at a window of Les Buissonnets and saw in the garden a man with her father's height and walk, but much more stooped, his head covered with a veil. He walked toward a grove and vanished. She called "Papa, papa," and Marie and Pauline searched the garden and found no trace. She stayed convinced it announced a trial. In 1889, when Louis's cerebral paralysis began, he often covered his head with a handkerchief, and the family read the vision as a foreseeing of his illness.

Status: sworn at the Church process; family report or tradition; her own report.

Source: Manuscript A, 19v–21r; Ordinary Process witness 1 (Pauline), f. 233r–v.

The smile of the Virgin, Les Buissonnets, 13 May 1883

Thérèse, aged ten, was ill from March 1883 with terrors, hallucinations, falls, loss of voice and states in which she did not know her sisters. Dr Notta is reported to have said that science was powerless. Her father had paid for a novena of Masses at Notre-Dame des Victoires. On Pentecost Sunday, 13 May, Marie, Léonie and Céline prayed before the statue of the Virgin, and Thérèse stared at it for four or five minutes with two large tears falling. She wrote that the Virgin "seemed to me beautiful" and that her pains vanished. Her sisters and Laveille say the statue moved, and she herself says the cure came gently.

Status: sworn at the Church process (Marie, Céline and Pauline); not one of the miracles chosen for the decrees; the statue is venerated as the "Vierge du Sourire".

Source: Manuscript A, 27r–31r; Laveille 1928 scan pp. 129–132.

Candles for her father at Dover, 1886

Thérèse lit a candle to the Virgin every day and prayed that Marie would learn in time to meet "le Père" at Dover. A telegram then came, which she took to mean that Marie had met him. This is her own report in a letter to Marie of 2 October 1886.

Status: her own report.

Source: Letter LT 21, to Marie, 2 October 1886.

Source: Manuscript A, 45v–46v.

This is her own report.

Status: her own report.

Source: Manuscript A, 50v–51v.

Source: Letters LT 37 (25 November 1887) and LT 50 (13 May 1888), to Marie Guérin.

Appendix 1: family and earlier events not attributed to Thérèse

These events concern her family and the Carmel’s foundress, and the two miracles approved for the beatification and canonization of her parents. They are listed so that readers can see what belongs to the family and what belongs to Thérèse.

Mother Elizabeth of St Louis cured by Archbishop d'Aviau, about 1835

The first prioress of Lisieux, Sister Elizabeth of St Louis, was threatened early in religious life by a lung disease thought mortal. Archbishop d'Aviau of Bordeaux, visiting his sister at Poitiers, blessed her and asked "Shall we give her the days of Ezechias?" She was completely cured. The same archbishop later baptised Louis Martin, born at Bordeaux in 1823. This is a report about the Carmel's foundress and not about Thérèse.

Status: recorded by the Carmel or shrine.

Source: Foundation of the Carmel 1913 p. 13.

Zélie's inner voice about lace, Alençon, 8 December 1851

On the feast of the Immaculate Conception, 8 December 1851, Zélie was making her bed after a novena when she heard the words "Have Point d'Alençon made." She started the business the next day, and Marie says Zélie ascribed it to the Virgin. This is a family report and is not attributed to Thérèse.

Status: family report or tradition.

Source: Marie Martin (Sister Marie of the Sacred Heart), notebook, Carmel of Lisieux.

Zélie hears "This is the one I prepared for you," 1858

In spring 1858, on the Saint-Léonard bridge, Zélie heard inwardly the words "This is the one I prepared for you," about Louis Martin. This is a family report from Zélie's letters and Laveille, and it is not attributed to Thérèse.

Zélie at Lourdes, June 1877, no cure

Zélie went to Lourdes with Léonie, Marie and Pauline, hoping at least for Léonie's cure. She went into the icy pool four times and nearly choked. Neither she nor Léonie was cured. She wrote that God could work a miracle for her even if nobody in the world knew of it. The doctor "who believes in nothing" appears in her letters, and the sources name her tumour.

Status: family report or tradition.

Source: Zélie Martin's letters of June 1877, Carmel of Lisieux family correspondence.

Pietro Schilirò, a lung malformation, Monza, 2002

Pietro was born on 25 May 2002 in Monza with a severe lung malformation and was unable to breathe unaided. A Carmelite suggested a novena to Louis and Zélie Martin. His condition turned on 29 June 2002, and he left the clinic by 27 July. This is the miracle approved for the beatification of Louis and Zélie, and it is not attributed to Thérèse.

Status: approved for the beatification of Louis and Zélie Martin (miracle approved 3 Jul 2008; beatification 19 Oct 2008); not attributed to Thérèse.

Source: Louis and Zélie Martin cause, "The beatification miracle," louisandzeliemartin.org.

Carmen Pérez Pons, a brain haemorrhage, Valencia, 2008

Carmen was born on 15 October 2008 in Valencia at about six months' gestation, with a grade 4 intraventricular brain haemorrhage, an infection, and lung and heart problems. The Carmelites of Serra gave the family prayer cards of Louis and Zélie. Prayer began around 19 October 2008, and she improved within days, left intensive care on the third day, and went home on 2 January 2009. This is the miracle approved for the canonization of Louis and Zélie, and it is not attributed to Thérèse.

Status: approved for the canonization of Louis and Zélie Martin (miracle approved 18 Mar 2015; canonization 18 Oct 2015); not attributed to Thérèse.

Source: Louis and Zélie Martin cause, "Canonization miracle," louisandzeliemartin.org.

Mother Geneviève and the butter, the apricots and the thaw

Three reports concern the Carmel's foundress, Mother Geneviève. A small piece of butter was said never to grow less over two months until an alms came. A hard frost withered the apricot blossom, and after she prayed and heard "Be not disturbed, there will be apricots," the tree bore so much fruit that leaves were hardly visible. On a bitter washing day, while she prayed before the tabernacle, a thaw set in like spring.

Status: recorded by the Carmel or shrine.

Source: Foundation of the Carmel 1913 pp. 42–43.

Appendix 2: collective and summary reports, and predictions

These entries are summaries, predictions and statements about many events, and not single events. They are kept because the sources treat them as part of the record.

Her promises of a mission after death, 1897

In her last months Thérèse promised to make roses rain after her death, right after a refectory reading on Saint Aloysius, according to Marie. Céline says she promised it many times and in varied forms. She told Marie on 9 June that it would be like a shower of roses, and she said she would go round little baptised children like a white lady in a mission report. She also said that her relics would be sought and that she would spend her Heaven doing good on earth. This entry collects the promises as reported by her sisters, and it is not a single event.

Status: sworn at the Church process.

Source: Marie Martin, added pages, Carmel of Lisieux; Céline, Conseils et souvenirs, section "Après sa mort"; Ordinary Process witness 1 (Pauline), f. 238v–239r.

A prediction of daily Communion, judged fulfilled, 1897

Thérèse told Marie that a time would come when the Carmel would perhaps have as chaplain Abbé Hodierne, who would allow daily Communion. Chaplain Youf died a few days after her, in October 1897, and his successor Abbé Hodierne was named. His first instruction took the text "Come and eat my Bread," inviting daily Communion before the community asked, and Mother Marie de Gonzague's prejudices fell at once. This is a prediction judged fulfilled by Marie and Céline.

Status: recorded by the Carmel or shrine; personal report or belief of the witness.

Source: Céline, Conseils et souvenirs, section "Piété eucharistique"; Laveille 1928 scan p. 268.

Other predictions judged fulfilled, 1897 to 1910

Marie and Céline swore to several other predictions that they judged fulfilled. One was that Mère Agnès would be kept so busy with her that she could not manage it all, and another was a harvest of young ones in the Carmel. A third was help to missionaries and the baptism of dying children, and a fourth was that there would be no incorruption, only a skeleton, which was verified on 6 September 1910.

Status: sworn at the Church process (Marie and Céline).

Source: Apostolic Process witness 7 (Marie), [574], [605], [615]–[616], [624]; Apostolic Process witness 8 (Céline), [778]–[780].

Perfumes, lights and a sense of presence in the Carmel, 1897 to 1915

All the nuns "except perhaps one or two" said they had smelled, at intervals, perfumes such as incense, roses and violets with no natural cause. Most were sceptical at first. The perfumes began right after Thérèse's death and were more frequent in 1908 to 1910. Pauline smelled them about ten times and, as prioress, made light of the reports. Marie describes a scent of flowers near the Child Jesus statue in winter, and a strong scent that followed her to her cell after a costly act of charity. Several Carmelites and outsiders said they saw luminous traces in her cell or in the sky.

Status: sworn at the Church process; listed in the Articles of the Cause (1910).

Source: Ordinary Process witness 1 (Pauline), f. 298r–v; Apostolic Process witness 7 (Marie), [621]–[622]; de Teil 1913 art. 105, p. 115.

Missions in general, from 1898

The Annals of the Propagation of the Faith recorded unhoped-for mass conversions from 1898. Fr Roulland testified to Thérèse's influence in Japan, China and India, and Japanese Trappistines credited vocations. This is a summary of popular reports and sworn testimony and not a single event.

Status: popular report / testimony at the process.

Source: Laveille 1928 scan p. 408; Taylor 1927 scan pp. 451–452.

The "Pluie de roses," favours reported worldwide, 1899 to 1910

Countless favours came from outside the monastery, including spiritual progress, conversions, healings and apparitions. Pauline published 167 accounts, from 1899 to 25 February 1910, under the title Pluie de roses, with the Bishop of Bayeux's imprimatur. Thousands of requests came for scraps of her clothing and objects, to obtain cures. The judges ordered the printed Pluie de roses and copies of recent letters added to the Acts. The Carmel counts these accounts by the thousand, and this entry is a summary and not a single event.

Status: sworn at the Church process.

Source: Ordinary Process witness 1 (Pauline), f. 299r–v, session 20.

Thérèse's influence at exorcisms, Paris, 1909 to 1911

Witnesses report that demons, speaking through several victims, said that Thérèse helped Flamérion, freed priests from demons, and chained those demons in the victims. One demon said, "It is your Thérèse who tore this soul from me." This is sworn testimony of what was said at exorcisms.

Status: sworn at the Church process.

Source: Ordinary Process witness 23, f. 1178r–1179v; Apostolic Process witness 20, pp. 1106–1107.

Missionary favours in China and the Ivory Coast, 1910 to 1912

Marie quoted letters to the Carmel in her testimony. Fr Irénée at Wei-Hsien in China wrote that a dispensary baptised 2,000 dying children in two months during an epidemic, and that the baptisers prayed to Thérèse on the road. Bishop Wittner named little Thérèse coadjutrix of the vicariate of East Shantung in November 1912. A superior at Mousso in the Ivory Coast wrote that an invisible hand led them to hidden huts of the suffering. These are letters quoted in sworn testimony, and they are not miracle claims.

Status: letters quoted in sworn testimony (Marie, PA 7 [622]–[624], Session 26); not miracles.

Source: Apostolic Process witness 7 (Marie), [622]–[624], session 26.

Favours reported by the provincial of the Little Sisters of the Poor, 1910

The provincial of the Little Sisters of the Poor for Scotland and Ireland reported that a nun who had suddenly left her convent came back. Several old men in the homes who had given up religion for years were converted.

Status: sworn at the Church process.

Source: Ordinary Process witness 2, f. 203r, session 13, 26 August 1910.

The Carmel's later favours, postulants, missions and rose petals

The Carmel counts favours by the thousand in its Pluie de roses, including showers of roses, with petals seen or found, perfumes and vocations. A White Father in Africa reported many conversions after her picture was placed in villages. Père Roulland reported conversions in China and Japan. This is a summary and not a single event.

Status: recorded by the Carmel or shrine.

Source: Laveille 1928 scan pp. 414–415 and 408–409.

Bullets stopped by relics, medals and pictures, 1914 to 1918

Relics, medals and pictures were said to have stopped or flattened bullets beyond count in the First World War. Many were sent to the Carmel with bullet marks. This is a summary of reports and not a single event.

Status: recorded by the Carmel or shrine.

Source: Laveille 1928 scan pp. 421–424; La Croix, 19 and 21 February 1925.

Thirty favours recorded on the day of the beatification, 1923

Laveille says thirty new miracles were recorded on the very day of the beatification, 29 April 1923. Taylor repeats the figure. This is a summary report and not a single event.

Status: recorded by the Carmel or shrine.

Source: Laveille 1928 scan p. 437; Taylor 1927 scan p. 279.

Carfin, Scotland, 1923

A quarter of a million pilgrims came to Carfin in under three months, and Taylor reads it as Thérèse's answer to a challenge. This is a popular report.

Status: popular report.

Source: Taylor 1927 scan pp. 433–434.

Pius XI's own favours, 1925

Pius XI wrote to Monsignor O'Gorman in July 1925 of very remarkable favours of which he had been the object. Laveille adds that Thérèse had thrown light on questions for him. This is a report of a statement by Pius XI, and the favours are not named.

Status: report of a statement by Pius XI (letter to Mgr O'Gorman, July 1925; Taylor 1926/30).

Source: Taylor 1927 scan pp. 454–455; Laveille 1928 scan p. 432.
