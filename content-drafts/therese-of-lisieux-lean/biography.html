Shortly after seven o’clock on the evening of 30 September 1897, the prioress of the Carmelite convent in Lisieux, in Normandy, Mother Marie de Gonzague, sent the community out of the infirmary. The young nun in the bed had been in agony for two hours. The prioress judged that the end was still some way off. The sisters filed out.

The patient asked the prioress whether she was not dying. The prioress said yes, but perhaps not for some hours. The patient answered that she did not wish to suffer less. She looked at her crucifix and spoke the words her sisters recorded as her last. Then the bell was rung again, and the doors were opened. The sisters came back and stood around her bed. Céline, her closest sister, had wet her lips with ice earlier in the evening. At about twenty past seven, Thérèse Martin, a woman of twenty-four, died of tuberculosis.

She had made a promise in the weeks before. She said she would spend her Heaven doing good on earth. She said she would let fall a shower of roses. A nun who had lived behind convent walls since the age of fifteen, and whose writings were still unpublished, was promising to keep working after her death. What could come of a promise like that? It would take twenty-eight years to get a public answer, and the answer came in St. Peter’s Basilica.

The Catholic Church canonized her in 1925. In 1997 it named her a Doctor of the Church. She is co-patron of the missions, although she lived in a convent from the age of fifteen. She is co-patron of France. Her autobiography has been translated into about fifty languages. Yet the life itself was short and hidden, and most of it was ordinary.

What set her apart was a simple claim with a hard edge. She said that a person does not need to climb to God by great deeds. She said that God lifts the small, and that a small person should stay small. She tested this idea in a laundry, in a refectory, and in a long dark stretch at the end of her life when she could not feel that Heaven existed. She wrote down the doubt as plainly as the joy. This is the story of how a sensitive, stubborn girl became that writer, and of what happened to her promise afterward.

The Youngest of Nine

Marie Françoise Thérèse Martin was born on 2 January 1873 in Alençon, in Normandy. She was baptized two days later. Her parents, Louis and Zélie Martin, had nine children. Thérèse was the youngest. Five daughters lived to grow up, and all five became nuns. The Church canonized Louis and Zélie together on 18 October 2015.

The family called her Thérèse. Later, after her book appeared, the world would call her the Little Flower. At home she was a child with a strong will. An official document prepared for her cause in 1910 lists the faults of her earliest childhood. It names stubbornness and pride at the age of three. The documents of the Church, which are meant to support a saint, admit this much at the start.

She wrote about her temper herself. One evening the maid Victoire, asked to lead the prayers at Thérèse’s little May altar, only looked at her and laughed. The small Thérèse stamped her foot at her. Then she wept, first with anger and then with real regret. The story is told without excuse. It shows a child who felt everything at full strength, both anger and regret, and who could not hide either.

When she was four and a half, the family moved to Lisieux. They settled near her uncle Guérin, in a house called Les Buissonnets. Thérèse later wrote that at the age of four and a half she lost the strength of mind she had had before. She became a child who cried easily.

Her sister Pauline filled the place of her mother. Thérèse called her, in her own account, her second mother. Pauline taught her, looked after her, and listened to her. At eight and a half Thérèse started school at the Benedictine Abbey in Lisieux. Her sister Léonie had been attending, and when Léonie left, Thérèse took her place.

Then Pauline told her that she intended to enter the Carmelite convent in Lisieux. The Carmelites are an order of nuns who live enclosed, in silence and prayer. Once a sister went through that door, her family saw her through a grille. Thérèse understood what this meant. She was losing a second mother.

The loss had a strange effect. A few months later she fell ill. It was not an ordinary illness, and her family did not know what to call it.

The Statue That Smiled

In 1883 Thérèse, ten years old, became so sick that her family feared for her life. She had weeks of a strange illness. Her father paid for a novena of Masses at Notre-Dame des Victoires in Paris, a church known for prayer to the Virgin Mary. At home, her sisters knelt by her bed and prayed to the Virgin.

Thérèse described what happened next in her own words. She had called for her sister Marie again and again. When Marie came in, Thérèse saw her enter but could not recognize her. Marie knelt with the others and prayed. Thérèse wrote that the Virgin then appeared to her, more beautiful than anything she had seen, and smiled at her. She looked down and saw Marie watching her with love. Marie thought she was cured. Thérèse wrote that the healing came gently, not all at once.

She called it a miracle of Our Lady of Victories, since her father’s novena had been offered there. When Pope John Paul II wrote about her in 1997, he listed this event among the graces of her life. The Church has never counted it as one of the miracles in her cause. It rests on her report, and on the word of the family who were in the room.

The sources disagree about the date. In her own list of the graces of her life, she gives 10 May 1883. The date usually repeated is 13 May, which was Pentecost that year. The difference is a few days. The story itself is the same in both.

The cure did not make her less sensitive. On 8 May 1884 she made her First Communion. She was confirmed on 14 June that year. She kept her wish for Carmel. She also kept her tears, and they would soon become a problem she had to face.

A Cry-Baby at Christmas

By her early teens Thérèse knew this about herself: any small word could make her cry, and she could not stop it. She was nearly fourteen, and she was ashamed of it.

On 25 December 1886 the family had left shoes by the chimney, with presents inside. Thérèse was thirteen. Her father, tired from midnight Mass, saw the shoes in the chimney and said something she was not meant to hear. In her own record his words were, in English, “Well, fortunately this is the last year!”

Thérèse was going up the stairs to take off her hat. Céline saw tears in her eyes and told her not to come down, because it would hurt too much to look in her shoes at once. Thérèse had heard her father’s remark. She felt the old tears rise. Then something happened that she could only describe as a change in her whole self. She held the tears back. She went down quickly, took her shoes, and, in her own words, pulled out the presents looking as happy as a queen.

Her father laughed and was cheerful again. Thérèse said that in a moment she did what she had failed to do in ten years: she lost the oversensitive habit. She called it her Christmas conversion. The change did not make her hard. It let her look outward, to other people.

The next year she found someone to look outward to. She found him in a newspaper.

Her First Sinner

In 1887 a murderer named Pranzini was condemned to death. Thérèse, who was fourteen, decided to pray for him.

She called him her first child, and she prayed for him. She also asked for one sign, because she wanted to know her prayer had been heard. The sign she asked for was some mark of repentance at the last moment.

The day after his execution she opened the paper La Croix. On the scaffold, it said, he had taken the crucifix the priest held out and kissed it three times. Thérèse read the lines and had to hide her tears.

She had not been sure that she could make a difference, and now she believed that she had. She did not claim to have converted him. She wrote that she had asked for a sign, and that her prayer was answered to the letter. The Church has never counted this as a miracle. It is her own report, and it stayed one of the private stories that shaped the way she understood her prayer.

That year she also meant to do what she was told was too early. She was not yet fifteen, and she wanted to enter Carmel. Her father would be the one person who took her side.

Kneeling Before the Pope

The first step was the bishop. On 31 October 1887 Thérèse and her father went to Bayeux to see Bishop Hugonin. The bishop received them coolly. He asked her to wait. Her father took her side in the room, and the two of them left with nothing settled. She was not willing to wait.

In November she went to Rome with a pilgrimage, and her father went too. Thérèse had a plan. She would ask the Pope himself. A rule of the audience forbade pilgrims to speak to him. She decided to break it.

The audience took place on 20 November 1887. When her turn came, Thérèse knelt. She rested her hands on his knee. She asked him to let her enter Carmel. She told him that everyone would agree if only he would say yes.

He answered, in the words she recorded, “Well, well! You will enter if it is God’s will.” It was not yet the answer she wanted. Then a Noble Guard lifted her away. The Pope laid his hand on her lips. Outside, she wept. She wrote afterward that in the depths of her heart she felt peace, but that bitterness filled her soul.

In her account of that trip she recalled a prayer she had made to the Child Jesus some time before. She had asked him to use her as a plaything.

“Like a little ball of no value, that could be thrown on the ground, kicked about, pierced, left in a corner.”

— Thérèse of Lisieux, Story of a Soul, Manuscript A (1912 English translation), on her prayer to the Child Jesus

She meant it as an image of surrender, and the thought would return later in her life. On the road out of Rome she had reason to feel that she had been left in a corner. The Pope had not said no. He had not said yes either. She went home with a half answer.

Doors Closing at Carmel

The answer came in the spring. On 9 April 1888 Thérèse entered the Carmel of Lisieux. The date was the Feast of the Annunciation, moved from its usual day. She was fifteen years and three months old.

She left behind the house where she had grown up. She left her father, who had taken her side at Bayeux and in Rome. On the morning of her entry he blessed her while she knelt in front of him. He was weeping, and so was she. Then she walked through the door of the enclosure. The door closed behind her. On the other side she received the embraces of the sisters who had been mothers to her.

Carmel was an enclosed convent. Thérèse had asked for this life at an age when most girls were still at school. She was clothed in the habit on 10 January 1889, and she took her vows on 8 September 1890. On the day of her profession she carried a letter on her heart. In it she asked Jesus to take her from this world rather than let her stain the robe she had received at her baptism.

The letter shows what she feared. She feared losing the clean state of a child.

Her family was also falling apart. Her father, who had blessed her on the day she entered, fell ill and was paralysed. He died on 29 July 1894. His last word to her was, in her record, “In Heaven!”

Behind the enclosure walls she also had to live with women she had not chosen. Some of them were old, and some were ill. The test of her ideas would not be a single act of courage. It would be an afternoon in the laundry.

Laundry Water and Sister Peter

In the novitiate Thérèse gave herself an odd training. She set out to do each small task for the sake of Jesus and to let no one see the effort. She tried to behave well toward the sisters she liked least. She describes the experiment in her autobiography with a dry humor that most of her readers miss.

There was an old nun, Sister St. Peter, who was ill and hard to please. She could walk only with help. Each evening, ten minutes before six o’clock, someone had to take her to the refectory. Thérèse offered. She led the old sister slowly to her place. Sister St. Peter was not easy. Thérèse kept going, and she smiled at the old woman every time. In the end the old nun was won over, and she smiled back.

The laundry gave her another test. One day the sister working opposite her splashed her with dirty water. Thérèse first drew back and wiped her face. Then she thought she was foolish to refuse such a gift. She wrote that she tried hard to want more of it.

She was not always so calm. She could be sharp with the younger sisters she was asked to help. Once she saw a novice walking slowly toward the laundry. She asked, more or less, whether that was how people hurry when they have children. When a sister complained about the noise she made, she wrote, she badly wanted to defend herself. Then she thought that if she began to justify herself she would lose her peace of mind. She also felt she lacked the virtue to be accused in silence. Her last plank of safety, she said, was flight. She left the room and sat down nearby, her heart pounding.

She taught the same lesson with a story from her own childhood. A horse stood at the garden gate, and her friends argued about how to get past it. Thérèse slipped between its legs while they talked. Later she used the memory with a novice who was tempted and struggling. She told the novice not to try to climb over the difficulty but to pass beneath it. They would dry themselves, she said, in the sunshine of love. The advice is typical of her. She did not ask a struggling person to become strong. She asked the person to go low.

She was honest about her failures in prayer. She often fell asleep during the time set aside for it. She did not find this frightening. She wrote that a child is dear to its father whether it is asleep or awake. She also said of her dry feelings in prayer that she was no saint.

From these years comes one of her best-known sayings about how small things count. She said it to a novice, who recorded it.

“If you feel too lazy to pick up a bit of thread, and yet do so for love of Jesus, you acquire more merit than for a much nobler action done in a moment of fervour.”

— Thérèse of Lisieux, in Counsels and Reminiscences, recorded by a novice

It is easy to picture what she meant. Nobody important would look twice at a thread on a convent floor. She was telling the sisters that such things were the work. Soon she would be asked to take that idea and write it down for someone else to read.

An Elevator to Heaven

Her sister Pauline, now Mother Agnes, asked Thérèse to write down her childhood memories. Thérèse agreed. She wrote what became the first of three manuscripts. Later they would be joined, edited, and published as a single book. At the time, she wrote it for her sisters.

On 9 June 1895 she made a private act of offering herself to Merciful Love. She called it her Act of Oblation. It is one of the prayers that her sisters kept.

She was working out an idea that she would put into words in her last manuscript, in 1897. Thérèse felt she could not climb to Heaven by great effort. She was too small. She found a way to say this in an image taken from the modern world.

“We live in an age of inventions; nowadays the rich need not trouble to climb the stairs, they have lifts instead.”

— Thérèse of Lisieux, Story of a Soul, Manuscript C (1912 English translation)

She went on to say that she, too, wanted a lift to raise her to Jesus. She found it in a line of Scripture about a mother who carries her child. In her French the sentence reads that his arms are the lift that must raise her to Heaven. She added that for this she did not need to grow. On the contrary, she must remain little, and become smaller still. A reader today might smile at the picture, and she meant it to be simple. The idea was to stay small and let God do the lifting.

Earlier, in September 1896, she had written to her sister Marie, called Sister Marie of the Sacred Heart. That letter is Manuscript B. She was looking for her place in the Church.

In the passage she wrote what she took to be her vocation.

“O Jesus, my Love, at last I have found my vocation. My vocation is love!”

— Thérèse of Lisieux, Story of a Soul, Manuscript B (1912 English translation)

A few lines later comes the sentence in French: “Dans le cœur de l’Église, ma Mère, je serai l’Amour.” In English, in the heart of the Church, my Mother, I will be Love.

Her confidence in God had a stubborn side. She told a novice that one can never have too much of it, because God is so mighty and so merciful. She also had favorite saints, and they were not the ones a reader would expect. Her special patrons in Heaven, she said, were those who, so to speak, stole it. She named the Holy Innocents and the Good Thief. A saint who sees Heaven as something one may take by trust, without earning it, will not be surprised that she later described her own crown as earned for her by God.

Her readers would later call this her little way. She did not announce a new teaching. She said that she had found something she could do. She wrote of it with playfulness. In her writings she talks of a ball, a top, and ninepins. At Christmas 1896 she left a top and a letter for a novice, addressed to her beloved little spouse, player of ninepins on the Mountain of Carmel. She played the same game with God.

Her way had been tested before she wrote any of this. The test began in the spring of 1896, after midnight on Good Friday.

A Sign in the Night

After midnight on Good Friday in 1896, Thérèse came back to her cell from the vigil at the altar of repose. As she lay down she felt something rise to her lips. She thought it was blood. She was twenty-three years old. She thought she might die. The lamp was out, so she waited until morning to be sure. At the window she saw that she was right.

Her reaction is one of the more surprising scenes in her writing. She was glad. She wrote that her soul was flooded with joy, because she took it as a first call from Jesus on the anniversary of his death. She wrote that the next night brought the same sign.

In her own account she told the prioress of her hope of Heaven at the chapter of pardons that morning, and added that she was not suffering. Over a year later, on 30 May 1897, with the prioress’s permission, she told Mother Agnes the secret of that night. Mother Agnes understood then that the disease was an old one.

A second event followed the first. In the joyful days of the Easter season of 1896, her inner life changed. The consolation she had felt in prayer left her. She described it as a fog. She asked her reader to suppose a person born in a land of thick fogs. She wrote that she sat at the table of bitterness where poor sinners take their food.

This is the night of faith that she endured for the rest of her life, about a year and a half. She did not lose her trust. She lost the feeling of it. She went on acting as if she believed, in prayers and in work, without any comfort. She also did not hide it. Her sisters heard her say that she had no consolation, and that her joys lay only in the depths of her soul. She asked that this be told after her death, so that other souls would not be discouraged.

The name Little Flower suggests sweetness. Her own words show a woman who was often in the dark. She kept up her cheerfulness by choice. On one occasion she said that she acted cheerful when she could, because it gave pleasure to others.

In June 1897 the prioress, Mother Marie de Gonzague, asked her to write about her life in religion. Thérèse started on the second or third of June. By the fourth she had put everything else aside. She wrote in a black notebook, and she knew that these pages would be used when it was time to tell the community that she had died. It became her third manuscript. She did not finish it.

A Promise of Roses

The last months are the best recorded of her life. Mother Agnes took notes of what Thérèse said, in pencil, on loose sheets. She did this from May to September 1897. The notes were later published in English as Novissima Verba. They are not neutral. Mother Agnes was a sister, an editor, and a person who wanted her sister to be a saint. Still, they give us dates and scenes that no one else recorded.

On 27 May Thérèse said that she welcomed the circular letter that the convent would send out on her death, and that she did not understand sisters who refused one. On 5 June she said that if they found her dead one morning, they should not worry, because the good God had come to fetch her. On 9 June she told her sister Marie that she would let fall a shower of roses after her death.

At the start of July the bleeding returned. The hemorrhages came two or three times a day for a month. On 8 July she was moved to the infirmary. On 9 July the priest in charge, Canon Maupas, came to consider giving her the last sacrament. She stayed so cheerful that the sacrament was put off. She told him that she had not earned her crown, because God had earned it for her, and she smiled. She received the last sacraments the next day.

The doctor who looked after her was Dr. de Cornière. He said that her suffering was dreadful. He was amazed at her patience and at her smile. Thérèse would not accept the praise. She denied that she was patient. Later she told a sister that she was no angel, because angels cannot suffer. She said also that she suffered only from moment to moment, and that discouragement comes from looking at the past or the future.

On 15 July a dead ember from a lamp lit the candle of her cousin Marie Guérin. Thérèse saw a picture in it. One small light could light all the candles, she said, and this was how hidden souls stood behind the saints and the Doctors of the Church. Two days later, on 17 July, Mother Agnes recorded the sentence that became her best-known promise.

“Je veux passer mon Ciel à faire du bien sur la terre.”

— Thérèse of Lisieux, 17 July 1897, as recorded by Mother Agnes (French from the 1911 edition; in English: I want to spend my Heaven doing good on earth)

She knew how it sounded. A sister said she would look down on them from Heaven. Thérèse answered that she would come down. To a novice she said that if her little way led anyone wrong, she would return to earth and tell them to take another road.

She kept her jokes to the last. When the priest talked of postponing the last sacrament, she said she did not know the trade of winning one’s point. She told her sisters that she had said certain things only to frighten them. She corrected a novice for sitting sideways on a chair. She had been, for the novices, something like a teacher, and she stayed one while she was dying.

She also named her own days of trial. She said her Sundays and feast days were the days when God tried her most.

“Do you know which are my Sundays and feast-days? They are the days on which God tries me most.”

— Thérèse of Lisieux, in Counsels and Reminiscences, recorded by a novice

Other sayings of those weeks show a person who would not play the part that others wanted. She said that she would not wish to die right after Communion, because little souls could not copy that. She looked at the crucifix and said she preferred to see Christ dead, because then he suffers no more. She asked a sister not to lament by her bed. She did not care for news of the world. When the sisters spoke of the reception of the Czar in France, she wanted to talk of God. She asked not to cough so that Céline could sleep, and she told the Virgin that she would love her even if refused.

On 19 August she received her last Communion. Mother Agnes’s pencil notes give that date. The printed autobiography says 16 August, and Mother Agnes says it is wrong. In the last weeks Thérèse said she feared for her mind. She did not want to be praised for her virtues. She was afraid, she said, of a proud thought, of saying to herself that she had acquired a virtue.

On 6 September she received a relic of Blessed Théophane Vénard, and told his story. On 8 September, the anniversary of her profession, a robin flew into the infirmary and sat on her bed. A sister brought wildflowers. On 14 September she stripped the petals from a rose over her crucifix. She asked the sisters to keep them, and said they would give pleasure later. Those petals would have a place in the story after her death.

The Last Evening

On the eve of her death, 29 September, At midday Thérèse asked the prioress whether this was the agony, and how she should set about dying. When Mother Agnes read the prayers for the dying and came to the mention of the devils, Thérèse made a mock threat with her hand. To Céline, who asked for a word of farewell, she whispered that all was said and accomplished, and that love alone counted.

On 30 September, at about three in the afternoon, she lay with her arms out in the shape of a cross. the prioress placed a picture of Our Lady of Mount Carmel on her knees. That afternoon, in Mother Agnes’s record, she said that she had never sought anything but the truth, and that she did not repent of having given herself up to Love. At about five the agony began. The community gathered in the infirmary. Céline put a piece of ice to her lips, and Thérèse looked at her.

Shortly after seven, the prioress sent the sisters away, because she thought the end was far off. Thérèse asked whether it was not yet the agony. The prioress answered that it was, but that it might last some hours. In Mother Agnes’s record Thérèse replied that she did not wish to suffer less. Then she looked at her crucifix. The words that the 1912 English edition gives as her last are these.

“Oh! I love Him! My God, I love Thee!”

— Thérèse of Lisieux, last words, 30 September 1897, as recorded by Mother Agnes

She fell back, her head a little to one side. The sisters thought it was over, and the prioress had the infirmary bell rung to call the community. The doors were opened. The sisters came in and knelt around the bed. In Mother Agnes’s account they saw a last ecstasy that lasted for the length of a Credo, and her face was like a lily. About twenty past seven she breathed her last sigh.

Those who were in the room recorded two details that have been repeated ever since. Mother Agnes says that birds sang in the tree outside through the whole of the agony, and that the sky cleared at dusk. The sisters confirm the birds. The Church makes no miracle claim about it. It is what the people in the room remembered.

When the sisters washed the body, they thought she looked twelve or thirteen years old. On 4 October she was buried in the municipal cemetery of Lisieux, in the new Carmelite plot. Taylor says she was the first Carmelite buried there. A palm branch was put in her hand. Mother Agnes noted that the limbs stayed supple until the burial.

The community now had a duty. A Carmel sent a circular letter on the death of a sister, and that letter had a history of its own.

A Book Goes Out

The circular letter of a Carmel is a short life of the dead sister, sent to other convents. On 30 May 1897 Thérèse told Mother Agnes the secret of her first hemorrhage, and Mother Agnes saw that the disease was old. The plan for a circular began there. It grew into a book.

The book appeared in 1898 as Histoire d’une âme, in English Story of a Soul. It was made from the three manuscripts: the childhood memories, the letter to Marie, and the unfinished black notebook. Mother Agnes and others edited the text. The Carmel of Lisieux now shows the 1898 pages beside the original, and the comparison shows additions and removed sentences.

The effect was quick. The Carmel counted more than a million French copies by 1926. An English version by Dziewicki appeared in 1901. A shorter English text by Taylor, from 1911, reached 175,000 copies by 1926. The first English edition of Soeur Thérèse came out at Christmas 1912, in 9,000 copies. In 1997 Pope John Paul II noted that the book had been translated into about fifty languages.

Pilgrims came to her grave in the municipal cemetery. Taylor, who wrote a life of her in 1927, gave the figure of about 80,000 a year between 1898 and 1910. Some came barefoot. A saying of the time compared praying there to praying at Lourdes. The Carmel kept a selection of the favors reported, and it filled seven volumes.

Some of these reports reach back to the night she died. The community told of a lay sister who kissed Thérèse’s feet and was cured, it was said, of cerebral anemia. One nun smelled violets. A child of ten smelled lilies, although only paper lilies stood in the room. The Carmel recorded these stories. The Church did not investigate them, and they belong to popular tradition.

The petals she had asked the sisters to keep also entered the story. In September 1910, at the hospital of the Little Sisters of the Poor in Lisieux, Ferdinand Aubry had cancer of the tongue. Mother Agnes reports in a footnote that a rose petal from 1897 cured him. The Carmel reported this. It was not one of the miracles the Church chose for her beatification.

The 1912 book also carried words that her sisters recorded near the end. Mother Agnes gave one of them in her closing chapter.

“I have never given the Good God aught but love; it is with Love He will repay.”

— Thérèse of Lisieux, in Soeur Thérèse of Lisieux, 1912 edition

Her sisters outlived her by decades. Her sister Marie died in 1940, and Léonie, who had become a Visitation nun at Caen, in 1944. Mother Agnes lived until 1951. Céline was still alive in 1952. But a readership had already grown. In 1907 Bishop Lemonnier of Bayeux and Lisieux asked the Carmelites to write down their memories of her, and he approved a prayer for her beatification.

The question was no longer whether people loved her. It was whether the Church could prove that she was what they said.

Testing the Witnesses

The Church does not declare a saint because a book is popular. A cause must be opened, witnesses must be heard under oath, and the life must be examined for heroic virtue. In January 1909 the cause received a postulator, Father Rodrigue of St Francis of Paula, a Discalced Carmelite. Monsignor de Teil was named vice-postulator in Paris. In the spring of 1910 the bishop ordered a search for her writings, and the court that examined them sat from February to June.

The diocesan tribunal for her life was formed on 3 August 1910 and opened on 12 August. Historian Claude Langlois counts 93 sessions and 48 witnesses, who answered 30 questions. Three sisters were heard in August and September 1910. The others were heard in February and March 1911. Only about half of them had known her.

The witnesses did not agree with one another. Mother Agnes had taken notes of the last weeks and had edited the book. Marie of the Sacred Heart, her godmother, was judged by Langlois the most sober and least calculated of the deponents. Céline, now Sister Geneviève of the Holy Face, gave testimony that he calls essential, too abundant, too sure, and close to Mother Agnes’s aims. The younger novices said that what mattered was how the young sisters had seen her every day. One of them, Marie of the Trinity, came into open conflict with Geneviève.

The process also shows how a testimony is made. Monsignor de Teil drew up a list of articles in April 1910, and the sisters prepared their depositions with it. The list records the faults of her childhood, such as stubbornness and pride at the age of three. Mother Agnes asked how she should cite Thérèse’s words. Langlois uses this to show how the testimony was built.

Meanwhile the body of the saint was examined. On 6 September 1910 the tribunal opened the grave for the first time. Only bones remained. The palm branch that had been placed in her hand in 1897 was fresh and whole. The remains were put in a cemented vault in the middle of the Carmelite plot. A popular story says that on the night before, Thérèse spoke to the prioress at Gallipoli in Italy, Mother Carmela, and said that they would find only her bones. Taylor reports it. It is a tradition, and there is no deposition behind it.

In 1911 a second process asked whether there had been any public cult of her before the Church had ruled. Sixteen witnesses were heard, and the diocesan process closed at Bayeux on 12 December 1911. In Rome the cause was handed to a critic. From January to April 1914 Monsignor Verde served as Promoter of the Faith, the official whose duty is to raise objections. Anne and Claude Langlois have translated his objections for the first time.

His doubts were sharp. He said she was admired mostly through her own book. He said that her humility was not heroic. He said that her words on her deathbed about relics and roses did not fit a humble person. He said that Lisieux had run a publicity campaign. Such questions are the reason the office exists.

On 10 June 1914 Pope Pius X signed the decree that introduced the cause. Another source gives the day as 9 June, and the later narratives use the 10th. The war then began, and the war changed the process. The apostolic process opened on 17 March 1915 in the sacristy of Bayeux cathedral. About twenty witnesses were heard. It closed on 30 October 1917. Among the witnesses was the priest who had heard her last confession.

The judges did not examine her reputation for holiness in this process. Soldiers at the front had sent petitions for her beatification, and their petitions made the point plain. She was called the little sister of the trenches. The petitions from England and Canada were the most striking. Benedict XV would later say, according to Monsignor de Teil, that English-speaking devotion showed the finger of God.

The second exhumation took place on 9 and 10 August 1917. Two medical experts identified the bones. The event was meant to be private, but thousands came. The bones were put into an oak chest inside a lead-lined rosewood chest. The record of the trial came to about 2,500 pages, and about 2,000 of them concerned her virtue. Rome confirmed on 10 December 1918 that the documents were valid. Benedict XV then waived the usual wait of fifty years.

A dark thread ran through the testimony. Langlois reports that in 1917 Mother Agnes wrote a document about the place where Thérèse had become a saint, and that it accused the former prioress, Marie de Gonzague. He says it fed a dark legend that ran against the official story. Marie de Gonzague had died before the trials began, and she could not answer. The research did not reach Mother Agnes’s own text or Gonzague’s side. On 14 August 1921 Benedict XV declared that Thérèse had lived the virtues to a heroic degree. She was now Venerable. In the same speech he spoke about her way of spiritual childhood. The next question was the miracles.

Miracles the Church Weighed

A beatification needs miracles, and the Church asks doctors to judge them. Six cures were examined in the cause, and two were chosen. The other four are not named in the sources that the research could reach. The two that were kept had each been reviewed by three doctors.

The first was the cure of Charles Anne. In 1906 he was a seminarian of twenty-three at the seminary of Bayeux, from Lisieux. He had tuberculosis of the lung, with cavities and hemorrhage. A novena to Our Lady of Lourdes, made through Thérèse, had failed. So a second novena was made to Thérèse alone, to make her part clear. After a severe hemorrhage he cried out a sentence that the accounts preserve.

“I did not come here to die! I came to work for God! You must cure me!”

— Charles Anne, seminarian, Bayeux, 1906, as reported by T. N. Taylor

He fell asleep holding a relic that, in the account, came mysteriously into his hands. He woke cured. An X-ray taken fifteen years later showed that the cure had held. The Church approved it as a miracle. An odd detail follows. The superior of that seminary, Father Duboscq, later served as Promoter of the Faith in the cause, according to Taylor. The man who had been Anne’s superior was asked to raise objections to her case. Anne attended the beatification in 1923.

The second case was a nun in the Daughters of the Cross at Ustarritz in the Basses-Pyrénées. One source calls her Sister Aloysia of St Germain, and Taylor and the Latin decree call her Louise. She had suffered since 1913 from a stomach ulcer with hemorrhage, and in 1915 she received the last sacraments. In September 1916 a novena was made, and a scent came and went in the room. On the night of 10 September she said that Thérèse appeared to her and promised that she would be cured. The two accounts give slightly different words. In the morning rose petals of many colors lay on the floor by her bed. The sources disagree about the day of the cure. One gives 22 September, and the other says she woke cured on 25 September. The surgeon Dr. Le Bec of Paris reported on her, and X-rays were taken. The roses in this story appear in a painting in St Peter’s.

The Church approved both miracles on 11 February 1923, the feast of Lourdes. The general congregation had met on 30 January with the Pope present. The final decree followed on 19 March. She could now be beatified.

Two more cures were approved for her canonization. The first was in Parma. Sister Gabriel Trimusi, of the Sacred Hearts congregation, had had a swollen left knee since 1913, when she was twenty-three. She had broken firewood against it. The swelling spread to her spine. She was treated in a hospital with light therapy. The doctors found tuberculosis of the knee and of the dorsal spine. She began a novena on 13 June 1923, before a small picture. On its last day, at a triduum at the Carmel, she walked there in great pain. That evening she knelt without pain. Later she ran from her room crying that she was cured. The doctors said that neither chronic synovitis nor spondylitis had a natural cure. The Promoter argued that the cure counted as two miracles because there were two diseases. The Congregation declined, and accepted it as one, but a stronger one.

The second was Marie Pellemans, a Belgian, in March 1923. One source spells her first name Mary. She had tuberculosis of the lungs and intestines from October 1909. She had been in a sanatorium at La Hulpe, and in 1920 she went to Lourdes, without result. She then went on pilgrimage to the Carmel of Lisieux. She knelt at the tomb, asked for Thérèse’s help, and was cured at once. Three doctors examined her. The Church approved the case.

Not everything that was said about Thérèse passed through this process. Some biographies say that in 1922 the seven-year-old Édith Piaf regained her sight after a pilgrimage to the grave. The only source in the research is a secondary one, and the account cannot be stated as fact. A printed letter of 1923 tells of a man who had planned a murder and then his own death. He heard her words about spending Heaven doing good on earth, went into Notre-Dame des Victoires in Paris, saw them on the wall of the choir, and repented. The letter is anonymous. It is a recorded testimony, without any investigation.

These accounts are given in full in the miracles section of this page, with their status. They differ in weight. Some passed the Church’s examination, some are records kept by the Carmel, and some are popular tradition. A reader can see the difference in each entry.

Roses in St Peter’s

On 25 March 1923 the remains were moved from the cemetery to the Carmel. More than two hundred priests took part, and about 50,000 pilgrims walked behind the rosewood casket. Taylor, who was there, reports that when the vault was opened a scent of roses filled the air. It is the report of one attendee, and it was not examined.

The Pope who beatified her was Pius XI. The ceremony took place on 29 April 1923, and it was the first beatification of his reign. He is reported to have called her the star of his pontificate. The same pope would canonize her himself.

The canonization took place on 17 May 1925 in St Peter’s Basilica. She had been dead twenty-eight years. Taylor, who described it as a witness, counts 33 cardinals and about 200 prelates, and a procession of about 4,000. Some 60,000 people were inside the basilica and about 500,000 in the square by evening. Taylor reports that a wireless relay carried the Pope’s voice, which he calls a first at a canonization. He says the crowd broke into applause at the end of the formula, also a first.

During the Pope’s homily, Taylor reports, five roses fell from a cluster of lights in the apse and landed at his feet. This is an eyewitness report from someone who attended. The Church makes no claim about it. Taylor adds that the night facade of the basilica looked like a giant rose of fire. The old torches and oil lamps of the dome had been lit again at the Pope’s wish. The Pope called her, in Taylor’s record, a miracle of virtues and a prodigy of miracles.

The next day French pilgrims met Pius XI. He spoke to them of a deluge of glory on Lisieux and of his dear little Saint. On 30 September 1925, the anniversary of her death, Lisieux held its first feast after the canonization. The papal legate, Cardinal Vico, put a golden rose, blessed by the Pope, into the hand of the effigy. The reliquary was carried through streets hung with roses. An aviator flew over the procession and showered it with roses.

Here is the answer to the promise made in the infirmary. She had said that she would spend her Heaven doing good on earth. She had said that she would let fall a shower of roses. In 1925 the Church recognized her, a crowd of half a million stood in a square, and an aircraft dropped flowers on a street in Lisieux. The Church does not say that any of these flowers were a sign. It does say that she is a saint, and that the people who asked for her to be one were right.

The objections of Monsignor Verde had been heard, and the Church had decided. Soldiers, doctors, and sick people had told their own stories by then. The question of whether her humility was heroic had been asked, and the Church had answered it.

After the Roses

In 1927 her feast was added to the General Roman Calendar for 3 October. On 14 December 1927 Pius XI named her patron of the missions, together with Francis Xavier, at the request of missionary bishops. The nun who had spent her religious life in one convent in Lisieux was now their patron. In 1944 Pius XII named her co-patron of France with Joan of Arc. The feast has been on 1 October in the General Roman Calendar since 1969. Other patronages listed in reference works include florists and gardeners, aviators, the sick, orphans, and the homeless.

The city of Lisieux built a basilica. Work began in 1929 and the crypt was finished in 1932. In 1944 bombing destroyed two-thirds of Lisieux, and the basilica had only minor damage. The sources disagree on the date of its consecration, 1951 or 1954. Pope John Paul II visited on 2 June 1980. The Carmel holds her remains. The effigy of her, based on drawings and photographs, contains her ribs and other remains.

In 1997, for the hundredth anniversary of her death, the Church took a further step. The bishop of Bayeux and Lisieux, the Superior General of the Carmelites, and the postulator asked on 8 March that she be named a Doctor of the Church. Consultors gave their judgment that her doctrine was eminent. The cardinals and bishops voted unanimously in June. Pope John Paul II announced his decision at the Angelus during World Youth Day in Paris on 24 August. On 19 October, World Mission Sunday, he proclaimed her Doctor of the Universal Church in St Peter’s Square. His apostolic letter was called Divini Amoris Scientia, the science of divine love. He wrote that the recognition of her holiness had been quick, universal, and constant.

Her parents followed. The Church beatified Louis and Zélie Martin at Lisieux on 19 October 2008, after a cure that the Archbishop of Milan had accepted in 2004. It was the cure of Pietro Schiliro, born in 2002, who had a lung disorder. A second miracle was the case of Carmen Pérez Pons, a premature girl in Valencia with a brain hemorrhage. On 18 October 2015 they were canonized together as a married couple. Her sister Léonie has been named a Servant of God, and a diocesan inquiry opened on 2 July 2015.

Her relics now travel. They began to tour the world in 1994. They went to Ireland and Canada in 2001. In September and October 2009 they went through England and Wales. On 1 October 2009 the casket spent a night in York Minster, an Anglican church, and about a quarter of a million people are reported to have venerated the relics on that tour. In South Africa they toured during the World Cup of 2010. In 2008 the astronaut Ron Garan carried a relic aboard the space shuttle Discovery. The National Shrine of St Thérèse in Darien, Illinois, is said to hold the largest relic collection outside Lisieux.

Whatever the merits of the stories, the promise was in her own voice. Near the end of her life she told the novices that if her little way led them wrong she would come back. She said it plainly, like a teacher.

“Should I guide you wrongly by my little way of love, do not fear that I shall allow you to continue therein; I should soon come back to the earth”

— Thérèse of Lisieux, Counsels and Reminiscences

Her book has been translated into about fifty languages, and the Church, which once asked whether she was heroic, now names her a teacher. A woman who asked to be treated like a ball kicked into a corner has a basilica, a feast, and a pair of saints for parents.

How This Life Was Researched

This account draws on three sets of research notes that list more than a dozen sources in English and French. They include her autobiography in the 1912 English and French editions, Mother Agnes’s notes of her last weeks, the archives of the Carmel of Lisieux, and Taylor’s and Giloteaux’s books from the 1920s. The Carmel’s transcripts of her three manuscripts were used to check the quotations and scenes from her own writing. Some quotations come from the edited 1912 text, as labeled. They also include John Paul II’s 1997 letter. The diocesan court in Lisieux held 93 sessions and heard 48 witnesses, according to Langlois. Some sources could not be reached, and the most important were the French critical edition of her last conversations and the full depositions. Where sources disagree, the text says so.
