In the spring of 1934 a young monk came up to Belgrade from a seminary in the south of Yugoslavia. On a streetcar he met a woman he knew, and he told her he was in the city by mistake. A letter from the bishops had come in his name, he explained, but they must have meant some other monk called John. The next day she saw him again, and he looked puzzled. “The mistake turned out to be bigger than I thought,” he said. “It is me they have appointed.” They meant to make him a bishop and send him to China. He protested that he could not even speak clearly. They answered that the Prophet Moses had had the same trouble.1

He went. For the next thirty-two years he followed a scattered people wherever they had to flee. He went with them to Shanghai and to a refugee camp on an island in the Philippines. For their sake he appeared before a committee of the United States Senate, and then he went on to France and at last to California. He spent himself on the daily liturgy, on the sick and the prisoners, and on children he gathered from the streets. Politics he would not touch. Orthodox Christians in many lands now know him as the shepherd of the Russian exiles, and they turn to him in sickness and in trouble.2

Saint John Grows Up in a Noble Family

Saint John was born Michael Maximovitch on 4 June 1896 in the village of Adamovka, in the province of Kharkov, in what is now eastern Ukraine. He was baptized with the name of the Archangel Michael, and at home they called him Misha. His father Boris was marshal of the nobility for the district, the leader whom the local noble families elected. His mother was Glafira. The family was old and known for its faith. In the eighteenth century it had given the Church Saint John of Tobolsk, the bishop who sent the first Orthodox mission to China.3

He was a sickly child who ate little. He got on well with everyone, yet he had no close friends. Animals he loved, dogs most of all. He kept away from noisy games and was often lost in his own thoughts. His toy soldiers became monks, and his toy forts became monasteries. Over the years he collected icons and books of history and religion until he had a large library. More than anything he loved the lives of the saints. His brothers and his sister learned those lives from him.4

In summer the family lived on its country estate, twelve kilometers from the great Svyatogorsk monastery on the Donets River. His parents went to the monastery often and sometimes stayed for a long time. When the boy passed through its gates, he stepped with delight into the world of the monks. There were great churches, many caves, small hermitages, and a high hill called Mount Tabor. Six hundred monks lived there. He went back again and again.5

As he grew, he thought he would serve his country as an officer or an official. At eleven he entered the Petrovsky Cadet Corps at Poltava, a school for future officers. There he stayed quiet and religious, and he looked very little like a soldier. Later he said that it was at that school that he began to feel he had to choose another road.6

In 1909 Poltava marked two hundred years since its great battle, and the cadets marched through the city in a parade grander than usual. The boy was thirteen. As the column passed the cathedral, he turned toward it and crossed himself, and he alone took off his cap.7

For a long time afterward the other boys laughed at him. His officers charged him with a breach of order and punished him. Then an officer of higher rank stepped in, and the punishment became a written word of praise for the boy’s healthy religious feeling. The boys who had laughed at him came to respect him.8

Saint John Studies Law and Finds His Teacher

When he was fifteen, his Catholic governess from France was baptized into the Orthodox Church. The boy’s life had made a strong impression on her. He helped her prepare and taught her the prayers.9

He finished cadet school in 1914 and wanted to go on to the Theological Academy in Kiev. His parents insisted that he study law instead. He gave way to them “for obedience’s sake” and entered the law faculty of the University of Kharkov. It was the first great choice of his life, and he made it by giving up what he wanted.10

When he was named a bishop twenty years later, he began his address with Christ’s call to His first apostles: “Follow Me, and I will make you fishers of men.” Then he looked back at his own life.11

“As a small child I did not think that the same call would ever come to me, though from the first days I became aware of myself, I wanted to serve justice and truth. My parents kindled in me a longing to stand firm for the truth, and my soul was captivated by the examples of those who gave their lives for it…”

— Saint John, address at his naming as Bishop of Shanghai, Belgrade, 1934 (our translation)12

On the very day he finished cadet school, a new archbishop came to Kharkov. This was Anthony Khrapovitsky, one of the great churchmen of his time. He took special trouble to get to know the young man, and from then on he kept him close. Saint John said later that Anthony became the guide of his spiritual life forever.13

At the university he read the lives of the saints more than he went to lectures, and he still did very well. Other students called religion “grandmothers’ tales.” He found more wisdom in the lives of the saints than in the whole university course. He said later that while he studied the sciences of this world, he went deeper and deeper into “the science of sciences,” the life of the spirit. The archbishop’s monastery and its church drew him more than the place where he studied law.14

Beneath the altar of one of the city’s monasteries was a painted cave. There lay Archbishop Saint Meletius, who had died in 1841. He had been an ascetic, a man who gives up comfort and rest for prayer. He went without sleep and spent whole nights standing still in prayer with his arms raised. He knew in advance the day and hour of his death. Those who had the prayers for the dead sung at his coffin received help from him. The young student held him in great honor.15

Then came the revolution, and the Russian state fell. Saint John took his law degree in 1918. He served in the provincial court while Hetman Skoropadsky ruled Ukraine. When General Denikin’s army took Kharkov, he served in the court again. He also sat on the council of a parish. But the fall of his country had settled something in him for good. At his naming as bishop he put it in one sentence.16

“The collapse of the might of our country’s state convinced me once and for all that everything earthly is frail and that human strength and gifts are weak, and I decided to renounce the vain cares of this world and give myself to the service of God alone.”

— Saint John, address at his naming as Bishop of Shanghai, 1934 (our translation)17

As the White armies fell back, he left with his family for the Crimea. In November 1920 they were evacuated across the Black Sea to Constantinople.18

Saint John Becomes a Monk in Exile

In 1921 the family reached the new Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes, which would later be called Yugoslavia. Saint John and his brothers entered the University of Belgrade. One brother became an engineer, and the other studied law and served in the Yugoslav police. Saint John chose theology. Later he explained why: “Even before my outward tie with the world was finally cut, a thirst for theology drew me to the school whose patron is the great Saint Sava, and then onto the path he showed.”19

He was poor. A small allowance from the kingdom’s commission for Russian refugees helped him, and he sold newspapers to live. When the rains came, the streets of Belgrade turned to deep mud. A fellow student remembered him as a short, heavy young man with broad shoulders. He would come late into the lecture hall in a thick fur coat and old Russian boots, spattered with mud. Without hurry he drew a greasy notebook and the stub of a pencil from inside his coat and wrote the lecture down in a big hand. Before long he fell asleep. As soon as he woke, he went back to writing. Many of the students wondered what kind of notes came out of all this, but no one dared to ask him. He kept to himself, yet he gave that fellow student the feeling of a great strength held within.20

His old teacher had come into exile as well. Metropolitan Anthony now led the Synod of Bishops, the council that governed the Russian Church Outside Russia. In 1924 he gave Saint John the first rank of church service: he made him a reader, who reads the Scriptures aloud at the services. This happened in the Russian Church of the Holy Trinity in Belgrade. Saint John finished his studies in October 1925 and went to teach religion at a state school in the town of Velika Kikinda.21

In 1926 Metropolitan Anthony made him a monk at the Milkovo monastery, which is dedicated to the Entrance of the Mother of God into the Temple. In those years it was the only men’s monastery in Serbia where Russian monks kept the Russian order of monastic life. He took the name John, in honor of his distant relative Saint John of Tobolsk. That year Anthony made him a deacon. Another bishop ordained him a priest on 4 December, the feast of the Entrance.22

In 1927 he wrote to the pupils he had taught for two school years. He was sorry not to be their teacher any longer, and he remembered each one of them. He told them not to skip church now that he was not there to watch them. They went to church for God, who sees who comes and with what heart. Then he gave them a rule to keep: “Remember: Whoever quarrels consoles the devil, and whoever makes peace with others helps Christ and will be accepted into the Heavenly Kingdom.”23

That same year the Serbian Church sent him to teach pastoral theology and church history at the seminary of Saint John the Theologian in Bitol, in the diocese of Ohrid. Four or five hundred young men studied there. He also looked after the hospitals and orphanages around the town. He wrote against Sophiology, a new teaching about the Wisdom of God that the theologian Sergius Bulgakov was spreading. The bishop of Ohrid was Saint Nikolai Velimirovich, a great Serbian preacher. He loved the young monk, and more than once he said, “If you want to see a living saint, go to Bitol, to Father John.”24

It was his students who first found out how he lived. They noticed that he stayed up long after everyone else had gone to bed. At night he walked through the dormitories, picked up the blankets that had slipped to the floor, covered the sleepers again, and made the sign of the cross over them. In the end they learned that he hardly slept at all, and never in a bed. He allowed himself an hour or two a night, sitting up or bent over on the floor in prayer before the icons. Later he said himself that since he had taken his vows as a monk, he had not slept lying in a bed. One of those students remembered him this way.25

“Father John loved us all, and we loved him. … Every night, like a guardian angel, he watched over us. He straightened one boy’s pillow and another boy’s blanket.”

— A student of the Bitol seminary (our translation)26

Saint John Is Made Bishop Against His Will

On 3 June 1934 the Synod named him Bishop of Shanghai, an assistant bishop in the diocese of China and Peking. At the service of his naming in Belgrade, he spoke to the bishops who would consecrate him. He did not pretend to be ready.27

“I do not dare to think myself worthy of this rank, knowing my sinfulness. Yet I am afraid to refuse it, for I hear the Lord’s words to Peter, who had sinned and repented: ‘If you love Me … feed My lambs … feed My sheep.’”

— Saint John, address at his naming as Bishop of Shanghai, 1934 (our translation)28

Then he told them what a shepherd of souls must be, and he did not make it sound easy. He said that a shepherd often meets misunderstanding, and sometimes deliberate resistance and even hatred “from those you love and care for.” A bishop “has no personal life.” He must give himself wholly to saving souls and leading them into the Kingdom of Heaven. He must be ready to bear malice, persecution, and death itself for the truth. When sheep stray, he must go out after them and carry them back on his shoulders. “You cannot preach the Gospel without showing love in deeds,” he said. And a clergyman “does not dare turn into an ordinary political or public figure.”29

He knew where he was being sent: “to the land from which the visible sun rises, but which needs to be enlightened by the rays of the spiritual Sun of Righteousness.” His strength was weak, he said. Yet out of obedience to the Church and to his spiritual guide he accepted and gave his whole self to its service. He asked God to help him and strengthen him “to struggle for the truth until death.”30

On 10 June 1934 Metropolitan Anthony consecrated him bishop in the Russian Church of the Holy Trinity in Belgrade. It was the church where he had been made a reader ten years before. Anthony was very old by then, and Saint John was the last of the many bishops he consecrated. The old man had himself been invited to retire to China. Instead he wrote to Archbishop Dimitry in the Far East that he was too old to travel, and he sent Saint John in his own place.31

“As my soul, as my heart, I send you Bishop John. This small man, weak in body, almost like a child to look at, is a miracle of ascetic firmness and strictness in our age of general spiritual slackness.”

— Metropolitan Anthony, letter to Archbishop Dimitry of Hailar, 1934 (our translation)32

So Saint John set out for the East. In Shanghai about fifty thousand Russians who had lost their homeland were waiting for a bishop. He had promised to have no personal life. Could a shepherd who kept nothing for himself hold together a flock that had lost everything?33

Saint John Comes to the Exiles of Shanghai

He reached Shanghai on 4 December 1934, the feast of the Entrance of the Mother of God into the Temple. He found the great cathedral unfinished and the Russian parishes quarreling among themselves. His first work was to make peace among them, and soon he did. He reached out to the Serbs, the Greeks, and the Ukrainians of the city as well. On the feasts each people loved best, he served and preached in Greek and in Serbian, and in Chinese too.34

His speech was still hard to understand. A voice teacher in Shanghai taught him how to breathe and how to shape his words, and at the end of every lesson he paid her twenty dollars. His people called him Vladyka, as Russians address a bishop.35

Every day he served the Divine Liturgy, the Church’s chief service of Holy Communion. He would do so for the rest of his life. When for some reason he could not serve, he still received Communion. He served in the cathedral morning and evening, even when he was sick. He ate once a day, at eleven at night. In the first and last weeks of Lent he ate nothing at all. For the rest of Lent and the fast before Christmas, he lived on the bread of the altar. He spent his nights in prayer. When his strength finally gave out, he would put his head down on the floor and sleep for a few hours before dawn.36

When it was time for the morning service, someone would knock at his door. Often no answer came. Then the door was opened. He lay huddled on the floor in the icon corner, overcome by sleep. At a touch on the shoulder he jumped up. A few minutes later he was in church and wide awake, with cold water streaming down his beard.37

He wore clothes of the cheapest cloth and light shoes without socks in every weather, and often he went barefoot. The rich never saw him at their tea tables. He went wherever there was need, at any hour of the day or night. Every day he visited the sick, heard their confessions, and gave them Communion. In the prisons he served the Divine Liturgy for the convicts on a rough little table. Outside the city stood a hospital for the mentally ill, and he went there too. The patients met him with joy, listened to him, and received Communion from him in peace. Each year he examined the children of every Russian school in the city on their knowledge of the faith.38

Under him the great cathedral was finished, and the church of Saint Nicholas was completed in 1935. A house of mercy, shelters for the homeless, homes for the old, a hospital, and a free canteen opened with his blessing. In 1936 he opened a house in Shanghai for fifteen nuns of the Convent of the Protection in Peking.39

Metropolitan Anthony reposed that same year at Sremski Karlovci in Yugoslavia, on 10 August 1936. In his last years he had not been able to stand or walk. Two years before, Saint John had called him the guide of his spiritual life forever.40

One day the bishop learned that a woman named Menshikova was dying of rabies. A mad dog had bitten her, and she had refused the injections or taken them carelessly. He came and gave her Holy Communion. Just then a fit of the disease seized her. Foam came to her lips, and she spat out the Holy Gifts, the Communion she had just received. The Holy Gifts may not be thrown away, so he picked them up and put them into his own mouth. Those who had come with him cried out, “Vladyka, what are you doing! Rabies is terribly contagious!” He answered them calmly: “Nothing will happen; these are the Holy Gifts.” And nothing did happen.41

Saint John Gathers Lost Children

In his Christmas message of 1935 he spoke to his people about the Good Shepherd, who leaves ninety-nine sheep on the mountains to look for the one that is lost. He asked each of them to bring to the Lord in the manger “some kind of good deed” from the heart.42

He went after the lost children himself, picking up sick and starving ones from the streets and from the dark alleys of the Shanghai slums. For them he opened a home for orphans and for the children of poor parents. He placed it under the protection of Saint Tikhon of Zadonsk, a saint who had loved children. It began with eight children.43

Marina Shakhmatova ran the orphanage for the bishop. One day the Shanghai newspapers reported that in the slums baby girls were being thrown into garbage cans, and that dogs were eating them. The bishop told her to buy two bottles of Chinese vodka. She was horrified. She was more horrified still when he said she must come with him into those slums. Everyone knew that grown men and women were murdered there. They went at night, through dark alleys, past drunkards and shady figures and growling dogs and cats. She carried the bottles behind him. In a doorway a drunk man sat growling, and from a garbage can nearby came the faint cry of a baby. The bishop hurried toward the cry, and the man growled a warning. “Hand me a bottle,” the bishop said to her. He raised it in one hand, and with the other he pointed at the can. He said nothing to the man. The drunk man got the bottle, and Shakhmatova took the child.44

The home of Saint Tikhon grew until it held as many as a hundred children at a time. Over the years about 3,500 Russian and Chinese children passed through it. He tried to take a father’s place for them, above all at Christmas and Easter, when parents try hardest to make their children happy. On those days he gave them Christmas trees and plays, and he found them musical instruments.45

One of the girls who grew up near him was Tatiana Urusova. She lived at Saint Olga’s orphanage in the convent, and from the age of ten to sixteen she helped the bishop put on his vestments for the services. At Christmas a nun would lead a group of the girls through the sleeping city to the cathedral after the night service at the convent. They carried a burning Christmas star, and they came to sing the praises of Christ and to greet their bishop. He always received them in his room, and he shone with joy. One by one they came up for his blessing, and each girl received a bag of sweets from the big baskets that stood there. They gave him woolen socks they had knitted for the feast. Later they were puzzled to see their socks on beggars.46

From the third-floor windows of the convent house, the girls could watch him walk down the street to Saint Tikhon’s in pouring rain, winter and summer. On the way he passed a House of Mercy for men, a home for old women, and a prison, and they knew he would stop at each one. Sometimes the convent bells rang on his way back to announce that he was coming to them. He came in soaked through, in sandals on his bare feet. In the church he kissed the altar and the wonderworking icons, and then he climbed the back stairs to the children. The girls sang him the Greek greeting for a bishop, “Many years, Master.” He asked them about their patron saints, sometimes about those saints’ lives, and about which Gospel had been read that day. He asked whether they had enough to eat. Food was very short in the war years, and the orphanage got its soup from a public canteen. He would take a spoon and taste it himself, straight from the pot.47

He could be strict with them. Yet whenever the children saw him walking through the city, they ran to him for his blessing.48

“We children felt light and joyful with Vladyka.”

— Tatiana Kennedy (Urusova), memories of the Shanghai orphanage49

In 1937 the Japanese army took Shanghai. The occupiers pressed the Russian colony to bend to their will, and two heads of the Russian Emigrant Committee who refused were killed. Fear and confusion gripped the colony. Russians who worked with the Japanese warned the bishop. But he declared himself the temporary head of the colony and kept it free to run its own affairs. Most people hurried home before dark, but he went on visiting the sick and the needy at any hour of the night, and no one ever harmed him.50

In the last months of the war a harder trial came. All contact with Western Europe was cut off, and the Russian bishops in China began to doubt that the Synod abroad still existed. In July 1945 they asked the new Patriarch of Moscow to take them under his care, and they urged Saint John to do the same. For a few weeks he had the Patriarch’s name prayed for in the churches of Shanghai. Then Metropolitan Anastasy, the head of the Synod, sent a short radio message from Geneva: the Synod was still at work. Saint John stayed with the Church that had sent him. He was the only Russian bishop in China who did.51

In January 1946 Archbishop Victor of Peking came to Shanghai and demanded that Saint John submit to Moscow. Saint John answered that he obeyed the Synod abroad and would do what it told him. The archbishop then forbade him to serve. Saint John would not accept the ban. He replied: “I will obey this decree only if someone proves to me, from Holy Scripture and from the law of any country, that breaking an oath is a virtue and keeping an oath is a grave sin.”52

In May 1946 the Synod made Shanghai a diocese of its own and raised him to archbishop. He had turned down the Synod’s offer of such a diocese eight years before, when the other bishops of China were absent. This time the news took him by surprise, but he accepted it. The Russians of Shanghai split. Up to ten thousand went with the Patriarch, and up to five thousand stayed with their archbishop. That October Archbishop Victor was arrested in Shanghai. Saint John at once demanded that the Chinese authorities set him free, and he went twice to see Archbishop Victor in prison.53

Three years later the Communist armies came near Shanghai, and the Russians of the city had to flee for the second time in their lives.54

Saint John Leads His People Out of China

The International Refugee Organization asked a number of governments to give the refugees shelter. Only the Philippines answered, and it offered an empty part of the island of Tubabao. Between January and May 1949 about five thousand Russians were taken there from Shanghai, and the whole orphanage went with them. In the camp they lived in poverty and want. They set up three churches. The church they called their cathedral stood in an old army barracks, and its bells were made from metal barrels. There were also a children’s home and a school.55

A few days before Easter their archbishop arrived. One of the refugees remembered the day.56

“Vladyka John of Shanghai came to Tubabao in a jeep and went straight to the Cathedral of the Mother of God. … The cathedral was packed with people who revered Vladyka.”

— A. N. Knyazev, a refugee on Tubabao (our translation)57

After a prayer service and a cup of tea he went on to the church of Saint Seraphim, where the bells rang and the clergy and a choir met him. When a short service there was over, he went on to the church of the Archangel Michael, where again the bells rang and a third choir sang. He stayed about three months. He gave himself to the spiritual needs of his flock and came to know their daily life in the camp. The island lay in the path of the typhoons. When a Russian spoke to the Filipinos of his fear, they told him there was no reason to worry: “your holy man blesses your camp from four directions every night.”58

Tubabao could only be a temporary shelter. On 12 July 1949 he left the island and his flock and set out for Washington. He went to ask the American Congress to let his people settle in the United States. He also gathered money for the refugees and began to look for homes for them.59

On 6 September he reached Washington and began to meet congressmen and officials. The Senate Judiciary Committee was weighing a bill that would let the Russian refugees into the country, and it invited him to come with his translator, Nikolai Alexandrov. After the hearing he wrote, “Today is a very important day in our case.” Senator William Knowland of California made a report. Then the archbishop was questioned in detail about the Russian colony of Shanghai since 1920 and about the number of refugees on Tubabao.60

But the bill stalled. President Truman wanted it, but a group of senators stood against it. The months went by. While he waited, the archbishop founded a Russian parish in Washington and named it for Saint John the Forerunner. He made more trips to the capital. He later wrote that Senator Knowland ran the last session “like a guard standing at his post.” The bill passed the Senate on Holy Thursday of 1950, three days before Easter.61

Saint John Serves the Orthodox of the West

The House of Representatives followed, and on 16 June 1950 President Truman signed the law. His statement said that special provisions were made for “4,000 European refugees who fled to the Far East to escape one form of totalitarianism and must now flee before a new tyranny.” From 1951 the people of Tubabao began to leave the island for the United States and Australia, and almost the whole camp came to the New World. The orphans of Saint Tikhon’s went to San Francisco.62

The archbishop himself now had no diocese. On 27 November 1950 the Council of Bishops named him Archbishop of Western Europe. He kept the care of the last parishes of his Chinese diocese, in Hong Kong, Singapore, and other places. In 1953 the bishops gave him the further title of head of the Russian Orthodox churches in China and the Philippines.63

He reached Paris on 21 July 1951 and lived at first in Meudon. The Russian churches of Paris were under other church authorities, so his seat as bishop was in Brussels. His title was Archbishop of Brussels and Western Europe. In Brussels he served in the church of Saint Job, built in memory of the last Tsar, and there he usually kept Easter. In 1952 he moved to Versailles and lived in the building of the Russian Cadet Corps, where he chaired the school’s board. As a boy he had been a cadet himself. Once again he lived among the children he loved.64

He was always on the road, through the countries of Western Europe and as far as Tunisia. Wherever he went, he visited hospitals and prisons and the camps for displaced persons. Help for the Russian refugees was still among his first cares. In church he served the Divine Liturgy in French, in Dutch, and in English. The small Orthodox communities of France and the Netherlands came under his care. He encouraged them to train priests of their own and to print the services in their own languages. For the Orthodox Church of France he consecrated its first bishop of modern times, Jean-Nectaire Kovalevsky.65

He also went looking for the saints of the West. Before the Churches of Rome and the East divided, the West had many saints of its own, honored only in their own lands and missing from the Orthodox calendars. He gathered their lives and their images and sent the Synod a long list of them. In his diocese the Orthodox began to pray again to Saint Genevieve of Paris, Saint Patrick of Ireland, and others. Some in his own Church held it against him.66

A woman from one of his parishes visited him more than once in his cell at the Cadet Corps. It was a small room on the top floor, with a table, an armchair, a few chairs, and in the corner the icons and a lectern with books. There was no bed, because he did not lie down to sleep. He prayed leaning on a tall staff with a cross-bar at the top, or sometimes on his knees. She thought that when he bowed to the ground he would doze for a little while on the floor. As they talked, he sometimes seemed to nod off. But the moment she stopped, he would say: “Continue, I hear you.”67

Her church had gone a long time without a priest of its own. Once a visiting priest served the evening service of Vespers in forty-five minutes. It usually took two and a half hours. The people were horrified. They went to the archbishop, hoping he would make the priest keep the proper order. He only smiled. “How difficult it is to please you people,” he said. “I celebrate too long, and he too short!”68

A Catholic priest in Paris told his young people that they did not need proof that saints still lived. A saint was walking the streets of Paris that very day. The priest called him Saint Jean Nus Pieds, Saint John the Barefoot.69

His own parents had gone to Venezuela. In 1952 his mother Glafira reposed.70

He did not forget the orphans in San Francisco. On the feast of the Entrance in 1952 he wrote to them that the Church had begun to sing the Christmas hymns. Now they must tune their souls to become cradles for the Infant Christ. The next year he spent a month with them, and afterward he wrote that he was now sure “that you can be very good when you want to be.” He wrote again on 1 February 1954, the anniversary of the day their orphanage was founded. He told them that he felt with all his soul what was happening to them, and that he grieved for them, because great changes lay ahead. Saint Tikhon had kept them through the hard years in Shanghai and had brought them safely across the sea. The orphanage had been founded on the feast of the prince Saint Vsevolod. He gave them the words carved on that prince’s sword: “I will not give away my honor to anyone.”71

In 1954 his father Boris reposed as well. At his naming twenty years before, Saint John had prayed for “those who raised me and taught me by their words and their example.”72

In 1962 the Synod sent him across the ocean one more time. In San Francisco his lifelong friend Archbishop Tikhon was ill. The Russians there had begun a great cathedral, and a bitter quarrel had stopped the work. Thousands of them had known Saint John in Shanghai, and they asked for him. He reached San Francisco on 4 December 1962. It was the feast of the Entrance of the Mother of God into the Temple, the same feast on which he had come to Shanghai twenty-eight years before.73

Saint John’s Opponents Take Him to Court

He made his home in a plain cell in the house of Saint Tikhon’s orphanage, which he had brought out of Shanghai. Money began to flow, and the work on the cathedral started again. Early in 1963 Archbishop Tikhon reposed, and at Tikhon’s own request the Synod made Saint John the ruling bishop of Western America. Through Lent the cathedral was crowded, and the people met Easter with great joy. They had begun to speak of the past year’s troubles as “former.” After Easter he called on them to go on building. The city’s permit to build would run out on 1 June, and it had lasted even that long only because he had asked for more time.74

In the week after Easter the Synod called him to a meeting in New York. On the second day the bishops took up the question of San Francisco, and they asked him to leave the room. He sat waiting in the Synod building for more than four hours. When they called him back in, they were already talking about something else. He asked to hear the decision about San Francisco, and they read it to him. He was recalled from his post. That night people telephoned him from San Francisco. They had heard the news from members of the parish council, and they wanted to know whether it was true.75

The next evening a few hundred people met him at the airport, and many gathered afterward in the house of Saint Tikhon’s. Word spread of a letter that members of the parish council had sent to the bishops. The newspapers printed a petition from about a dozen parishioners. It called the archbishop “generally unstable.” It said that he had not checked the cathedral’s finances, and that in Shanghai he had favored the Soviet church. His supporters called it lies. More than six hundred people came to the planned meeting about the building of the cathedral. They sent a delegation to New York to ask the Synod to change its decision, and the Synod refused. He told his people to obey the Synod and not to act in haste. But anger flared against those who had signed the letter, and fist fights threatened to break out. He described those weeks in his report to the bishops.76

“I restrained people as much as I could … but to my profound sorrow, that which had been done to establish peace in the flock during the course of four months was destroyed in one day, with one stroke.”

— Saint John, report to the Council of Bishops, 196377

Metropolitan Anastasy spoke with him by telephone for more than an hour, and the Synod changed its decision. Saint John could stay in San Francisco for six more months. He called a parish election and a general meeting for 9 June, to set the building going again. The next meeting of the parish council broke down in bitter, abusive speech and wild shouting, and he had to close it.78

On the evening of Thursday 6 June 1963, the service had just ended when an American court officer arrived with one Russian beside him. As soon as Saint John came out of the church, the officer handed him a complaint. Sixteen members of the parish council and their helpers had filed it. It named sixteen members of the former council, the men who had raised money to build the cathedral. It named Archpriest Nicholas Dombrovsky, and it named Saint John himself. The former council was charged with causing losses to the church in running its affairs. Saint John was charged with covering up what that council had done, and with calling an election that would bring its members back. A court order forbade him to call the meeting or to sign building contracts, and he was ordered to appear in court a week later.79

His friend Bishop Savva had come to San Francisco to help him. The next day the two of them went to a short hearing, and the judge lifted the order against the meeting. On Sunday, 9 June, the meeting was held, and the people chose a new church warden and a new council from those who wanted the cathedral built. At the next session of the court, Saint John and Bishops Savva and Nektary were allowed to wait in the court library. The decision was put off. On 9 July the San Francisco Examiner printed a photograph of Saint John in court, with his friends around him.80

In the end the American court ruled that it had no power to judge the affairs of the Church. The court ordered an audit of the accounts, and the audit found no wrongdoing by Saint John. On 13 August 1963 the bishops confirmed him as archbishop of San Francisco. He called on his flock to put an end to their ruinous feud.81

In 1964 his Church glorified Saint John of Kronstadt, proclaiming him a saint. Saint John Maximovitch helped prepare the glorification and wrote a hymn for the new saint. He called Saint John of Kronstadt “the incarnation of mercy,” a man who “took pity on everyone, helped everyone.” That May the aged Metropolitan Anastasy asked to retire. When the bishops voted for his successor, Saint John and another archbishop were only one vote apart. To keep the Church from splitting, a bishop asked them both to withdraw. Saint John put forward Bishop Saint Philaret of Brisbane, and Saint Philaret was elected. In November 1964 the cathedral was finished. It was the largest Russian church in the Americas.82

Yet the charges against him did not stop. They kept coming almost until his death, and he answered them without complaint and without judging anyone.83

Saint John’s Last Visits to His Orphans

The girl who had carried the Christmas star in Shanghai was a grown woman now. Tatiana was married and lived in San Francisco with her three children. In those hard years she often drove the archbishop in her car. After the liturgy he would stay on to teach her eight-year-old son to read from the Book of Hours, the church book of daily services. He talked with her three-year-old daughter, Musenka, and gave her his cross to kiss and blessed bread to eat. Shining all over, he would listen to her baby talk while the church warden, the lawyers, and other public figures waited for him on urgent business.84

He was still strict, and he still played. After the service he joked with the altar boys and rapped the mischievous ones on the head with his staff. Sometimes he would bend down in the middle of a service in the cathedral to play with a small child. When he blessed the people with holy water on feast days, he splashed it right into their faces with a glint in his eye. “He squirts you,” one little girl said.85

Then Musenka had to go into the hospital for an operation on a gland. In the strange place she could not sleep and cried all the time. The nurses were afraid to go into the children’s ward, because she cried even harder when they came. At eleven one night a black-bearded man dressed all in black headed for her ward. The frightened nurses expected her to burst into tears and wake the other children. But when Musenka saw him, she stopped crying. She smiled at him and talked with him, and after he left she fell sound asleep. In the morning a nurse telephoned Tatiana to ask who the man in black had been.86

In 1964 her husband was transferred to Oregon, and the family was worried about being parted from Vladyka. Before they left, he blessed them sadly with two small icons of Saint Nicholas and Saint Seraphim of Sarov. The icons came from the walls of his own cell. Even after they moved to Oregon, he kept in touch. He wrote them letters and sent the children postcards on their name days, and he thanked them for the small parcels they mailed him. He always signed, “Loving you, Archbishop John.”87

Sometimes he stopped to see them on his way from Seattle to San Francisco, and once he brought the wonderworking Kursk Root Icon of the Mother of God. His last visit came late in the evening of 12 November 1965. He was on his way from Seattle with a priest and an altar server, and all three were worn out from the road. Tatiana’s husband tried to persuade him to stay the night. But the archbishop wanted to go on through the night to San Francisco, because he never missed the Divine Liturgy and Holy Communion. At last he gave in, on one condition: he would serve the liturgy in their home in the morning, and the whole family would receive Communion. That evening he taught them how to prepare. He had them read the prayers before Communion and the Akathist to the Queen of Heaven, a long hymn of praise to the Mother of God. He heard the confession of each of them. And he told Tatiana to read the Holy Gospel for ten minutes every day.88

The Repose of Saint John in Seattle

In the spring of 1966 the manager of the orphanage where he lived spoke to him about a diocesan meeting to be held three years later. “I will not be here then,” he said. In May he amazed a woman who had known him for twelve years. “I will die soon, at the end of June,” he told her, “not in San Francisco, but in Seattle.”89

At the end of June he set out for Seattle, accompanying the wonderworking Kursk Root Icon of the Mother of God of the Sign on its travels. He served a prayer service for a man on the evening before he left, four days before the end. Afterward he told the man, “You will not kiss my hand again.”90

In Seattle he stayed at the cathedral of Saint Nicholas, a church built in memory of the new martyrs of Russia. On 2 July 1966 he served the Divine Liturgy there. When it was over, he stayed on alone in the altar for three hours, praying, and came out only a little while before the end. Then he took the icon and went with it to visit some of his spiritual children who lived near the cathedral. Afterward he went back to the room in the parish house where he was staying, and there he prayed.91

Then a crash came from his room. The men who had once served at the altar with him ran in and saw that he was dying. They lifted him into a chair. There, before the wonderworking icon of the Mother of God, he gave his soul to God. He went peacefully, with little sign of pain. He was seventy years old.92

To those who loved him, the news seemed impossible. His flock had always been able to reach him. Without quite knowing why, they had come to feel that this man would simply never cease to be.93

The next day his body was brought home to San Francisco, to the cathedral he had come to finish. The funeral had to wait until the bishops could gather. When Archbishop Averky was a young monk, he had heard the Bitol students speak of their teacher. Now he and Abbot Laurus drove for three days from the monastery at Jordanville, nearly three thousand miles. The body had not been embalmed. For six days it lay in an open coffin in the summer heat, and there was no smell of decay. His hand stayed soft, and he looked like a man asleep. Metropolitan Saint Philaret led the funeral. Two years before, Saint John had put him forward. The city gave special permission to bury him under the cathedral, and a storage room in the basement was made into his tomb. On 7 July they laid him there.94

Saint John’s Tomb Is Opened in 1993

People began to come to his tomb. At first they prayed for him. Then they began to pray to him, and they left him lists of names. Prayers for the dead were sung there almost every day, and those who asked his help read the Psalms over his coffin. In 1987 Father Seraphim Rose and Father Herman Podmoshensky of the Saint Herman Brotherhood printed the memories of those who had known him. Their book, Blessed John the Wonderworker, held about a hundred accounts of miracles. In May 1993 the Council of Bishops of the Church Abroad decided to glorify him as a saint. That autumn the Synod sent a commission to examine his relics.95

On the evening of 11 October the members of the commission went down into the crypt: three bishops, seven priests, three deacons, a reader, and the custodian of the tomb. Archbishop Anthony of San Francisco led them. With him was Archbishop Laurus, who had once come three thousand miles by car to the funeral. All of them had kept a strict fast that day. Some had gone to confession, and others had served the Divine Liturgy and received Communion. Each bishop and priest read the Gospel over the tomb in turn. At nine o’clock they sang the service for the dead. Then Archbishop Anthony called on them all to be at peace, bowed to the ground, and asked everyone’s forgiveness.96

They lifted the heavy lid of the concrete sarcophagus and raised the metal coffin out onto a board. It had rusted through in many places. A monk named Father Peter had kept the key to the coffin for twenty-seven years. But the lock had rusted shut, and he could not open it. So had the screws on the hinges. No one wanted to use force in such a sacred task, so they tried to pry the lid up slowly. Seeing their trouble, Archbishop Anthony began to sing the hymn “We have no other help,” and almost at once the lock opened. Everyone stepped back to make way for him.97

At ten past ten, with trembling and the fear of God, he opened the coffin. Only one of them had ever seen holy relics uncovered before. The awe in the crypt grew. Saint John’s face was covered, and every eye turned first to his white hands. His white vestments had turned green. Archbishop Anthony crossed himself, read the fiftieth Psalm, and lifted the cloth from the face. The skin was white, and the beard was whole. The face was incorrupt: decay had not touched it.98

“After the uncovering of the face, there was an extraordinary silence.”

— Report of the commission, 11 October 199399

There was no hurry and no fuss, and no one wanted to leave. They wished only to stand beside him, to pray, and to touch him. Each of them venerated the holy relics in turn. When they lifted the body into a new wooden coffin, it was light and stiff.100

When they had covered his face with a new cloth, Archbishop Anthony told them about a sick child. The two-year-old son of Father Yaroslav Belikow, a priest of the diocese, was very ill. Father and son were waiting nearby to venerate the relics. They sent for Father Yaroslav. Soon he came down into the crypt, carrying his son Vsevolod, and he touched the child to Saint John’s hands.101

The boy was healed.102

A Note on the Sources

Most of what we know of Saint John comes from people who knew him, and most of it was written down after his repose. His own words come from his address of 1934, which we read in Russian. His letters, his Christmas message, and his report on the lawsuit of 1963 were read in English translations. The meeting where he first called his appointment a mistake is told two ways. One Life puts it on a Belgrade streetcar and adds the answer about Moses. Maria Shatilova put it at Sremski Karlovci, where the Synod met, and she gave the words about the bigger mistake. The parade at Poltava rests on two accounts that disagree about who stepped in: one names Grand Duke Konstantin Konstantinovich, the other a visiting inspector general. Only the second mentions the cap. Many early details rest on one Life printed for the glorification in 1994. One source says he was made a monk on the feast of the Entrance, but most put his ordination as a priest on that feast. The night in the Shanghai slums is known from one retelling. Father Herman Podmoshensky told it from Marina Shakhmatova’s memory, and we read it as a historian quotes it, since the 1987 book that first printed it was not available. The retelling adds, as hearsay, that the bishop came home that night with two babies. One Life counts about 1,500 orphans and the Orthodox Encyclopedia about 3,500. His arrival on Tubabao rests on one memoir quoted in a Russian history, and the Orthodox Encyclopedia says he sailed with the last group of refugees on 4 May 1949. The sources differ on the date of Archbishop Tikhon’s repose and give 13 or 14 August for his confirmation in 1963. No court record of the lawsuit was found. Its result comes from the Orthodox Encyclopedia and from accounts written inside his own Church, and the newspapers we found cover only the recall and the protests. The commission’s report of 1993 notes that the white on his hands was mildew, and that after they were washed, the relics were a dark bronze color. It does not say what became of the boy Vsevolod; Father Peter Perekrestov, who was in the crypt that night, told of the healing in a later interview. He also remembers the lid opening after the archbishop read Psalm 50, and he dates the opening 13 October.

How This Life Was Researched

This life rests on twenty-three sources in English and Russian. Saint John speaks in it through his address of 1934, which we translated from the Russian. He speaks through five letters and a Christmas message, through his words on Saint John of Kronstadt, and through his report on the lawsuit of 1963. The memories of people who knew him are here too. Among them are a fellow student in Belgrade, a student at Bitol, and an orphan girl from Shanghai who grew up to drive him through San Francisco. A refugee on Tubabao and a parishioner near Paris speak as well. The documents include four San Francisco newspaper reports from 1963, President Truman’s statement of 1950, and the signed report of the commission that opened his tomb in 1993. The Orthodox Encyclopedia supplied the dates and the church history. Every quotation from Russian was translated for this life.
