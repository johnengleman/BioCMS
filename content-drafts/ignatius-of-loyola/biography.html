The Bone Below the Knee

In the summer of 1521, in his family's castle of Loyola, a wounded knight looked at his leg and did not like what he saw. A cannon ball had broken it at the siege of Pamplona. The surgeons had set it, then broken it again and reset it, and he had come within a night of dying. Now the flesh had healed at last, but one bone rode over another below the knee and stuck out in a lump, and the leg had come out shorter than the other. He asked the surgeons whether the bone could be cut away. It could, they said, but they did not advise it.

He told them to go ahead. Long afterward he called it "that martyrdom," and he did not pretend it had been for God. Alban Butler, writing his life two centuries later, gave the reason in one plain phrase: it was done so that "his boot and stockings might sit handsomely." His elder brother marveled at what the sick man could bear. When the bone was gone, instruments were strapped to the leg for days on end to stretch it, and still he could not stand on it without pain.

Thirty-two years later, in Rome, the same man gave in at last to the begging of his companions and told the story of his life to Luis Gonçalves da Câmara, who wrote it down. By then he was the general of the Society of Jesus, the order he had founded. He told the story in the third person, calling himself simply "the pilgrim," and he began it without a word of excuse.

"Until his twenty-sixth year he was given up to the vanities of this world, delighting chiefly in feats of arms, and engrossed with an inordinate desire of acquiring renown."

— Ignatius of Loyola, Autobiography, ch. I, dictated to Luis Gonçalves da Câmara (tr. E. M. Rix, 1900)

Renown was what Iñigo de Loyola wanted, and he had the will to get it. A man who would have a bone sawn out of his leg for the sake of a boot could endure almost anything for a goal he had chosen. That will made him the founder of the Jesuits and the author of the Spiritual Exercises, a short book of guided prayer that a pope approved in his lifetime. In 1622 the Church declared him a saint in the same ceremony as Teresa of Avila, Francis Xavier, and Philip Neri.

Yet the question at the heart of his life is what God did with a will like that. When Iñigo turned it on God, it very nearly destroyed him. In a Spanish town called Manresa he set out to match the saints in penance, confessed the same sins over and over, and was tempted to throw himself from the window of his cell. Effort could not save him. What did was something he had first noticed on his sickbed: some thoughts left his soul dry and sad, and others left it glad, and in that difference God was teaching him. He spent the rest of his life learning to hand his will over, and showing others, step by step, how to do the same.

It began in a castle in Guipúzcoa, with the youngest child of a very large family.

A Cannon Ball at Pamplona

The castle stood in the Spanish province of Guipúzcoa, and the child born there in 1491, by the commonly received date, was named Iñigo. His father was Beltrán Yáñez de Oñaz y Loyola, his mother María Sáez de Balda. His first biographer, Pedro de Ribadeneira, counted five daughters and eight sons in the family, and Iñigo was the last of them all. The family sent him young to the court of the Catholic Kings, and Butler says he served there as a page of King Ferdinand under the Duke of Nájera.

The young man who emerges from the sources is easy to picture. He was careful about his hair. He loved arms and the romances of knights. Butler adds that he hated gambling, was good at settling soldiers' quarrels, and wrote verses, among them a poem in praise of St. Peter. Above all he wanted to be talked about. "Feats of arms" and "renown" are his own words for what filled his first twenty-six years.

In 1521 a French army besieged Pamplona, and Iñigo was among the defenders. The nobles inside wanted to surrender on terms. Iñigo would not hear of it. He gave the commander reason after reason why the town could hold out a little longer, and his arguments carried the day against all the other nobles. So the fight went on. Before the assault he made his confession to one of his fellow knights, and the knight confessed to him in turn.

Then a cannon ball struck. It passed between his legs, shattering one and wounding the other. With Iñigo down, the defenders gave up. The French, to their credit, treated their wounded enemy kindly, and after twelve or fifteen days they had him carried home to Loyola on a litter.

He had argued for one more fight because he wanted glory. What he got was a long ride home on a stretcher with a shattered leg.

Two Books in a Sickroom

At Loyola the surgeons decided that the leg had to be broken again and reset. Iñigo let them do it. Of this, and of everything else he endured then and later, he said only that he never uttered a word, "nor was any sign of pain wrung from him, beyond the vehement clenching of his fists."

He grew worse, and fever set in, and the doctors gave him up. On the eve of the feast of Sts. Peter and Paul he received the last sacraments. If there was no change by midnight, the doctors said, he would be a dead man. Iñigo had always had a great devotion to St. Peter. That night, as he told it, "in the middle of the night he began to grow better." Later biographers added a dream in which St. Peter appeared and touched him, and Butler says that Iñigo ever after looked on the recovery as a miracle. In his own account there is only the turn at midnight. Then came the business of the bone, and weeks in bed.

Weeks in bed are long for a soldier. He asked for the books he liked best, the romances of knights and their ladies. There were none in the house. Someone brought him what there was, a Life of Christ and a book of saints' lives called the Flowers of the Saints, both in Spanish. He read them because there was nothing else. In between he lay for two, three, even four hours at a time imagining what he would do for a certain great lady. She was no countess or duchess, he admitted later; her rank was higher than either.

Slowly the other books began to take hold of him. Reading about Christ and the saints, he caught himself thinking in a new way, and years later he told it with complete honesty.

"What if I did this thing which Blessed Francis did? What if I copied Blessed Dominic in this? … St. Dominic did this; therefore I also will do it: this was done by Blessed Francis; then I too will do it."

— Ignatius of Loyola, Autobiography, ch. I (tr. E. M. Rix, 1900)

It was still a knight's ambition, only with new heroes to beat. For a long while the two kinds of daydream simply took turns. Then he noticed something. When he dreamed of the lady and the world, the thoughts gave him great delight while they lasted, but when he put them aside he "found himself sad and dry." When he imagined going to Jerusalem and doing the hard things the saints had done, the pleasure did not stop when the daydream did: "joy remained with him." He wondered at the difference until the meaning came clear. One kind of thought leaves sadness behind it, and another gladness. "This was the first exercise of reason which he made on the things of God," the account says. Everything he later taught about telling the movements of the soul apart, the good spirit from the bad, grew from that discovery on a sickbed.

One night he saw Our Lady with the Child Jesus. The vision left him with such a loathing for his past life, and especially for the sins of the flesh, that he said no such desire ever won his consent again, from that night until August 1555, when the words were written down. He would not claim for certain that the vision came from God. He only said what it had done. The household saw the change in him plainly. Butler, following a later life, adds that one night as he dedicated himself to Our Lady the house shook, a window broke, and a crack opened in the wall. Iñigo himself never told that story.

As he recovered he began to copy out the best passages of his two books into a volume of about three hundred quarto leaves. "Therein he wrote the words of Christ in red, and the Blessed Virgin's in blue," the account says. Between writing and praying he found his greatest comfort in looking up at the sky and the stars. He made up his mind to go to Jerusalem as a pilgrim. He thought about entering the Carthusian monastery at Seville afterward, as a lay brother whom nobody knew, and even sent a servant who was going to Burgos to ask about their Rule.

His elder brother suspected what was coming. When Iñigo said he must go to see the Duke of Nájera, his brother took him from room to room of the castle, begging him not to ruin himself. Iñigo answered him carefully, without telling a single lie, and slipped free. Then he climbed onto a mule and rode away from Loyola.

The Mule at the Fork

Another of his brothers rode with him as far as Oñate. On the way they kept a night of prayer at the shrine of Our Lady of Aránzazu, and every night of the journey Iñigo scourged himself. At Nájera he collected money that the duke's treasurer owed him and used it to pay off his creditors and to have an image of Our Lady restored. He sent away the two servants who had come with him. From then on he traveled alone.

On the road toward Montserrat a Moor on a mule fell in with him, and they talked as they rode. The conversation turned to the Virgin Mary, and the two of them argued about her virginity. Then the Moor rode on ahead and out of sight. Iñigo was left burning. The old knight in him began to think that he ought to go after the man and stab him with his dagger.

He could not decide. When he came to a fork in the road, he left the choice to the mule. If the animal took the Moor's way, he would find the man and stab him. If it kept to the highway, he would let him go. The mule kept to the highway. It is hard to read the story as anything but a mercy. He had set out to serve Christ, and a mule had stopped him from committing murder in the name of Christ's mother.

Before he reached Montserrat he bought what a pilgrim to Jerusalem should wear: a long tunic of rough sackcloth, a pair of hempen shoes, of which he kept only one, because his bad leg still swelled every night, a pilgrim's staff, and a gourd for water. His head was still full of Amadis of Gaul and the other romances. The knights in those books kept a night-long vigil before they were dubbed, and he resolved to keep one of his own, before Our Lady.

At the Benedictine abbey of Montserrat he wrote out a confession of his whole life and made it over three days. It was the first time he had ever opened his desires to anyone. He gave his mule to his confessor. His sword and dagger were placed on the altar of Our Lady. On the night before the feast of the Annunciation, in March 1522, he went out secretly to a beggar, gave him his own clothes, and put on the sackcloth. Then he went back into the church and kept his vigil before the altar of Our Lady, kneeling and standing by turns all night, staff in hand. At daybreak he received Communion and left.

The road to Barcelona was full of people who would know him and do him honor, so he turned aside toward a town called Manresa. He had not gone far when a man came running after him. Was it true, the man wanted to know, that he had given his clothes to a poor man? The beggar was in trouble, suspected of having stolen them. Iñigo said yes, he had given them, and at the thought of what his gift had cost the poor man, his eyes filled with tears.

He would stay at Manresa about a year, and the year very nearly broke him.

One Hour of Life at Manresa

At Manresa Iñigo begged his bread. He ate no meat and drank no wine. He had once been particular about his hair, so now he let it grow uncombed, and his nails with it, for the same reason. Butler, writing from the later lives, adds an iron girdle, three scourgings a day, and children throwing stones at the strange penitent, and a later tradition places him for a time in a dark cave outside the town, where he was found half dead. Iñigo's own account says nothing of a cave. It speaks instead of what happened inside him.

He began to see something in the air near him, a shining thing shaped somewhat like a serpent, covered with what looked like eyes. He did not yet know what it was. About that time a hard thought came at him, mocking the life he had chosen, and the old fighter in him answered back.

"How can you possibly endure such a life as this through the seventy years yet before you?" … "Canst thou, O wretched creature, promise me even one hour of life?"

— Ignatius of Loyola, Autobiography, ch. II (tr. E. M. Rix, 1900)

The temptation left him, but from then on his soul would not stay still. Some days prayer had no taste at all for him. Then, suddenly, the heaviness would be lifted, "like as when one snatches a cloak from off a man's shoulders." An old woman of Manresa once said to him, "O that Jesus Christ my Lord would one day show Himself to thee!"

Before that came, there was a long darkness. Iñigo began to be tormented by scruples. He had confessed his whole life at Montserrat, yet the old sins kept rising in his mind, and he confessed them again, and then again, and no confessor could give him rest. He prayed seven hours a day. None of it helped. One day he cried out to God.

"Make haste to help me, Lord, for there is no help in man, neither in any creature do I find relief! Ah, if I knew where I might find it, no labour would seem great nor hard, Lord, show me where it is hid. As for me, had I to go after a dog's whelp and take my cure from him, I should do it."

— Ignatius of Loyola, Autobiography, ch. III (tr. E. M. Rix, 1900)

His cell had a great window, close to the place where he prayed, and he was often tempted, violently, to throw himself down from it. He knew it would be a sin, and he kept crying out, "Lord, I will not do what offendeth Thee." He tried fasting. For a whole week he ate nothing at all, until his confessor ordered him to eat, and he obeyed. Still the old sins kept coming back to him one after another, until he grew weary of the life he was leading, and there came "a sudden impulse to put an end to it."

Instead he resolved never again to confess the sins of his past life, and from that day the scruples were gone. He was free. The man who had tried to win heaven by force of will had found that he could not even win his own peace that way. What he found instead was a teacher.

A Master and a School-Boy

Now his nights were so full of consolation that he lost the sleep he needed, until he began to doubt where it came from and decided to sleep. Once, after he had given up meat, he saw meat set before him in a vision, ready to eat. He came to believe that God was dealing with him "as a master with a school-boy whom he teaches." The man who had tried to storm heaven was being sent back to school.

The lessons came one after another. One day, on the steps of the Dominican monastery, he saw the Holy Trinity in the likeness of three keys of a spinet, and he wept all that day. Another time he understood how God had created the world, seeing something white with rays coming from it. At Mass, when the Host was raised, he saw white rays coming down from above and understood how Christ is present in the Sacrament. In prayer he often saw the humanity of Christ as a white body. If he said he had seen it twenty or forty times at Manresa, he told Gonçalves, he would not be sure he was wrong. Even if Scripture had never taught these mysteries of faith, he said, he would have been ready to die for them after what he had seen.

The greatest lesson came beside a river. He was walking along the bank of the Cardoner to a church about a mile from Manresa, which he thought was dedicated to St. Paul. He sat down for a while facing the stream, which ran deep there.

"While he was sitting there the eyes of his mind were opened, not so as to see any kind of vision, but so as to understand and comprehend spiritual things."

— Ignatius of Loyola, Autobiography, ch. III (tr. E. M. Rix, 1900)

Everything seemed new to him. He could not describe what he understood. He could only measure it. If all the help he had received from God in his whole life, down to his sixty-second year, were gathered into one heap, he said, "these all would appear less than he had been given at this one time." He came away "as much enlightened as if he had been made another man and another mind had been given to him."

He knelt before a cross that stood nearby to give thanks. There the shining thing with the many eyes appeared again, and there, before the cross, he knew it at last for a demon. He drove it off with the stick he carried.

The rest of that year softened him. A fever brought him near death, and afterward he begged the noble ladies of Manresa who cared for him to call out to him, "O sinner," if they ever saw him dying again. One winter he fell ill once more, and the ladies nursed him and made him wear shoes, a cap, and two coarse gray garments. He gave up the extreme penances of the first months and began again to cut his hair and his nails. Butler says he spent ten months there, and Iñigo himself said about a year. In that time he had learned, on his own soul, what he would later set down in the Spiritual Exercises. When Pope Pius XI wrote of the book four centuries later, he spoke of it as having been given to Iñigo in that retreat at Manresa by the Mother of God herself, adding, with care, that this is what is reported.

Iñigo's own thoughts were on Jerusalem. Early in 1523 he went down to Barcelona to find a ship.

No Companion but God

At Barcelona there was talk of finding him a companion for the journey, and Iñigo would have none. No companion at all, he said, "not even if he were the son or the brother of the Duke of Cardona, for that he wished to have the three virtues of faith, charity, and hope." He wanted to lean on God alone.

He left even the question of food for the voyage to his confessor, who told him to take ship's biscuit, and he obeyed. When he found he had a few small coins left over, he left them on a bench by the landing. He had another reason for traveling quietly. He told Gonçalves, thirty years later, that he had fought a hard battle with vanity for two years, and that when he sailed for Jerusalem he did not dare tell anyone where he was going.

A storm drove the ship to Gaeta in five days. Plague was in the country, and among the pilgrims on the road with him were a mother and her daughter. At a village where they stopped for the night, men of the place tried to assault the two women. Iñigo flew into such a rage, shouting at the villagers, that no one dared lay a hand on him.

He reached Rome on Palm Sunday and left after Easter with the blessing of Pope Adrian VI. He had accepted six or seven gold coins for the voyage and then regretted it, so he gave them away to beggars on the road to Venice. One morning a man who met him at dawn ran away in terror at his "extremely pale and bloodless aspect."

In Venice he slept in the Piazza San Marco until a rich Spaniard took him home and introduced him to the doge, who gave him passage on the governor's ship to Cyprus. On the day it sailed he was in bed with fever. Asked whether he could go aboard, the doctor said "he could certainly go if they wanted to bury him in the ship." He went. At sea he rebuked the crew for the shameful things he saw done on board, until his fellow Spaniards warned him that he might be put off on an island.

The pilgrims rode the last stretch to Jerusalem on asses and made the final approach in silence. When the city came into view, Iñigo was filled with consolation, and it was said that "a certain more than mortal joy was shared by every one." He wanted to stay there for good.

The Franciscans who kept the shrines said no. The Guardian, and then the Provincial, told him plainly that many who stayed had been killed or captured, and that they had authority from the pope to excommunicate anyone who refused to leave. Iñigo said he would obey. But first he slipped away alone and climbed the Mount of Olives to see the footprints of Christ shown there. He gave the keepers a penknife, and later his scissors, to let him in.

On the way down a servant of the friars came at him in a rage, seized him by the arm, and dragged him back toward the monastery. All the way back, he said, he saw Christ walking above him.

This Fellow Is Mad

The road home was harder than the road out. At Cyprus a ship's captain refused to carry him. "Let him get across as Blessed James did," the captain said, if the pilgrim was such a saint. Iñigo found passage on another vessel, and the captain's own ship was lost at sea.

He was back in Venice in January 1524. At Ferrara he gave every coin he had to the beggars who asked him, until he had nothing left. Italy was at war, and people warned him not to take the road through the armies' camps. He went straight on. Near Genoa soldiers seized him as a spy, stripped him, and brought him before the governor.

He had a habit, in those years, of calling everyone simply "You," with no titles. Before the governor he kept to it, "showing no deference," and told him almost nothing. "This fellow is mad," said the governor, and had him sent away.

Back in Barcelona in 1524, the man who had once argued a commander out of surrender sat down to study Latin grammar under a master named Ardèvol, while a devout woman of the city, Isabel Roser, helped to support him. At once a new trial came. Whenever he tried to learn his lessons by heart, his mind filled with new spiritual lights and delights, and he could memorize nothing. He went to his master and promised to come to every lesson, so long as he could find bread and water to live on, and the distraction stopped. Butler, following the later biographers, tells that when Iñigo conjugated amo, "I love," he could only think, "I love God; I am loved by God."

In 1526 he moved on to the university town of Alcalá, and the Inquisition soon took an interest in him.

So Would You, Were You Convicted

At Alcalá Iñigo lodged at a hospital and begged for his living, and he tried to study logic, physics, and theology all at once. He also began to do what he had come for. He gave the Spiritual Exercises to people who asked and taught Christian doctrine. A few young men joined him, Calisto, Cáceres, and Arteaga among them, and they all dressed alike. One gentleman, Diego de Eguía, handed him bed-hangings and candlesticks to give away to the poor.

The inquisitors of Toledo came to look into the little group, and Iñigo's host warned him that people were calling the little band names, Illuminati among them. The vicar of Alcalá, Figueroa, examined everything and found no fault. He only ordered that they stop dressing alike: Iñigo and Arteaga were to wear black, Calisto and Cáceres brown. Iñigo said they would obey. Then he asked what good all these inquiries had done. Had they found any heresy in them?

"No," said Figueroa, "if we had detected any you would have been burnt."

"So would you," said the pilgrim, "were you convicted of heresy."

They dyed their clothes as ordered. The suspicion did not go away: one priest gave Iñigo Communion only unwillingly. Another inquiry followed, about a married woman who came to see him veiled, and again nothing was found. Then, on 21 April 1527, he was arrested and put in prison, and for seventeen days nobody told him why. His trust was all in one direction: "I look to Him to set me free, if it please Him, for Whose sake I came hither." The cause turned out to be two noble widows, a mother and daughter, who had set off on foot to see the relic of the Veronica at Jaén. Figueroa also asked whether he kept the Sabbath. Calisto had himself locked in the prison to be with his friend, until Iñigo got him out.

After forty-two days the sentence came. The pilgrims were to dress as ordinary students, and they were not to teach the faith for four years, until they had studied more. The archbishop of Toledo, Alonso de Fonseca, gave Iñigo four gold crowns. Iñigo moved on to Salamanca, and there the Dominicans of San Esteban questioned him. How did a man with so little schooling come to preach?

"We do not preach," said the pilgrim, "but we talk familiarly to people about divine things, just as we talk after dinner with those who have invited us."

— Ignatius of Loyola, Autobiography, ch. VI (tr. E. M. Rix, 1900)

The friars had a trap ready. If he had not learned these things by study, they reasoned, then he must be claiming to speak by the Holy Spirit. Iñigo saw it and would say no more except before his superiors. He and Calisto were put in prison and chained together by the feet, on a chain ten or twelve handbreadths long. The bachelor Frías examined the Exercises, and Iñigo, untaught as he was, explained how he distinguished mortal from venial sin. Francisco de Mendoza, who would one day be cardinal of Burgos, came to see him in prison and asked how he bore it. Iñigo answered that for all the "fetters, handcuffs, and chains in Salamanca," he would wish to wear "still more for the love of God."

One night all the other prisoners broke out and escaped. In the morning the doors stood open, and the two pilgrims were still inside. After twenty-two days the judges found nothing to condemn, but they forbade him to define mortal and venial sin for four years. Iñigo decided to go to Paris.

A Beggar at the University of Paris

He reached Paris about February 1528, as he reckoned it, and started again at the bottom. At the Collège de Montaigu he sat with boys over the humanities. He had brought some money with him, and a fellow lodger borrowed it and spent it all, so Iñigo had to beg and to sleep at the hostel of St. James. To pay for his studies he went begging every year to Flanders, and once to England.

He could not keep still about God for long. He gave the Exercises to three young Spaniards, Peralta, Castro, and Amador, and all three gave away everything they owned. Their countrymen came to the hostel armed and dragged them out. Master de Govea of the Collège de Sainte-Barbe threatened Iñigo with "the Hall," a public flogging before the college. Butler, following the later biographers, says Iñigo went to Govea and explained himself, and that Govea knelt before him in front of the assembled college and asked his pardon. The story does not appear in Iñigo's own account. Butler also tells of him standing up to his neck in a frozen pond to turn a young man from sin, a tale of the same late kind.

His own account has a better story about the borrowed money. The lodger who had spent it all fell sick at Rouen. Iñigo decided to go to him, barefoot, eating and drinking nothing, the whole way from Paris. He was afraid, and the fear went with him as far as Argenteuil, until, on the higher ground beyond, it fell away and great joy took its place. Fourteen leagues went by that first day. On the third day he reached Rouen, still fasting, and looked after the man who had spent his money.

When he began his course in arts, he laid aside his talks about God, so that he could study. To a doctor of the university he explained the change. "It is because I do not speak to anybody on religious subjects," Iñigo told him. "But we shall resume our former relations as soon as I have finished my course." About that time he went into a house struck by plague and touched a sick man's sore with his hand. Afterward his hand began to ache, and he was seized with horror that he had caught the plague. He mastered it the way he had mastered everything. He forced his fingers into his mouth, saying, "If you have plague in your hand, then get it in your mouth also."

In those years he won two men who would become saints, Peter Faber and Francis Xavier, and he won them, the account says, "by means of the Spiritual Exercises." The book that had grown out of Manresa opens with what Iñigo called the Principle and Foundation.

"Man is created to praise, reverence, and serve God our Lord, and by this means to save his soul."

— Ignatius of Loyola, Spiritual Exercises, Principle and Foundation (tr. Elder Mullan, 1914)

Everything else, the Foundation goes on, is to be used as far as it helps toward that end and set aside as far as it hinders. So a person should want "not health rather than sickness, riches rather than poverty, honor rather than dishonor, long rather than short life," but only what leads to the end for which he was made. The man who gave the Exercises was not to push. He should stand "in the centre like a balance," the book says, and "leave the Creator to act immediately with the creature." Iñigo had learned that on his sickbed and by the river. Now he was teaching it to others, and they were changing their lives.

On the feast of the Assumption, 15 August 1534, Iñigo, Faber, Xavier, and four others, among them Diego Laínez and Simão Rodrigues, went down into the crypt at Montmartre, received Communion from Faber, and made vows together. They would go to Venice and then to Jerusalem. If they could not sail within a year, they would go to the pope instead. Then Iñigo's health failed. Attacks of stomach pain that could last sixteen or seventeen hours had worn him down, and the doctors sent him back to Spain. In 1535 he went home.

One Is Enough for Me

At Azpeitia, the town of his childhood, he would not stay in his brother's house. He went to the public hospital instead. He resolved to teach the children Christian doctrine every day, and his brother told him no one would come. "One is enough for me," he said, and he taught them every day.

The vain young knight had come home as a beggar, and his own town listened to him. At his urging the magistrate forbade gambling. He had a law made against the women who lived with priests as if they were their wives, and he arranged for the bell to be rung three times a day for the Angelus. Butler adds that he confessed in public to a theft from an orchard in his boyhood, for which another man had paid the damages, and made it good.

Then he set out for Italy to meet his companions. On a cliff path above a river he lost his way and had to crawl on his hands and knees. It was, he told Gonçalves, the worst bodily suffering of his life. He got through it, and at Bologna he fell off a footbridge, and then went begging from door to door through the whole city without getting a coin.

In Venice, through 1536, he gave the Exercises to several men, among them a man named Hozes. He faced another inquiry there, and was cleared. In January 1537 the companions came from Paris, nine of them now, and they spent the waiting months nursing the sick in the hospitals of Venice. On 24 June 1537 Iñigo was ordained a priest.

Then Iñigo went with some of the companions to Vicenza. There they found a ruined house with neither door nor window, slept on straw, and gave forty days to prayer. Then they went out into the squares of the town, waving their caps to gather a crowd, and preached. At this time word came that one of the companions lay dying at Bassano. Iñigo, feverish himself, set out at once and walked so fast that Faber could not keep up. On the road, he said, God made it certain to him that the sick man would not die, and he told Faber so. The man lived.

The year of waiting passed without a passage to Jerusalem. As they had vowed, they turned toward Rome.

Placed With the Son

On the road, with Faber and Laínez, Iñigo stopped to pray in a little church a few miles outside Rome, at a place called La Storta. Something happened there that he described to Gonçalves in very few words. He saw so clearly "God the Father placing him with Christ His Son" that he could not dare to doubt it. When Gonçalves told him that Laínez had recorded more details, he answered simply: "Whatever Lainez may have said about it is undoubtedly true."

Laínez's fuller account comes down through Ribadeneira, who was present years later when Laínez, then general of the Society, told it to the community in Rome. In that version Christ appeared carrying his cross, and the Father commended Iñigo to him, and Christ said, "Ego vobis Romae propitius ero": I will be favorable to you at Rome. Iñigo, coming out of the chapel, read it soberly. He did not know, he told his companions, whether they would be crucified in Rome, but Jesus would be favorable to them.

He expected trouble. When they reached the city he said he saw its windows shut against them. He was right. In 1538 a man called Michael began to spread charges against Iñigo, and others joined in. Iñigo would not let the matter quietly fade. He wanted a sentence, so that everything would be clear. When the pope, Paul III, came back to the city, Iñigo went to see him at Tusculum and made his case, and the pope ordered the sentence to be given. It was given in favor of Iñigo and his companions. He had been examined at Alcalá, at Salamanca, at Paris, and at Venice, and never once condemned for heresy.

Not long before, giving the Exercises at Monte Cassino, he had seen in prayer the soul of Hozes, the man he had guided in Venice, entering heaven. Iñigo had been ordained for a year and a half before he said his first Mass, at Christmas 1538, in the church of Santa Maria Maggiore. Within two years the little band of friends would be a religious order, and he would be its head.

The General in the Kitchen

On 27 September 1540 Pope Paul III approved the Society of Jesus by the bull Regimini militantis Ecclesiae. The companions elected Ignatius, as he now called himself in Rome, as their first general. He refused. He accepted only when his confessor ordered him to, and he took up the office at Easter 1541. On 22 April the first fathers made their solemn profession at St. Paul's Outside the Walls.

Butler says that his first act as general was to go down to the kitchen and work there as a scullion under the cook, and that for forty-six days afterward he taught catechism to poor children in the Society's church. In the years that followed he set up a house in Rome for Jews who wanted to become Christians while they were being instructed, a house for women who wanted to leave a life of prostitution, and homes for orphans. He founded the Roman College. He took up the mission to Ethiopia with such energy that he sent Gonçalves to the Portuguese embassy every third day for three months.

Above all he wrote. In 1548 Paul III approved the Spiritual Exercises by a brief, Pastoralis Officii, and the little book from Manresa went out with the Church's blessing. His other great work was a book of laws for the Society. He drew up the Constitutions of the Society in Spanish, and his secretary, Juan de Polanco, put them into Latin. Ignatius told Gonçalves how he did it. He said Mass every day, offering each point to God and praying over it, always with tears. On one question, whether the Society's churches could have fixed incomes, he spent forty days. He kept a bundle of papers recording the visions that came to him while he worked. When Gonçalves asked to borrow the bundle, he said no.

The Constitutions are a book of rules, but they begin by saying that rules are not the main thing. What holds the Society together, Ignatius wrote, is something no rulebook can supply.

"… the interior law of charity and love, which the Holy Spirit is wont to write and imprint upon hearts …"

— Ignatius of Loyola, Constitutions of the Society of Jesus, Preamble (our English, from the Latin of 1558)

Still, the old soldier knew that love needs a form, and the form he chose was obedience. The Constitutions ask Jesuits to obey "as if they were a corpse," or to be "like an old man's staff." In 1553, when the Jesuits of Portugal clung to their former superior, Simão Rodrigues, and would not accept anyone else, Ignatius wrote them his most famous letter, telling them that of all the virtues it was in obedience that he most wished to see them excel. Ribadeneira tells of a Dominican, Fray Reginaldo, who once asked Ignatius what he thought of a nun in Bologna said to have ecstasies, to feel no fire, and to bear marks like the stigmata. Of all that was said of her, Ignatius answered, nothing was less open to suspicion than her prompt obedience. Obedience was his test for any such gift.

The man who had once argued a commander out of surrender now asked his sons to give up their own will. What was happening in his own soul while he asked it, he kept almost entirely hidden.
