Saint Clare of Assisi

In the morning, people in Assisi stopped to stare at a door. It belonged to the house of a knight, and it had been barred so that no one could open it. Heavy logs and a stone column stood against it, and many men together could hardly have moved them. Now the logs were pushed aside and the door stood open. The daughter of the house was gone, and people marvelled that a young girl could have done it.1

The girl was Saint Clare of Assisi, and she was about eighteen. Her father and mother had planned to marry her splendidly, as their rank allowed, to great and powerful men. Seven knights belonged to her family’s house. In the night she had dragged the logs aside by herself, because she wanted the one thing all that wealth could never give her. She wanted to be poor.2

She would hold on to that choice for the rest of her life. Her family fought it. In time, so did people who loved her and wanted her safe. At the very end she would have to race time itself. What did being poor mean to her, that she would trade a whole world for it? And what would it cost her to keep it?3

A Knight’s Daughter Meets Saint Francis

Saint Clare’s story begins with a prayer. Her mother, Blessed Hortulana, had gone on pilgrimage across the sea, and also to the shrine of Saint Michael at Monte Gargano and to Rome. When her time to give birth was near, she prayed hard before the cross in a church. She asked the Crucified to bring her safely through the danger of childbirth, and she heard a voice: “Do not be afraid, woman. You will give birth safely to a light that will shine very brightly on the world.” The baby girl was born in Assisi in about 1193, and her mother had her baptized Clara, which means “bright.”4

Her father, the knight Favarone, kept one of the grandest and costliest households in the city. His daughter did not care for its riches. She would hide the good food she was served and send it to the poor. Under her fine clothes she wore a rough white cloth. When she sat with the people of the house, she always wanted to talk about God.5

She grew up beautiful, and her family began to talk of a husband for her. Many relatives begged her to agree. A man who had married into the family came to the house often, and he begged her himself, many times. She would not even listen to him. Instead she preached to him about how little the world was worth.6

In those years Saint Francis of Assisi had chosen the way of poverty. When Saint Clare heard of it, she “decided in her heart” to do the same. She wanted to stay a virgin and to live in poverty, and no one could talk her out of it.7

Saint Francis had heard of her too, and he wanted to speak with her. Their meetings had to be hidden from her family. With only one trusted friend beside her, she slipped out of her father’s house again and again to find him. “His words seemed to her like fire, and his deeds seemed more than human.”8

He and a friar named Brother Philip urged her to give herself to Jesus Christ, and she listened gladly. Saint Francis spoke to her of Christ, who became man out of love, and he urged her to keep herself for Christ alone. She did not wait long to say yes. At once she caught a glimpse of the joys of heaven, and beside them the whole world seemed cheap. From then on she put herself in Saint Francis’s hands, as her guide after God.9

Saint Clare Leaves Home at Night

For Saint Clare, giving herself to Christ meant being poor, because Christ himself had been poor from the first night of his life. She would later urge her sisters to become like “Christ the Poor One,” whom his poor little mother laid as a baby in a narrow manger. Her family could see only loss in such a choice. Years afterward she gave it another name: “a great and praiseworthy exchange.”10

At the very start, she sold her whole inheritance and gave the money to the poor. She even sold part of the share that belonged to one of her sisters. Her relatives offered more for it than anyone else, but she would not sell to them. She sold it to others instead, “so that the poor would not be cheated.” She kept nothing of the price for herself.11

As Holy Week drew near, she went to Saint Francis to ask what she should do. He told her to go to the blessing of the palms on Palm Sunday with everyone else, dressed in her finest clothes. On the night after, she was to leave. So on Palm Sunday she went to the cathedral among the crowd of women, dressed for the feast. When the others pressed forward to receive their palm branches, she was too shy to move, and she stayed where she was. Then the bishop came down the steps, went to her, and put a palm into her hands.12

That night she set out on her “longed-for flight.” She did not dare use the usual door, for fear that someone would stop her. Instead she went to the other door, the barred one, and set her own hands to the logs and the stone. She moved them by herself, “with the help of Jesus Christ,” and her strength surprised her. Long afterward she wrote that the Father in heaven had chosen “to light up my heart by his grace.” That night she followed the light. She opened the door and left behind her home, her city and her family.13

With a few trusted companions she hurried through the night to Saint Mary of the Porziuncola, the little church where Saint Francis and his brothers had begun. The friars were keeping watch at the little altar with lighted torches, and they welcomed her in. Saint Francis cut off her hair there, before the altar of the Virgin Mary. She laid aside her ornaments and received the robe of penance. It was the night after Palm Sunday, about the year 1211.14

She had given up everything, and she was glad. Looking back, she wrote that she and her first sisters “feared no poverty, work, sorrow, shame or contempt from the world.” “We held them as great delights,” she said. Saint Francis led her straight from the Porziuncola to the Benedictine nuns of San Paolo, near Bastia, to stay there until God provided another place.15

Her relatives soon learned where she had gone.16

Her Family Comes to Take Her Back

The news broke her family’s hearts, and it made them angry. They banded together and ran to San Paolo. Force, poisonous advice, sweet promises: they tried them all. They told her that this wretched life was unworthy of her birth. No one in those parts had ever heard of such a thing.17

Saint Clare took hold of the cloths on the altar. Then she bared her shorn head before them. She told them that nothing would ever again pull her away from the service of Christ. Her courage grew as her family’s war against her grew fiercer. Their harshness wounded her love, and the wound only gave her strength.18

Many years later she put into words what she had learned at that altar. “One who is clothed cannot wrestle with one who is naked,” she wrote, “because the one who has something to be grabbed by is thrown to the ground sooner.” Her family had nothing left to take hold of.19

For many days they kept at her, with harsh words and harsh deeds, and through it all she held on to her hope. At last her relatives gave up, fell quiet, and let her stay.20

She had left someone behind in her father’s house. Her younger sister, Saint Agnes, was still at home. The two girls had different natures. Still, they loved each other deeply, and the parting hurt them both. Saint Clare prayed hard that the world would lose its taste for her sister, and that she would come to Christ too. Sixteen days after Saint Clare left home, Saint Agnes came to her and said she wanted to give her whole life to God. Saint Clare took her in her arms with joy. “I thank God, sweetest sister,” she said, “that he has heard my prayer for you.”21

By then Saint Clare had moved to another small church, Sant’Angelo di Panzo. The next day twelve men of the family came there in a fury. They had given up on Saint Clare, so they turned on her sister and ordered her home. When the girl refused, one knight struck and kicked her and tried to drag her off by the hair. They hauled her down the mountainside and tore her clothes. She cried out, “Help me, dearest sister! Do not let them take me from Christ the Lord!” Saint Clare threw herself down in prayer, in tears. Suddenly the men could not lift the girl from the ground, not even with help from the fields and vineyards. “She must have been eating lead all night,” they mocked. When an uncle raised his arm to strike her, a sharp pain seized it. At last Saint Clare came and asked them to leave her sister in her care, and they went away bitter. Saint Francis cut Saint Agnes’s hair with his own hands, and the two sisters began their new life together.22

Saint Clare’s heart was not fully at rest at Panzo. On Saint Francis’s advice she moved to San Damiano, a small church outside Assisi. Saint Francis knew the place well. Years before, a voice had spoken to him from the wooden cross while he prayed there: “Francis, go and repair my house. As you see, it is completely destroyed.” He had worked hard to rebuild it.23

Saint Clare remembered one more thing about that church. Saint Francis had climbed up on its wall one day, while he was still rebuilding it and had no brothers yet. Full of joy, he called out in French to some poor people living nearby: “Come and help me build the monastery of San Damiano. Ladies will live here one day, and through their holy way of life our Father in heaven will be glorified in his whole Church.” Now the ladies had come. Saint Clare did not shrink from the narrowness of the place or from its loneliness.24

She would live inside those walls for the rest of her life, forty-two years.25

Abbess of San Damiano Against Her Will

Other women came to join the sisters at San Damiano. In time her mother came too. By then Blessed Hortulana was a widow. She followed her daughter into the Order and lived there humbly among the young women until she died.26

For three years Saint Clare refused to be called abbess. She wanted to be under the others, and to serve them. Saint Francis pressed her until he “almost forced her,” and she gave in. The office filled her with fear, not pride. She gave orders “with much fear and humility,” and most of the time she did the task herself. She poured water over her sisters’ hands, served them at table, and washed the sick.27

One Thursday in Lent she was washing the feet of a serving sister who had come in from outside, and she bent to kiss them. The sister could not bear to be served like that. She pulled her foot back, and it struck Saint Clare in the mouth. Saint Clare rejoiced at it. She gently took the foot again and kissed its sole.28

At night she prayed long after the others had gone to bed, lying face down on the ground and weeping. When she came back from prayer, she spoke words of God that set her sisters’ hearts on fire. They were glad to see her, as if she had come from heaven.29

Sometimes she wore a shirt of knotted horsehair, tied on with rough cords. One of the sisters asked to borrow it, and Saint Clare lent it to her. The sister wore it for three days and then gave it back, much more readily than she had asked for it. Saint Clare’s fasts were so hard that her sisters wept over them. At last Saint Francis and the bishop of Assisi ordered her to eat at least an ounce and a half of bread a day, and she obeyed. Her face stayed glad through all of it, as if she did not feel her hardships at all, or else laughed at them.30

Saint Francis watched over the little community with a father’s care. When he saw how gladly the sisters took on hardship, he wrote them a short way of life. In it he gave them a promise: “I will and I promise, for myself and for my friars, always to take special care of you, as I do of them.” Saint Clare never forgot it. “He kept this promise faithfully as long as he lived,” she wrote.31

About 1219 Saint Francis sent Saint Agnes away to Monticello, near Florence, to lead the sisters there as their abbess. The two sisters who had begun their new life together at Panzo would now live far apart, and the parting was hard for them both. From her new home Saint Agnes wrote to Saint Clare:32

Dearest mother, what endless sorrow and sadness fill my body and soul! They weigh on me and torment me in more ways than I can say, and for one reason only: I am separated from you and from my holy sisters, when it seemed to me that we would live happily together until death. This grief does not leave me for a moment. It keeps on growing.

Saint Agnes, letter to Saint Clare33

She asked her sister to pray for her. She said she did not know whether she would ever be allowed to come back.34

Saint Francis Dies

Near the end of his life Saint Francis was very ill, and the sisters at San Damiano were greatly troubled by it. He could not visit them himself. So he wrote some words of comfort for them, set them to music, and sent them by his companions. In them he begged the sisters to be one in love, in poverty and in obedience, and to live and die that way. He asked the healthy to work for the sick, and the sick to bear their pain with patience.35

Shortly before his death he wrote to them once more. He wanted to make sure they would never fall away from the poverty they had started with. Saint Clare later wrote his words into her own Rule:36

I, little Brother Francis, want to follow the life and poverty of Jesus Christ our Most High Lord and of his most holy Mother, and to persevere in it until the end. And I beg you all, my ladies, and I advise you, to live always in this highest life and poverty. Watch yourselves well, so that you never leave it in any way because of anyone’s teaching or advice.

Saint Francis, in the Rule of Saint Clare, ch. VI37

In the week that he died, Saint Clare lay seriously ill as well. Her own illness had already begun. It would stay with her for the rest of her life, nearly thirty years in all. Now she was afraid she would die before him. She grieved bitterly and could not be comforted, because she feared she would never again see “her only father after God,” her comforter and her teacher. She sent a friar to tell him. Saint Francis loved her with a father’s love, more than anyone, and he was filled with pity for her. He knew he could not go to her. So he sent his blessing in a letter and gave the friar a message for her.38

Go and tell Sister Clare not to grieve or be sad because she cannot see me now. Let her know that she will surely see me before her death, and that she and her sisters will be greatly comforted when they do.

Saint Francis, in the Mirror of Perfection39

Saint Francis died during the night at the Porziuncola, in early October 1226. At dawn the people and clergy of Assisi came for his body. They carried him toward the city with hymns and trumpets and many lights, and everyone held a branch of olive or some other tree. On the way the procession stopped at San Damiano, and they set him down in the church.40

In the sisters’ wall there was a small window, the one through which they received Communion. Now it was opened. Saint Clare came to it with her daughters, “to see the father who would not speak to them again, nor come back to them.” The friars lifted his body from the bier and held it up in their arms before the window for a long time. The sisters wept and cried out to him. Caught between sorrow and joy, they kissed his hands. Then they carried him away, and the opening was closed again. His promise had come true. She had seen him once more.41

They laid him in the church of San Giorgio, inside the town. For Saint Clare he had been “our pillar, our only comfort after God, and our support.” She wrote that while he lived he had always tended the sisters in word and deed, as a gardener tends “his little plant.” Now the sisters were afraid of their own weakness. So they bound themselves to poverty again and again, so that no sister, then or later, would ever turn away from it.42

Soon a Pope would ask her to let that poverty go.

She Refuses the Pope’s Offer

When her community was still young, Saint Clare had asked Pope Innocent III for a strange favor. She called it the privilege of poverty: the right to own nothing at all. The Pope was delighted. He said that no one had ever asked the Holy See for such a thing. To answer an unusual request with an unusual favor, he wrote the first lines of the grant with his own hand.43

In March 1227 Cardinal Hugolino became Pope Gregory IX. He loved Saint Clare with a father’s love, and he came to San Damiano in person. The times were dangerous. Because of those dangers he urged her to accept some property, which he offered freely himself. He wanted to give her many things and to buy land for the monastery.44

She would not accept it. Saint Francis had warned her never to leave poverty “because of anyone’s teaching or advice.” Now the advice came from a Pope who loved her, and she still said no. Then the Pope tried one more thing. “If you fear your vow,” he said, “we release you from it.”45

“Holy Father,” she answered, “in no way do I ever want to be released from following Christ.”46

The Pope saw a promise that might weigh on her. She saw only Christ walking ahead, and herself following him. Gregory gave way. On 17 September 1228 he sent her a letter from Perugia that granted exactly what she wanted. “As you have asked,” he wrote, “we confirm your resolve of the highest poverty, and we grant you that no one can force you to receive possessions.” And he added words of trust: “He who feeds the birds of the sky and clothes the lilies of the field will not fail you in food or clothing.”47

She kept that letter carefully, “fearing to lose it,” and she lived it every day. Sometimes the friars who begged for the sisters came home with whole loaves. Then she would pretend to scold them: “Who gave you these whole loaves?” Broken pieces made her happier.48

She Sends the Friars Away

Saint Francis had promised that his friars would always take special care of the sisters. They preached to the sisters and begged bread for them. In 1230 Pope Gregory forbade any friar to visit the women’s monasteries without his permission. Saint Clare grieved, because her daughters would now hear the word of God far less often. She sighed and said, “Let him take all the friars from us now, since he has taken away the ones who gave us the food of the soul.”49

Then she sent them all back to their superior at once, even the friars who begged bread for the house. If her sisters could not have bread for the soul, she would not keep the men who brought bread for the body. She would rather they went short of food than lost the word of God. When Pope Gregory heard about it, he eased the ban at once and left the matter in the hands of the friars’ Minister General.50

Soldiers Inside the Convent

In those years the Church suffered under Emperor Frederick II, and the valley of Spoleto suffered with it again and again. The emperor sent swarms of soldiers and Saracen archers to ruin castles and besiege towns. Once the emperor’s captain Vitalis of Aversa brought his army against Assisi. He swore that he would not leave until he had taken it. When Saint Clare heard this, she called her sisters together. “We have received many good things from this city,” she said, “and so we must pray to God to protect it.” She sent for ashes, uncovered her shaved head, and poured ashes over it. Then she put ashes on the head of every sister and sent them all to pray. The next morning the army broke up and went away.51

The sisters lived in fear of such armies. Saint Clare kept telling them not to be afraid, because God was with them. “If they come,” she said, “put me in front of them.”52

Then they came. The emperor’s men were attacking Assisi, and Saracen soldiers climbed the wall of San Damiano and dropped down into the cloister. The sisters’ hearts sank, and their voices shook. Weeping, they ran to their mother. Saint Clare was ill. Even so, she had herself led to the door of the dining hall to face the enemy. She had the Sacrament carried before her in a small silver box set in ivory. It held the Body of the Lord. Two sisters held her up. She lay face down on the ground and prayed in tears:53

Does it please you, my Lord, to hand over to the pagans your defenseless servants, whom I have fed with your love? Lord, I beg you, protect these servants of yours. I cannot protect them now.

Saint Clare54

Then she heard a voice like a little child’s, wonderfully gentle: “I will always protect you.” She prayed for the city too: “My Lord, if it pleases you, protect this city as well, for it supports us out of love for you.” The voice answered, “It will suffer troubles, but my protection will defend it.” Saint Clare lifted her wet face and turned to her weeping daughters. “Do not be afraid,” she told them. “I promise you that you will suffer no harm, now or in the future, as long as you obey God’s commandments.” The soldiers climbed back down the walls they had scaled and left without hurting anyone.55

That evening Saint Clare called the two sisters who had held her and heard the voice. She told them to tell no one as long as she lived.56

Letters to a Princess in Prague

Far away, a king’s daughter had chosen the same poverty. Saint Agnes of Prague was the daughter of the king of Bohemia, and she could have married the emperor himself. Instead she chose, in Saint Clare’s words, “most holy poverty and bodily want,” and in 1234 she entered a monastery in Prague. The news spread to nearly every part of the world, and in San Damiano Saint Clare heard it with joy. That year she wrote to the young princess. “O blessed poverty,” she exclaimed, “who gives eternal riches to those who love and embrace her!” Then she named the choice they had both made:57

Truly, it is a great and praiseworthy exchange: to leave the things of time for the things of eternity, to win heaven instead of earth, to receive a hundred for one, and to have a blessed life forever.

Saint Clare, First Letter to Saint Agnes of Prague58

Soon the princess faced the same test. In 1235 Pope Gregory gave her monastery the revenues of a rich hospital, so that it would have a sure living. She wrote to Saint Clare for help. Saint Clare wrote back as someone who had stood in that very place. “What you hold, may you always hold,” she told her. “What you do, may you go on doing and never stop.” Even if someone she must respect advised her against her calling, she should not follow that advice. “Poor virgin, embrace the poor Christ.”59

In 1238 the Pope gave way to Saint Agnes of Prague too, and he granted her the privilege of poverty. Saint Clare’s third letter almost sings. “I can truly rejoice,” she wrote, “and no one could ever take such joy from me, for I already hold what I longed for under heaven.” “Place your mind in the mirror of eternity,” she told her friend. Then she added a mother’s warning, one she had learned the hard way:60

But our flesh is not bronze, and our strength is not the strength of stone. No, we are frail and open to every bodily weakness. So I ask you, dearest, and I beg you in the Lord, to draw back wisely and with discretion from the indiscreet and impossible strictness in fasting that I know you have taken up.

Saint Clare, Third Letter to Saint Agnes of Prague61

By then she had been ill herself for more than twelve years.62

A Rule Written, Not Yet Sealed

The illness went on, year after year, and she did not grumble or complain. What came from her lips was talk of God and thanks. When she could not get up, she had herself propped up in bed with cushions, and she spun. Her thread was made into fine cloth, and the sisters sewed the cloth into altar linens called corporals. Fifty pairs of them went out to churches on the plain and in the hills around Assisi.63

Her sense of fun stayed with her in bed. One day she wanted a large cloth that lay out of reach, and no one was there to bring it. The monastery’s little cat began to drag it across the floor toward her. “Rascal, you don’t know how to carry it,” Saint Clare told the cat. “Why are you dragging it on the ground?” As if it understood, the cat began to roll the cloth up so that it would not touch the floor.64

While the Pope was still far away in Lyons, she grew worse than usual, and her daughters were heartbroken. A nun had a vision at San Paolo, the convent where Saint Clare had once held on to the altar. She saw herself and her sisters at Saint Clare’s sickbed, weeping and waiting for her to die. A beautiful woman stood at the head of the little bed and spoke to them: “Do not weep, daughters, for one who will win. She cannot die until the Lord comes with his disciples.”65

After a short time the Pope’s court came to Perugia. Cardinal Rainaldo heard that Saint Clare was worse, and he hurried from Perugia to San Damiano. He was bishop of Ostia and the Order’s protector, a father to her by his office and a devoted friend by his own affection. He gave her Communion and spoke words of comfort to her sisters. With tears she begged him never to forget her soul and the souls of her sisters. Above all, she begged him to ask the Pope and the cardinals to confirm her privilege of poverty. He promised, and he kept his word.66

That visit was on 8 September 1252. On 16 September Cardinal Rainaldo drew up a Rule for the Poor Ladies, a written way of life for them and for all who would come after. In places it spoke in Saint Clare’s own voice, and at its heart stood the poverty she had chosen at eighteen:67

The sisters shall not take anything as their own, neither a house nor a place nor anything. … This is that summit of the highest poverty which has made you, my dearest sisters, heirs and queens of the kingdom of heaven.

Rule of Saint Clare, ch. VIII68

But a Rule needed the Pope’s own approval, under his seal. That had not come.69

On Christmas night 1252 the sisters went to the chapel to sing the night prayer, and they left her alone in her sickbed. Saint Clare began to think about the little Jesus, and it grieved her that she could not be there for his praises. She sighed: “Lord God, here I am, left alone with you in this place.” Suddenly she heard the music being sung at the church of San Francesco, about two kilometers away. She heard the friars chanting the psalms. She heard the singers’ harmony and even the organ. And she saw the manger of the Lord.70

When her daughters came back in the morning, she told them: “Blessed be the Lord Jesus Christ, who did not leave me when you left me. By the grace of Christ I really did hear all the services that were sung last night in the church of San Francesco.”71

The Pope Comes to Her Bed

Early in 1253 Saint Clare called her sister home. Saint Agnes had gone away to Monticello about 1219, not knowing whether she would ever be allowed to come back. After more than thirty years apart, she came home to San Damiano and stayed at her sister’s side.72

Nearly fifteen years had passed since Saint Clare’s last letter to the princess in Prague. Now she wrote to her from her bed one last time, to “the other half of her soul.” She was sorry she had written so seldom, she said. There were few messengers, and the roads were dangerous. But her love had not cooled. She asked her friend to look into Christ every day as into a mirror, and she began where her own story had begun:73

Look closely, I say, at the beginning of this mirror: the poverty of him who was laid in a manger and wrapped in swaddling clothes. O wonderful humility! O astonishing poverty! The King of angels, the Lord of heaven and earth, is laid in a manger!

Saint Clare, Fourth Letter to Saint Agnes of Prague74

“Remember your poor little mother,” she wrote near the end. Her sisters sent their greetings, she added, and above all “the most prudent virgin, Agnes, our sister.” She closed with a farewell that looked past her own death: “until we meet at the throne of glory of the great God.”75

In late April 1253 Pope Innocent IV came to Assisi with his cardinals to consecrate the great church of San Francesco. He visited Saint Clare a few days after he arrived, and he came again in August, a few days before her death. He came to her bed and held out his hand for her to kiss. She kissed it gratefully. Then she asked with great reverence to kiss his foot. The Pope stepped up onto a wooden footstool and held out his foot, and she kissed it above and below. Then she asked him to forgive all her sins. “I wish to God I had no more need of forgiveness,” he said. He gave her absolution, the Church’s full pardon, and a generous blessing.76

That same day she received Communion. When everyone else had gone, she lifted her eyes to heaven, joined her hands, and spoke to her sisters with tears: “Praise the Lord, my little daughters. Today Christ has given me a gift so great that heaven and earth could not repay it. Today I have received the Most High himself, and I have seen his Vicar.”77

The nun’s vision had come true. The Lord’s Vicar had come to her, and his cardinals had stood around him like disciples. Now she longed with all her heart to be set free and to see Christ reigning, the Christ whose poverty she had followed for so long.78

Only one wish on earth was left. She longed to see her Rule sealed by the Pope, so that she could kiss the seal one day and die the next.79

The Seal Arrives, and Saint Clare Dies

Her dying went on for many days. For seventeen days she could eat nothing at all. Day by day the people’s devotion grew, and cardinals and bishops came often to see her, honoring her already as a saint. Yet she was the one who gave strength to everyone who came. When a friar urged her to be patient in her long suffering, she answered him:80

Dearest brother, ever since I came to know the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ through his servant Francis, no suffering has troubled me. No penance has been hard, and no illness has been difficult.

Saint Clare81

Saint Francis had been dead for almost twenty-seven years, and he was still the one through whom she had found Christ. She asked the priests and friars to read her the Passion of the Lord. When a friar known for his fiery words about God came in, she was filled with new joy and asked him whether he had any news about the Lord. Two of Saint Francis’s companions stood by her bed. One of them wept, and yet he comforted the others. The other kissed her bed.82

Her sister could not stop weeping, and she begged Saint Clare not to go and leave her behind. Saint Clare answered, “It pleases God that I go, dearest sister. But stop crying. You will come to the Lord soon after me. Before I leave you, the Lord will give you a great comfort.” Then she turned to her daughters and urged them to keep the Lord’s poverty. She blessed everyone who had been kind to her, men and women, and all the Poor Ladies of her houses, those living and those still to come.83

On the Friday before she died, Saint Clare was alone with her niece, who was one of the sisters. More than once Saint Clare asked her, “Do you see the King of glory, whom I am seeing?”84

That evening several sisters sat by her bed, weeping. With no one speaking to her, Saint Clare began to speak softly to her own soul:85

Go out without fear. You have a good guide for your journey. Go out, for he who made you has made you holy. He has always protected you, and he loves you as tenderly as a mother loves her son. Blessed are you, Lord, for you have made me.

Saint Clare86

A sister asked whom she was talking to. “I am speaking to my blessed soul,” she said. Then she spoke of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit in words so deep that the sisters could not follow her. One sister told another to keep every word in her memory. Saint Clare heard her. “You will remember what I am saying now,” she told them, “only as far as the One who makes me say it allows you.”87

One of the sisters by the bed was thinking about how good Saint Clare was. In her thoughts it seemed that all the court of heaven was getting ready to honor her, and that the Virgin Mary was preparing clothes for the new saint. She was wide awake when a great crowd of virgins dressed in white came in at the door, with crowns on their heads. One among them was taller and lovelier than all the rest. Her crown was larger, and on top of it was a golden ball like a censer, which gave off so much light that it seemed to fill the house. The virgins came to the bed. The tallest one covered Saint Clare with a cloth so fine that she could still be seen through it, and bent down over her. And they were all gone.88

Near the end, a friar came to San Damiano with a letter from the Pope. It bore his seal and approved her Rule, with all its poverty.89

Saint Clare was very close to death. She took the letter reverently. With her own hands she lifted the seal to her lips and kissed it.90

It had happened just as she wished. She sang old Simeon’s song from the Gospel, the prayer of a servant whom the Lord lets go in peace.91

All through her last night she spoke to her sisters and encouraged them. At the end she made her confession, because she was afraid she might have failed in some small way the faith promised at her baptism. One of the sisters there had never heard a confession so beautiful and good. Saint Clare asked another sister to pray the prayer of the five wounds of the Lord. Her voice was very low now, but the Passion and the name of Jesus were always on her lips. Almost the last words she said to that sister came from a psalm: “Precious in the eyes of the Lord is the death of his saints.”92

Saint Clare died at San Damiano on Monday 11 August 1253, the day after the feast of Saint Lawrence. The girl whose mother had been promised a light went, “truly Clara,” without stain, “to the clarity of the eternal light.”93

Saint Clare Is Declared a Saint

As the news spread, the people of Assisi streamed to San Damiano, so many that the city seemed empty. All of them called her a saint, and many wept. The mayor sent knights and armed men to keep watch all night over her body. The next day the Pope and his cardinals came. When the friars began the prayers for the dead, the Pope stopped them. He said they should sing the prayers for virgin saints instead, as if he wished to declare her a saint before she was even buried. Cardinal Rainaldo answered that they should do what was usual, and the Mass for the dead was said.94

They carried her body with hymns and great rejoicing to the church of San Giorgio in the town, the same place where Saint Francis had first been laid to rest. The man who had prepared her way of life while he lived now, in death, prepared a place for her. Saint Agnes followed her sister to God ninety-seven days later, on 16 November. Before she died, she received the comfort Saint Clare had promised her.95

That autumn Pope Innocent IV ordered the bishop of Spoleto to find out the truth about her life and miracles. In late November 1253 the bishop questioned twenty witnesses under oath. Most of them were her own sisters. The last was a man who had once served as a man-at-arms in her father’s house and had known her as a girl.96

Pope Alexander IV declared her a saint at Anagni in 1255. He was the same Rainaldo who had heard her plea at her sickbed, written her Rule and preached at her funeral. His decree played on her name: “Clare, bright with bright merits, shines brightly: in heaven with the brightness of great glory, and on earth with the splendor of lofty miracles.” Her feast was first set for 12 August, because the day before already belonged to Saint Rufino, the patron of Assisi. Now the Church keeps it on 11 August, the day she died.97

On 3 October 1260 her body was carried to the new church of Santa Chiara and buried deep under the high altar. A new monastery was built beside it for the sisters of San Damiano, so that they would not be parted from the body of such a mother.98

For centuries the Pope’s letter with her Rule was thought to be lost. In the spring of 1893 the abbess of Santa Chiara found it. It was wrapped inside an old habit of Saint Clare’s. The parchment was faded and crumpled. In the top margin the Pope’s own hurried words could still be made out. “For reasons known to me and to the protector of the monastery,” he had written, “let it be done.” On the back, someone from her own time had written: “Blessed Clare touched this and kissed it out of devotion many, many times.”99

Her life is not hard to understand. She shows a young reader that a choice made at eighteen can be kept gladly for a whole life. She gives anyone lying ill and alone her Christmas night, when the Lord stayed with her after everyone else had gone. And she shows anyone pressed to trade what they love for safety the other trade, the one she made, and a joy that no one could take from her.100

A Note on the Sources

The Legend of Saint Clare was written between 1255 and 1261 and is ascribed to Thomas of Celano. Its author says he asked the companions of Saint Francis and the sisters, and he built on the sworn testimony of 1253. So the Legend and the Process are not independent witnesses. We read the Process in a modern Spanish translation, and its quotations are our English from the Spanish. Gilliat-Smith dates the flight to the night after Palm Sunday 1211. Robinson places it in 1212, and one sister at the Process said Saint Clare entered on Holy Monday. Her birth year is uncertain; Gilliat-Smith thinks 1193 most likely. Robinson doubts the printed text of a privilege from Innocent III. He notes that Saint Clare’s Testament speaks only of asking that Pope to confirm her poverty. The story of the Pope writing in his own hand comes from the Legend. The Testament is not found in the early manuscripts, and its authenticity has been questioned; we quote it as hers with that caution. Saint Agnes’s letter from Monticello bears no date. Wadding placed it in 1221; we read it in de Chérancé’s English, from the Chronicle of the Twenty-Four Generals. The song and the message Saint Francis sent the sisters before his death come from the Mirror of Perfection, a later collection of his companions’ stories. Gilliat-Smith dates his death to the evening of 4 October 1226, and de Chérancé to 3 October. The years of Gregory IX’s visit, the Saracen attack, and the siege by Vitalis are not given in the early texts. Sister Francisca thought the Saracens came on a Friday in September, about the middle of the morning. The bull of 9 August 1253 reached Saint Clare near the end. Sister Felipa said she died the next day, while Gilliat-Smith says two days later. The reading of the Pope’s note in the margin is the Abbot Cozza-Luzzi’s, and the song of Simeon comes from Robinson’s account of the bull, not from the witnesses. Gilliat-Smith says only that Saint Clare was believed to have worn the habit in which the bull was found. The friar who urged her to be patient was Brother Rainaldo; the friar of fiery words was Brother Juniper; Saint Francis’s two companions were Brothers Angelo and Leo. The Process gives the question about the King of glory to her niece Amada. It gives the vision of the virgins to Sister Benvenuta di Diambra, who said the tall virgin bent her face to Saint Clare’s face, or perhaps to her breast. The Legend calls Saint Clare’s sister Agnes; the sister Beatrice who testified in 1253 is a different woman. Gilliat-Smith thought the scene at San Paolo was calmer than the Legend tells it.

How This Life Was Researched

This life draws on ten main sources in three languages: Latin, Spanish and English. The sworn testimony that twenty witnesses gave in November 1253 was read in full in a Spanish translation. The Latin Legend and the Testament were read in the Acta Sanctorum, and the Legend, the Rule and Robinson’s notes in his 1910 English translation. Saint Clare’s four letters to Saint Agnes of Prague were translated from the Latin. For the death of Saint Francis we read Celano’s first Life of him in Latin and the Mirror of Perfection. Gilliat-Smith’s study of 1914 and de Chérancé’s life of 1910 filled in dates, the Testament, and Saint Agnes’s letter from Monticello.
