Saint Clare of Assisi

Pope Gregory IX came in person to San Damiano, the small convent at Assisi where Saint Clare lived with her sisters. He loved her as a father loves a daughter. The times were hard and dangerous, he told her, and her house ought to have some property to live on. He would buy it for the sisters himself. Saint Clare refused. She had made a vow of poverty, a solemn promise to God to own nothing, but the Pope had an answer for that as well. “If you fear your vow,” he said, “we release you from it.”1

“Holy Father,” Saint Clare answered, “in no way do I ever want to be released from following Christ.”2

She had already turned down a great deal more than a Pope’s gift. She was born into one of the noblest families of Assisi, a house with seven knights in it. Her parents had hoped to marry her in splendid style to “great and powerful men.” She would not even listen. At about eighteen she left her father’s house one night and went to Saint Francis, who had chosen to be poor.3

For forty-two years she lived in that one small convent. Women came from every side to share her life, and houses that followed her way rose in “cities without number.” She asked one Pope for a favor no one had ever asked before: the right to own nothing at all. Later she defended it against the Pope who loved her. In her letters to a king’s daughter in Prague she praised it, and as she lay dying she begged for it again. A sealed letter from the Pope reached her bed just in time, and she kissed it. Two years after her death, the Church declared her a saint.4

Her story begins before she was born, with a promise her mother heard in a church.

Saint Clare’s Mother Hears a Promise

Saint Clare was born in Assisi about 1193. Her father Favarone was a knight. Both sides of the family were of knightly blood, and they were rich in land and goods. Her mother was Blessed Hortulana, a woman who gave as much of her time to God as a wife with a household could. She had crossed the sea with other pilgrims to pray in the places where Christ had walked, and she had come home full of joy.5

When her child was nearly due, Blessed Hortulana went into a church to pray. She knelt before the cross and asked the crucified Lord to bring her safely through the dangers of childbirth. Then she heard a voice speak to her:6

“Do not be afraid, woman. You will give birth safely to a light that will shine on the world more clearly.”7

— The voice heard by Blessed Hortulana, in the Life of Saint Clare

Because of those words, Blessed Hortulana had her daughter named Clara at baptism. The name means “bright” or “clear.” She hoped the promised light would somehow shine out in her child, as God willed. Many years later Saint Clare told the story to the sisters of her convent. One of them had also heard it from Blessed Hortulana herself.8

The little girl learned the first truths of the faith from her mother. Her father’s house was one of the grandest in the city, and food was plentiful. Saint Clare sent the poor the good things she was supposed to be eating herself. Her companion Bona carried them out for her many times, in secret. She had no beads to count her prayers, so she counted them on a little heap of pebbles.9

Under her soft and costly dresses she wore a hair shirt, a rough garment worn next to the skin as a penance. A man-at-arms who lived in her father’s house saw the rough white cloth under her clothes. He saw her fast and pray, too. Without seeking it, she began to win the praise of her neighbors.10

Saint Clare Refuses to Marry

Saint Clare was beautiful, so her family began to look for a husband. Her father was noble and powerful in Assisi, and so were the other men of his house. Her parents wanted a match that suited their rank. She was about seventeen. Many of her relatives begged her to agree, and she never did.11

One man came and went freely in the house, because his wife was her relative. He pleaded with her many times to take a husband. She would not even hear him out. Instead she preached to him “contempt of the world,” the old Christian teaching that the world and all it offers count for nothing next to God. More than forty years later he told the story under oath.12

She handled her family with care. She let them think she only wanted to wait a while before marrying, and meanwhile she promised her virginity to God. A neighbor knew why no one could move her. She wanted to stay a virgin and to live in poverty.13

By then all Assisi knew the name of Saint Francis. When Saint Clare heard that he had chosen the way of poverty, she decided in her heart to do the same. She longed to see him and hear him speak. Saint Francis had heard of her as well, and he wanted to win her for God.14

They met many times, and they took care that no one found out. Bona often went with her. The two walked out in secret so that her relatives would not see them. Saint Francis always urged her to give herself to Jesus Christ, and Saint Clare listened gladly.15 The early Life of Saint Clare was written soon after she was declared a saint. It describes those visits:

“With only one trusted companion, the girl left her father’s house in secret. She often visited the man of God. His words seemed to her like fire, and his deeds seemed more than human.”16

— The Life of Saint Clare

Saint Francis showed her how empty the world’s hopes are and how its beauty deceives. He spoke to her of Christ as the Bridegroom who became man out of love. He urged her to keep herself for him alone. She did not hesitate. She gave her consent, and from then on she put herself under the guidance of Saint Francis. After God, he was the one who directed her steps. He did not want her to stay long in the world.17

Saint Clare Leaves Home at Night

Palm Sunday was coming. On that Sunday before Easter, the Church blesses palm branches and gives them to the people. Saint Clare went to Saint Francis and asked him what she should do, and how. He told her to go to the blessing of the palms with everyone else, dressed in her finest clothes. On the night that followed, she was to leave the world.18

On Sunday she went into the cathedral with the crowd of women, bright in her festive clothes. When the others pressed forward to take their branches, Saint Clare was too modest to push ahead, and she stayed where she was. The bishop came down the steps, went over to her, and put a palm into her hands.19

That night she set out with a few companions she could trust. She did not dare use the usual door, because she was afraid someone would stop her. So she went to another door of the house. It was barred with heavy logs and a stone column, and many men together could hardly have moved them. A woman who lived in the house at the time said that Saint Clare moved them alone, with the help of Jesus Christ, and opened the door. Her own strength amazed her. It was about the year 1211, and she was about eighteen.20

Leaving her home, her city, and her family behind, she hurried to Saint Mary of the Porziuncola. This was the little church where Saint Francis and his first brothers had begun their life of poverty. The brothers were keeping watch in prayer at the little altar, and they welcomed her with lighted torches.21

There she “gave a bill of divorce to the world,” in the words of the early Life. In the Bible, a bill of divorce was the paper that ended a marriage. Her sister Beatrice later said that Saint Francis cut off her hair before the altar. Saint Clare put on the plain robe of penance. Then Saint Francis took her straight on to the church of Saint Paul, a house of Benedictine nuns, where she was to stay until God showed her another place.22

In the morning many people saw the barred door standing open at her father’s house. They marveled that a young girl had been able to open it. Before long the news reached her family.23

Saint Clare’s Family Comes to Take Her Back

Her relatives were heartbroken. They condemned what she had done, and they ran together to the church of Saint Paul. They tried force, bad advice, and sweet promises. Such a life was beneath her birth, they told her, and no one in those parts had ever heard of such a thing. Saint Clare gave them her answer at the altar:24

“But Clare took hold of the altar cloths and bared her shorn head. She declared that nothing would ever again separate her from the service of Christ. Her courage grew as her family’s war against her grew stronger.”25

— The Life of Saint Clare

Years later her sister Beatrice told the same story under oath. Saint Clare gripped the altar cloths and uncovered her head to show them it was shaved. She would not agree to leave. She would not be taken away, and she would not go home with them. The struggle went on for many days. At last her relatives gave up and went quiet.26

After a few days Saint Francis and two of his brothers took her to another church, Sant’Angelo di Panzo. She had never stopped praying for her younger sister, Saint Agnes, who was still at home. The two were different in temperament, but they loved each other dearly, so the parting had hurt them both. Sixteen days after Saint Clare left home, Saint Agnes came to her and said she wanted to give her whole life to God. Saint Clare embraced her. “I thank God, sweetest sister,” she said, “that he has listened to my concern for you.”27

The next day twelve men of the family arrived at Panzo, pretending to come in peace. They had already given up on Saint Clare, so they turned to Saint Agnes and ordered her home. When she refused to leave her sister, one of the knights fell on her with blows and kicks. The men dragged her down the hillside, tearing her clothes and leaving torn hair along the path. “Help me, dearest sister!” Saint Agnes cried. “Don’t let them take me away from Christ the Lord!”28

Saint Clare threw herself down in prayer, in tears. Suddenly Saint Agnes’s body seemed fixed to the ground. Several men pulled with all their strength and could not carry her across a little stream. Workers ran in from the fields and vineyards to help, and they could not lift her either. The men turned it into a joke: “She’s been eating lead all night, so no wonder she’s heavy.” At last Saint Clare came and asked them to stop and to leave her sister in her care. They went away bitter. Afterward Saint Francis cut Saint Agnes’s hair with his own hands.29

Saint Clare still did not feel at rest in Panzo. On the advice of Saint Francis she moved one last time, to the little church of San Damiano. Saint Francis had worked hard to repair it years before. While he was praying there, a voice had spoken to him from the wooden cross: “Francis, go and repair my house. As you see, it is completely destroyed.” In that small place Saint Clare shut herself away for the love of Christ. She would live there for forty-two years.30

Saint Clare Becomes Abbess Against Her Will

Women soon came to join her. Pacifica had lived across the square from her, and she entered religious life together with her and served her almost day and night. When Blessed Hortulana was widowed, she came too, and she lived among the sisters until she died. Word of Saint Clare reached even queens, and new houses following her way rose in many towns. Saint Francis sent Saint Agnes to Florence about 1219, to lead one of them.31

For three years Saint Clare refused the title and work of abbess, the mother who governs a convent. She wanted to serve the others rather than rule them. In the end Saint Francis pressed her so hard that Pacifica said he “almost forced her.” Pacifica was there when it happened. The new office filled Saint Clare with fear, not pride.32

She led by serving. She poured water over the sisters’ hands and waited on them at table, and she nursed the sick and cleaned up after them with her own hands. When she gave an order, it was with fear and humility, and most of the time she did the job herself. On cold nights she went around covering the sleeping sisters.33

She also washed the feet of the serving sisters who worked outside the convent, and then she kissed their feet. One Thursday in Lent she was washing a serving sister’s feet and bent down to kiss them. The sister could not bear such humility. She pulled her foot back fast, and it struck Saint Clare in the mouth. Saint Clare gently took the foot again and kissed its sole. A sister who had come to San Damiano as a small girl was watching. She said Saint Clare rejoiced at it.34

She was hardest of all on herself. At times she wore a shirt of knotted horsehair, tied on with rough cords. The same young sister once asked to borrow it. She gave it back after three days, because she found it so rough that she could not bear it at all. Saint Clare slept on the bare ground or on cuttings of vines, with a block of wood or a stone for a pillow.35

In the two Lents of the year, the long seasons of fasting, she lived on bread and water. On Mondays, Wednesdays, and Fridays she ate nothing at all. Her sisters grieved over it, and Pacifica admitted that she had sometimes cried. At last Saint Francis and the bishop of Assisi ordered Saint Clare to eat at least an ounce and a half of bread every day, and she obeyed. Through it all her face stayed cheerful. She seemed either not to feel the hardship or to laugh at it.36

Saint Francis gave the sisters a short written way of life. In it he promised that he and his brothers would always take special care of them, as they did of one another. Shortly before his death, he wrote to them one last time:37

“I, little Brother Francis, want to follow the life and poverty of Jesus Christ our Most High Lord and of his most holy Mother, and to persevere in it until the end. And I beg you all, my ladies, and I advise you, to live always in this highest life and poverty. Watch yourselves well, so that you never leave it in any way because of anyone’s teaching or advice.”38

— Saint Francis to Saint Clare and her sisters, kept in her Rule, chapter VI

Saint Clare held on to those words. Soon a Pope would ask her to let them go.39

Saint Clare Refuses the Pope’s Offer

Poverty had been her choice from the very beginning. When she sold her inheritance, her own relatives offered to pay more than anyone else. She would not sell it to them, because she did not want the poor to be cheated. Everything she received went to the poor. Later she asked Pope Innocent III for a privilege of poverty, a promise that no one could force her community to own property. The Pope was delighted. He said that no one had ever asked the Holy See for such a thing. To answer an unusual request with an unusual favor, he wrote the first lines of it in his own hand.40

Pope Gregory IX had known her for years. Before he became Pope he was Cardinal Hugolino, bishop of Ostia. Even then he wrote to Saint Clare whenever a new trouble arose, to ask for her prayers. He believed they had helped him. His visit to San Damiano probably came in 1228, when he was in Assisi to begin the work of declaring Saint Francis a saint. A sister who had entered in Saint Clare’s first year stood by and heard him urge her to accept property. Pacifica saw that he wanted to give her many things and to buy land for the house.41

Saint Clare would not take any of it, and she would not be released from her vow. In September 1228 the Pope gave way. He granted her community a privilege in writing. It said that no one could ever force them to accept property. Saint Clare guarded that privilege with great care for the rest of her life, afraid that she might lose it.42

Poverty at San Damiano was a matter of bread. Brothers went out begging for the sisters and brought back what people gave. Saint Clare was glad of broken pieces and scraps. When the brothers came back with whole loaves, she scolded them. “Who gave you these whole loaves?” she asked. She preferred crusts.43

In 1230 Pope Gregory issued an order that no friar could visit the convents of women without his permission. Saint Clare grieved that her sisters would now hear far less preaching. She sighed: “Let him take all the friars from us, … now that he has taken away the ones who gave us the food of the soul.” Then she sent away every friar who served the convent, even the brothers who begged bread for them. If she could not keep the men who fed the soul, she would not keep the men who fed the body. When the Pope heard, he relaxed his order at once.44

Pope Gregory had warned her about the dangers of the age. Those dangers soon came to her own door.45

Soldiers Break Into San Damiano

In those years the Emperor Frederick II was at war with the Church, and the valley around Assisi suffered again and again. His army included bands of Saracen archers, men whose ancestors had once ruled Sicily. The soldiers were sent to lay waste to castles and to besiege cities. One day the army came against Assisi itself, and some of the Saracens climbed the wall of San Damiano. They dropped down into the cloister, the enclosed yard at the heart of the convent.46

The sisters were terrified, and they ran in tears to their mother. Saint Clare had once told them, “If they come, put me in front of them.” Now she was ill in bed. Even so, she had herself led to the door of the dining hall. She sent for the little silver and ivory box that held the Body of Christ, the bread consecrated at Mass. Two sisters stayed at her side to support her. She lay face down on the ground before the Lord and prayed with tears:47

“Does it please you, my Lord, to hand over to the pagans your defenseless servants, whom I have fed with your love? Lord, I beg you, protect these servants of yours. I cannot protect them now.”48

— Saint Clare’s prayer, in the Life of Saint Clare

Then a voice like a little child’s sounded in her ears: “I will always protect you.” Sister Francisca was one of the two sisters holding her, and she heard it too. Saint Clare prayed for the city as well, because it supported the sisters for the love of God. “It will suffer troubles,” the voice answered, “but my protection will defend it.” Saint Clare raised her face, wet with tears, and turned to her sisters. “Do not be afraid,” she said. “I promise you that you will suffer no harm, now or in the future, as long as you obey God’s commandments.”49

The Saracens climbed back down the walls they had scaled. They left without touching anyone in the house. That evening Saint Clare called the two sisters who had heard the voice, and she ordered them to tell no one as long as she lived. Francisca told it under oath after Saint Clare’s death. She thought it had been a Friday in September, at about the middle of the morning.50

Another time the danger fell on the whole city. Vitalis of Aversa, a captain of the Emperor, cut down the trees and ruined the countryside. He swore he would not leave until he had taken Assisi. When Saint Clare heard, she called her sisters together. “We have received many good things from this city,” she said, “and so we must pray to God to protect it.” She told them to come to her very early in the morning.51

When they came, she sent for ashes. She uncovered her shaved head and heaped ashes on it. Then she put ashes on the head of every sister and sent them all to the chapel to pray. Some of the sisters fasted on bread and water that day, and some ate nothing. The next day Vitalis left with his whole army, and he never troubled that land again.52

Saint Clare never left San Damiano, but her words traveled far beyond it. One of the women who read them was a princess.53

Saint Clare Writes to a Princess in Prague

Saint Agnes of Prague was the daughter of the king of Bohemia. She could have married the Emperor. She turned him down and chose a life of poverty instead, and she founded a monastery in Prague built on the same poverty as San Damiano. In 1234 Saint Clare wrote to her for the first time. She wrote that news of the princess’s choice had spread through nearly the whole world. Saint Clare rejoiced at “a great and praiseworthy exchange”: to give up what passes away for what lasts forever.54

Soon the princess faced the pressure Saint Clare knew so well. Pope Gregory IX did not want to answer for a convent of women with no fixed income. So he gave her monastery the rich Hospital of Saint Francis in Prague, with all its revenues. The princess wrote to Saint Clare for help. Saint Clare told her to listen to no one who tried to turn her from her calling, even a man she had to respect. “Poor virgin, embrace the poor Christ,” she wrote. In 1238 the Pope gave way and granted the princess her own privilege of poverty.55

Saint Clare wrote her third letter that same year, and it is full of joy. “Place your mind in the mirror of eternity,” she told the princess. She wrote that by contemplation the princess could be changed into the image of God himself. Contemplation is quiet prayer that looks at God with love. Then Saint Clare turned to a practical question. The princess was fasting too hard, and Saint Clare knew that danger from the inside:56

“But our flesh is not bronze, and our strength is not the strength of stone. No, we are frail and open to every bodily weakness. So I ask you, dearest, and I beg you in the Lord, to draw back wisely and with discretion from the indiscreet and impossible strictness in fasting that I know you have taken up.”57

— Saint Clare to Saint Agnes of Prague, 1238

Nearly fifteen years passed before the last letter. Saint Clare said she was sorry it had been so long. She explained that there had been few messengers and the roads were dangerous. She wrote it on her deathbed in 1253, after nearly thirty years of illness. In it she told the princess to look every day into a mirror, and the mirror was Christ. In that mirror shone “blessed poverty, holy humility, and love beyond words.” Christ was born poor in a manger and died on a cross. “Remember your poor little mother,” Saint Clare wrote. Her sister Saint Agnes sent greetings too: “the most prudent virgin, Agnes, our sister.”58

Saint Clare’s Long Years of Illness

Her illness began nearly thirty years before her death. Years of hard penance had worn out her body, and for long stretches she could not get out of bed. In all those years she never grumbled or complained. Words of thanks and talk of God were always on her lips.59

She would not sit idle. Propped up in bed with cushions, she spun fine thread. Her thread was made into fine cloth, and the sisters sewed the cloth into altar linens called corporals. These are the small white cloths laid on the altar at Mass. The bishop blessed them, and the brothers carried them to churches all over the plain and hills around Assisi. Sister Francisca counted fifty pairs herself.60

One day she wanted a certain cloth brought to her, and there was no one nearby to bring it. The convent cat began tugging the cloth across the floor toward her. “Rascal, you don’t know how to carry it,” Saint Clare said. “Why are you dragging it on the ground?” Then the cat seemed to understand, and it began to roll the cloth up so that it would not drag. Saint Clare told this story to Francisca herself.61

While the Pope and his court were away in France, Saint Clare grew worse than ever, and her daughters were heartbroken. Then a nun at the convent of Saint Paul had a vision. It was the convent where Saint Clare had once clung to the altar. She saw Saint Clare lying ill at San Damiano, with the sisters weeping around her bed. A beautiful woman stood at the head of the bed. “Do not weep, daughters,” the woman said. “She cannot die until the Lord comes with his disciples.” Soon afterward the Pope came back to Italy and settled with his court at Perugia.62

In September 1252 Cardinal Rainaldo hurried from Perugia to see her. He was the protector of her order. He gave her Communion and spoke to the sisters. Saint Clare begged him through her tears to ask the Pope to confirm her privilege of poverty. He promised, and he kept his word. On 16 September he obtained the confirmation she wanted, in the form of a written Rule for her sisters. It was built on poverty, and Saint Clare had helped to shape it. All it lacked now was the Pope’s own seal.63

On Christmas night in 1252 she was too ill to get up. The sisters went to the chapel for Matins, the long night prayer of the feast, and they left her alone. She thought of the little Jesus and grieved that she could not join in his praises. “Lord God,” she sighed, “here I am, left alone with you in this place.”64

Suddenly she heard the music of the church of San Francesco, about two kilometers away. She heard the friars chanting the psalms and the voices of the singers, and she even heard the organ. She saw the manger of the Lord as well. When her daughters came back to her in the morning, she told them:65

“Blessed be the Lord Jesus Christ, who did not leave me when you left me. By the grace of Christ I really did hear all the services that were sung last night in the church of San Francesco.”66

— Saint Clare to her sisters, Christmas morning, 1252

It was the last Christmas of her life.67

The Pope Comes to Saint Clare’s Bed

In late April 1253, Pope Innocent IV and his cardinals came from Perugia to Assisi. The early Life saw the nun’s vision coming true. The Pope stood in the place of the Lord on earth, and the cardinals were like his disciples. Saint Clare had called her sister Saint Agnes back from Florence, and the two sisters were together again.68

Her dying lasted many days. Cardinals and bishops came to see her almost every day, and the devotion of the people grew. For seventeen days she could eat nothing at all. Yet she gave strength to everyone who came to her. One friar urged her to be patient in her long suffering, and she answered him:69

“Dearest brother, ever since I came to know the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ through his servant Francis, no suffering has troubled me. No penance has been hard, and no illness has been difficult.”70

— Saint Clare in her last illness, in the Life of Saint Clare

She asked the priests and friars to read her the Passion, the Gospel story of Christ’s suffering and death. Brother Juniper was a friar known as the Lord’s jester, and he often spoke fiery words about God. When he appeared, she was filled with new joy and asked him whether he had anything new to tell her about the Lord. Two of Saint Francis’s first companions stood by her bed. One of them comforted the others while he wept himself. The other kissed the bed.71

A few days before she died, the Pope himself came to San Damiano with his cardinals. He went to her bed and held out his hand for her to kiss. She kissed it gratefully and then asked to kiss his foot. The Pope stepped up onto a wooden footstool and held out his foot, and she kissed it above and below. Then she asked him to forgive all her sins. “I wish to God I had no more need of forgiveness,” he said, and he gave her full absolution and his blessing.72

That day she had also received Communion. When everyone had gone, she lifted her eyes to heaven, joined her hands, and spoke to her sisters through tears:73

“Praise the Lord, my little daughters. Today Christ has given me a gift so great that heaven and earth could not repay it. Today I have received the Most High himself, and I have seen his Vicar.”74

— Saint Clare after the Pope’s visit, August 1253

One wish was left. Saint Clare longed to see her Rule confirmed by a sealed letter from the Pope, called a bull. Sister Felipa had lived with her for thirty-eight years. She said Saint Clare wished she could kiss the bull one day and die the next. On 9 August 1253 Pope Innocent IV approved the Rule at Assisi, in the bull Solet annuere. Its eighth chapter said what Saint Clare had always said. The sisters must take nothing as their own, “neither a house nor a place nor anything.”75

A friar brought the sealed letter to San Damiano. Saint Clare took it reverently and raised it to her lips.76

The Death of Saint Clare

Her daughters stood around her bed and cried night and day. Saint Agnes begged her sister not to go and leave her. Saint Clare answered: “It pleases God that I go, dearest sister. But stop crying. You will come to the Lord soon after me. Before I leave you, the Lord will give you a great comfort.”77

On the Friday before she died, her niece Amada was alone with her. Saint Clare asked her, “Do you see the King of glory, whom I am seeing?” She asked it more than once.78

That night the sisters sat around her bed in tears. No one had spoken to her, but she began to speak softly. She was talking to her own soul:79

“Go out without fear. You have a good guide for your journey. Go out, for he who made you has made you holy. He has always protected you, and he loves you as tenderly as a mother loves her son. Blessed are you, Lord, for you have made me.”80

— Saint Clare to her own soul, in the Life of Saint Clare

A sister asked her whom she was talking to. “I am speaking to my blessed soul,” Saint Clare said. Then she spoke about the Trinity in words so deep that the sisters could not follow her. Felipa told another sister with a good memory to take in every word. Saint Clare heard her. “You will remember what I am saying now,” she told them, “as far as the One who makes me say it allows you.”81

Sister Benvenuta di Diambra sat by the bed, weeping. As she listened, she began to think about Saint Clare’s holiness. In her mind she pictured the whole court of heaven getting ready to honor her. She was wide awake. With her own eyes she saw a great crowd of virgins in white, with crowns on their heads. They came in through the door of the room. One of them was taller and more beautiful than the rest. On her crown was a golden ball shaped like a censer, a vessel for burning incense, and its light seemed to fill the whole house.82

The virgins came to the bed. The tallest covered Saint Clare with a cloth so fine that Saint Clare could still be seen through it. Sister Benvenuta called this tallest one the Virgin of virgins. The Virgin bent her face over Saint Clare’s face, or perhaps over her breast, and Sister Benvenuta could not tell which. Then they all vanished. Sister Benvenuta told no one what she had seen until she was questioned under oath.83

All through her last night Saint Clare encouraged her sisters and taught them. At the end she made a confession so beautiful that Felipa said she had never heard anything like it. She made it because she feared she might have failed in some way the faith promised at her baptism. She kept the Passion and the name of Jesus on her lips. Almost her last words went to the sister who had once borrowed her horsehair shirt. They came from the Psalms: “Precious in the eyes of the Lord is the death of his saints.”84

Saint Clare died at San Damiano on Monday, 11 August 1253. It was the day after the feast of Saint Lawrence. Felipa said that “the next day” after she kissed the Pope’s letter, Saint Clare “passed from this life to the Lord.” She went “truly Clara,” without stain or the darkness of sin, “to the clarity of the eternal light.”85

When the news spread, the people of Assisi ran to San Damiano in such crowds that the city seemed empty. They called her a saint, and many wept. The mayor came with knights and armed men, and they kept watch all evening and through the night so that no one could carry off her body.86

The next day the Pope came with his cardinals. When the friars began the Office of the Dead, the Pope stopped them. He said they should sing the Office of Virgins instead. These are the prayers for the feast of a virgin saint. It seemed as if he wanted to declare her a saint before she was even buried. Cardinal Rainaldo advised him to do what was usual, so a Mass for the Dead was sung, and the cardinal preached. Then her body was carried into the city with hymns and shouts of joy. It was laid in the church of San Giorgio, where Saint Francis had first been buried.87

Saint Clare’s Sisters Testify Under Oath

Crowds came to her tomb, blessing God. “She really was a saint,” they said. On 18 October 1253 Pope Innocent IV ordered Bishop Bartholomew of Spoleto to look into her life and her miracles. It was barely two months after her death. He wanted the truth written down faithfully in a public record, under the bishop’s seal. In the same letter the Pope said that her spiritual family had already spread “to almost every region of the world.”88

The bishop came to San Damiano in person on 24 November. Two of Saint Francis’s old companions were with him in the cloister as he put the sisters under oath. He asked each one how she knew what she said. Had she seen it herself? In what month, on what day? Between 24 and 29 November, twenty witnesses spoke. Most of them had lived with Saint Clare for twenty or thirty years or more.89

Saint Agnes was not among them. She had died on 16 November, ninety-seven days after her sister. As Saint Clare had promised, she followed her soon.90

The bishop also questioned people of the town in the church of San Paolo in Assisi. The last of them was Juan Ventura, on 29 November. He had been a man-at-arms in her father’s house when she was a girl. He told the bishop something he had seen after her death, which he thought was in September. A stranger from beyond the mountains was carried to her tomb, tied with ropes. He was raging or possessed. There he was healed at once. Juan Ventura did not know the man’s name, because he was not from those parts. But everyone in the square saw it, and they ran with the man to the tomb.91

The early Life tells of a young Frenchman from the Pope’s court who was carried to the tomb in the same way. He was tied to a stretcher with strong ropes, and his countrymen carried him there against his will. In front of the tomb he was set free, through the faith of the men who had brought him.92

Saint Clare Is Declared a Saint

The blind, the lame, and the troubled in mind came to her tomb, and the news of their cures spread. By then the Pope was Alexander IV. He was the same Cardinal Rainaldo who had won the confirmation of her Rule in 1252 and preached at her funeral. He had careful men examine her miracles and her life. Then he asked the cardinals and bishops for their judgment. They all agreed that she whom God had glorified in heaven should be glorified on earth.93

In 1255 Pope Alexander IV declared her a saint in the principal church of the town of Anagni. It was near the second anniversary of her death. In the bull that announced it, he played again and again on her name:94

“Clare, bright with bright merits, shines brightly: in heaven with the brightness of great glory, and on earth with the splendor of lofty miracles.”95

— Pope Alexander IV, declaring Saint Clare a saint, 1255

Her feast was first set for 12 August. The day before already belonged to Saint Rufino, the patron saint of Assisi. Today the Church keeps it on 11 August, the day she died.96

Assisi built a great new church in her honor, Santa Chiara. Her sisters moved into a new convent beside it so that they would not be parted from their mother’s body. In 1260 she was buried deep beneath its high altar. By an old law of the city, the leaders, clergy, and people of Assisi used to walk each year on 22 June to San Damiano. There they gave thanks for the city’s rescue through her prayers.97

The life she began at San Damiano did not stay there. Women who followed her way of poverty had already spread across Italy and beyond. Saint Agnes had led a house near Florence. Pacifica had spent a year training new sisters at Spello. In Prague a king’s daughter had kept the same poverty, with Saint Clare’s letters to guide her. Her daughters came to be called the Clares, after her, and the Rule she kissed on her deathbed became known as the Rule of Saint Clare.98

The parchment itself survived. In 1893, six hundred and forty years after her death, the original bull was found in the monastery of Santa Chiara. It was placed in a reliquary in the nuns’ choir. It is the answer Saint Clare gave Pope Gregory at San Damiano, finally sealed by a Pope: her sisters would own nothing at all. On the back of the parchment, someone of her own time wrote a short note:99

“Blessed Clare touched this and kissed it out of devotion many, many times.”100

— A note on the back of the bull that confirmed Saint Clare’s Rule

A Note on the Sources

Almost everything known about Saint Clare comes from two sources. The first is the sworn testimony of 1253. Its original Latin was not read for this life, so our English comes from a modern Spanish translation. The second is the Life of Saint Clare, written between 1255 and 1261 and traditionally ascribed to Thomas of Celano. Its author drew on that testimony and on the memories of Saint Francis’s companions, so the two sources are not independent.

The sources differ on a few points. Sister Benvenuta of Perugia said Saint Clare entered religious life on the Monday of Holy Week, while the Life gives the night after Palm Sunday. Gilliat-Smith dated her flight to 1211 and Robinson to 1212. Her birth year is uncertain: Gilliat-Smith thought 1193 most likely, within a range of 1190 to 1196, and others give 1194. Felipa said Saint Clare died the day after she kissed the bull, but the bull is dated 9 August, two days before her death. Copies of the bull that declared her a saint carry different dates in 1255. Beatrice, who testified in 1253, and Saint Agnes, who died just before the hearings, were two different sisters of Saint Clare.

How This Life Was Researched

This life rests on five sources in three languages: English, Latin, and Spanish. The chief one is the sworn testimony of twenty witnesses, taken at Assisi in November 1253. Most of them were sisters who had lived with Saint Clare for decades. The others are the Latin Life of Saint Clare and its English translation of 1910, which includes her Rule. A study published in 1914 and the Latin text of her four letters to Saint Agnes of Prague complete the list.
