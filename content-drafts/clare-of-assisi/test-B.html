Saint Clare Becomes Abbess Against Her Will

Saint Clare refused for three years to be called abbess, the mother who leads a house of nuns. She wanted to live under the other sisters rather than over them, and to serve rather than be served. Saint Francis kept pressing her. Pacifica said that in the end he "almost forced her," and so Saint Clare took charge of the sisters. Pacifica had grown up across the square from her and had entered the convent with her, and she was there when it happened. The new office brought fear into Saint Clare's heart, not pride.1

As abbess, she led by serving. She poured water over the sisters' hands and waited on them at table. She cared for the sick and cleaned their basins with her own hands. Giving orders was hard for her. When she had to give one, she gave it "with much fear and humility," and most of the time she did the task herself instead. At night she covered the sisters to protect them from the cold.2

She also washed the feet of the serving sisters when they came back from outside, and when she had washed them, she kissed them. One Thursday in Lent, she was washing a serving sister's feet and bent down to kiss them. The sister would not allow it. She pulled her foot back in a hurry and struck Saint Clare in the mouth by accident. Saint Clare gently took the foot again and kissed its sole. Sister Agnes of Opportulo saw it happen, and she said that Saint Clare rejoiced at the blow. Two other sisters who were present told the same story.3

In time Saint Clare's mother came to San Damiano too. Now a widow, Blessed Hortulana followed her daughter into the same Order. Pacifica had once gone on pilgrimage with her. She said that Hortulana lived among the sisters "in much humility" and died there.4

Saint Clare was hard on her own body. Under her plain wool tunic she secretly wore a garment of pigskin, with the cut bristles turned toward her flesh. At times she also wore a hair shirt, a rough undershirt worn as a penance. Hers was made of knotted horsehair and tied to her body with cords. Now and then she lent such garments to sisters who asked for them. Agnes of Opportulo had come to San Damiano as a very young girl. Once she borrowed the horsehair shirt for three days. It felt "so rough," she said, that she could not bear it at all. She gave it back more quickly than she had cheerfully asked for it.5

Saint Clare fasted just as hard. In Lent, and again in the Lent of Saint Martin, a second season of fasting, she lived on bread and water. On Sundays she drank a little wine, if there was any. For a long time she ate nothing at all on Mondays, Wednesdays, and Fridays. She slept on the bare ground or on vine cuttings, with a stone for a pillow. Her sisters wondered how her body stayed alive. They grieved over her long nights of prayer and her fasts, and Pacifica admitted that she had sometimes wept over them.6

At last the fasting made her ill. Then Saint Francis and the bishop of Assisi stepped in. They ordered her to eat something every day, even on those three days: at least half a small loaf, about an ounce and a half of bread. One sister was present when Saint Francis gave the command. She said that from then on Saint Clare took a little bread and water each day, to obey him.7

None of this made her gloomy. Through all her hardships she kept a pleasant and joyful face. She seemed not to feel them at all, or even to laugh at them. Her sisters said she was always glad in the Lord, and they never saw her upset.8

In time, even a Pope would try to persuade her to let her sisters own property.

Saint Clare Refuses the Pope's Offer

When she first left the world, Saint Clare had sold her share of her father's estate. She gave all the money to the poor and kept nothing for herself. From then on she wanted to have nothing but Christ, and she would not let her daughters own anything else either. She wanted her Order to carry the name of poverty. So she asked Pope Innocent III for a "privilege of poverty," a grant from the Pope that would let the sisters live without property. The Pope was delighted. He said that no one had ever asked a Pope for such a privilege before. To answer so rare a request, he wrote the first lines of it in his own hand.9

Each day the sisters lived on what others gave them. Some brothers of Saint Francis's Order went out to beg bread, oil, and other gifts for them. When these brothers came back with whole loaves, Saint Clare scolded them: "Who gave you these whole loaves?" She was happier with broken pieces and scraps.10

Pope Gregory IX loved Saint Clare like a father. Before he became Pope he was known as Cardinal Hugolino, the bishop of Ostia. The times were dangerous, he told her, and she should accept some property for the convent. He offered it freely himself. He wanted to give the sisters many things and to buy land for them, and he came to San Damiano in person to ask her. Two of her sisters were there and heard him. Saint Clare would not agree.11

So the Pope offered to free her from her vow, the solemn promise she had made to God. "If you fear your vow," he said, "we release you from it."12

"Holy Father," Saint Clare answered, "in no way do I ever want to be released from following Christ."13

Her sisters said that neither Pope Gregory nor the bishop of Ostia could ever persuade her to accept property. In the end the Pope gave way. On 17 September 1228 he granted her the privilege in writing. His letter to Saint Clare and her sisters said:14

He who feeds the birds of the sky and clothes the lilies of the field will not fail you in food or in clothing, until he comes and gives himself to you in eternity. … So, as you have asked, we confirm with our authority as Pope your resolve to live in the highest poverty. By this letter we grant you that no one can force you to accept possessions.15

Pope Gregory IX, letter to Saint Clare and the sisters of San Damiano, 17 September 1228

Saint Clare treasured that letter. One of her sisters said she honored the privilege with great reverence and guarded it with care, because she was afraid of losing it.16

In 1230 Pope Gregory forbade any friar to visit the sisters' convents without his permission. Saint Clare grieved, because now her daughters would hear the friars' teaching less often. She sighed and said: "Let him take all the friars from us, … now that he has taken away the ones who gave us the food of the soul."17

Then she sent all the friars back to their superior, even the brothers who begged bread for the sisters. If she could not keep the friars who brought the sisters bread for the soul, she would not keep the ones who brought bread for the body. When Pope Gregory heard what she had done, he softened his order at once. He left the matter to the head of the whole Order of friars.18

In the years that followed, the emperor's war came to the walls of San Damiano.

Saint Clare Faces the Emperor's Soldiers

The Emperor Frederick II was at war with the Church, and the valley of Spoleto, around Assisi, suffered again and again. By his command, bands of soldiers and Saracen archers, "numerous as bees," were stationed there to lay waste to castles and lay siege to cities. These Saracens came from the people who had once ruled Sicily and Calabria.19

The sisters of San Damiano were afraid of the Saracens, the Tartars, and the other enemies of God and the Church. Saint Clare gave them courage. She told them that their enemies could not harm them if God was with them. Then she promised that no harm would come to them, and she added: "If they come, put me in front of them."20

Once the danger came to Assisi itself. Vitalis of Aversa was a brave soldier who wanted fame. As the emperor's captain, he led the imperial army against the city. He cut down the trees and laid waste the whole countryside. Then he swore that he would not leave until he had taken Assisi, and people feared the city would fall. When Saint Clare heard this, she sighed deeply and called her sisters together. "We have received many good things from this city," she said, "and so we must pray to God to protect it."21

She told the sisters to come to her early in the morning. When they had gathered, she had ashes brought as a sign of sorrow for sin. She uncovered her head and told them all to do the same. Then she poured a great deal of ash on her own head, which had been freshly shaved, and put ashes on the head of each sister. "Go to our Lord," she told them, "and beg him with all your hearts to free the city." The sisters went to the chapel to pray. They spent that day fasting on bread and water, and some of them ate nothing at all.22

The next day the whole army broke up and fled in disorder. Vitalis left, against his own oath, and he never troubled that land again.23

Assisi remembered. Under an old law of the city, its magistrates, its clergy, and its citizens used to gather every year on 22 June at the church of Santa Chiara. From there they walked in procession to San Damiano for a Mass of thanksgiving, because God had saved the city through Saint Clare's prayers.24

On another day, the enemy came inside the convent. The army was already near the gates of Assisi when a band of Saracens climbed the wall of San Damiano. They came down into the cloister, the walled inner court of the convent. The sisters' hearts sank, and their voices trembled. In tears, they went to their mother.25

Saint Clare was gravely ill. Even so, she had herself led to the door of the dining hall to stand before the enemy. She ordered the sisters to bring the little box that held the Blessed Sacrament, the Body of Christ, and to set it before her. It was a silver box, enclosed in ivory. Two sisters held her up. One of them was Francisca. She later remembered that it was a Friday in September, about the hour of Terce, the mid-morning prayer.26

Saint Clare lay face down on the ground and prayed in tears:

Does it please you, my Lord, to hand over to the pagans your defenseless servants, whom I have fed with your love? Lord, I beg you, protect these servants of yours. I cannot protect them now.27

Saint Clare at the door of the dining hall (our translation)

Francisca heard the answer. It came in a voice of wonderful sweetness, like the voice of a little child: "I will always protect you." Then Saint Clare prayed for Assisi too: "My Lord, if it pleases you, protect this city as well, for it supports us for love of you." The same voice answered: "It will suffer troubles, but my protection will defend it."28

Saint Clare lifted her face, wet with tears, and turned to the weeping sisters. "Do not be afraid," she said. "I promise you that you will suffer no harm, now or in the future, as long as you obey God's commandments." At once the Saracens went back down the walls they had climbed. They left without harming anyone in the house.29

Francisca and the other sister who had held her both heard the voice. That evening Saint Clare called the two of them to her. She ordered them to tell no one about it for as long as she lived.30
