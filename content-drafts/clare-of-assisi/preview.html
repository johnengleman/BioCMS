Saint Clare of Assisi

Pope Gregory IX came in person to the convent of San Damiano at Assisi, and he came with an offer. Saint Clare was the abbess there, and he loved her like a father. The women in her care owned nothing at all and lived on bread and other scraps that the friars begged for them. The Pope thought the times were too dangerous for such a life. He wanted to give them property, and he was ready to buy it for them himself.1

Saint Clare refused, and one of her sisters stood by and heard the Pope press her to accept. He believed it was her vow of poverty that held her back, so he offered to free her from it. “If you fear your vow,” he said, “we release you from it.”2

“Holy Father,” Saint Clare answered, “in no way do I ever want to be released from following Christ.”3

She was a knight’s daughter. Seven knights belonged to her family’s house in Assisi, and her parents had meant to marry her to “great and powerful men.” She had walked out of that house one night as a girl of about eighteen, and she had sold her inheritance and given the money to the poor. Then she had followed Saint Francis of Assisi into a life with nothing in it but Christ. Now a pope was offering her a little security, and she would not take even that.4

Saint Clare held to that poverty for more than forty years. She led her sisters in one small house, and she never left it again. In her lifetime her way of life spread to “cities without number.” She asked one pope for the right to own nothing, and she defended it against the next. In letters to a king’s daughter in Prague she taught the same poverty. She was ill for nearly thirty years, and in her last year she was still asking the Church to protect her poverty. Two years after her death, the Pope declared her a saint.5

Her mother had been promised a light before Saint Clare was even born.

Saint Clare’s Mother Hears a Promise

Saint Clare’s mother was Blessed Hortulana, and she was a pilgrim. She had crossed the sea to pray at the holy places, and she had gone to the shrine of Saint Michael on Monte Gargano and to Rome. Her neighbor Pacifica traveled with her. Only the town square lay between their two houses, and Pacifica often talked with Saint Clare while she still lived at home.6

When Blessed Hortulana was expecting a child and her time was near, she went to a church to pray. She prayed before the cross and begged Christ to bring her safely through the danger of giving birth. Then she heard a voice.7

“Do not be afraid, woman. You will give birth safely to a light that will shine on the world more clearly.”8

— The voice heard by Saint Clare’s mother, in the Legend of Saint Clare

Blessed Hortulana took the words to heart. When the baby was baptized, she had her named Clara. She hoped the promised light would shine in her daughter. The name means clear and bright, and in English it is Clare. Years later Saint Clare told this story to her sisters herself. Blessed Hortulana told it too, and a sister named Cecilia heard it from her.9

Saint Clare was born in Assisi in about 1193, and her father was a knight named Favarone. On both her father’s side and her mother’s, she came from one of the noblest families in the city. Her father’s household was among the largest in Assisi, and it spent money freely.10

A man-at-arms named Juan Ventura lived in that house when she was a girl, and he saw what she did with her own food. She set it aside and hid it, and then she sent it to the poor. A woman named Bona lived with her, and Bona carried the food out for her many times. Saint Clare had no prayer beads, so she counted her prayers with a heap of pebbles. Under her soft and costly clothes she wore a little hair shirt. A hair shirt is a rough shirt worn next to the skin as a penance. Juan Ventura remembered a rough white cloth that she wore under her dress.11

She stayed indoors and kept out of sight of the people who passed the house. When she sat with her family, she always wanted to talk about God. She was also beautiful, and so her family began to look for a husband for her.12

Saint Clare Refuses to Marry

Her father, her mother, and her relatives all wanted a match worthy of her rank. A neighbor later said that they meant to marry her “splendidly, to great and powerful men.” She was about seventeen. Many relatives begged her to agree, but she never did.13

One of them was Ranieri di Bernardo. His wife was her relative, so he came and went freely in the house. He begged her many times to accept a husband, and more than forty years later he told the story under oath. She would not even listen to him. Instead she preached to him “contempt of the world.”14 The neighbor gave the reason: nobody could persuade her, because she wanted to stay a virgin and “live in poverty.”15

By then another man of Assisi had chosen poverty, and his name was becoming famous. He was Saint Francis. When Saint Clare heard what he had done, she decided in her heart to do the same. Saint Francis had heard of her as well, and he wanted to meet her. They met in secret, and they chose their times so that no one would know and no gossip would spread.16

Bona often walked with Saint Clare to see him. Bona said that they went secretly so that Saint Clare’s relatives would not see them. Saint Francis always urged Saint Clare to turn to Jesus Christ, and a friar named Brother Philip urged her too. She listened to them gladly.17 The Legend of Saint Clare was written soon after she was declared a saint, and it describes those visits.

“With only one trusted companion, the girl left her father’s house in secret. She often visited the man of God. His words seemed to her like fire, and his deeds seemed more than human.”18

— Legend of Saint Clare

Saint Francis spoke to her about Christ as a bridegroom and urged her to keep herself for Christ alone. She agreed without delay, and from then on she put herself wholly under the guidance of Saint Francis. As Palm Sunday drew near, she went to him and asked what she should do and how to do it.19

Saint Clare Leaves Home at Night

Saint Francis gave her a plan for Palm Sunday, the Sunday before Easter. On that feast she was to dress up in her finery and go with everyone else to the blessing of the palms. On the night after the feast, she was to leave.20

On Palm Sunday she went to church dressed for the feast, in the crowd of women. When the others pressed forward to receive their palm branches, Saint Clare stayed where she was. The Legend says that she held back out of modesty. Then the bishop came down the steps, went to her, and put a palm into her hands.21

That night she set out with some trusted companions. She did not use the usual door, because she was afraid someone would stop her. Instead she went to another door of the house, which was barred shut with heavy logs and a stone column. Sister Cristiana lived in that house at the time, and she later said that many men could hardly have moved them. Saint Clare moved them alone, “with the help of Jesus Christ,” and opened the door.22 The Legend adds that her own strength amazed her. The next morning people saw the open door, and many of them marveled that a young girl had opened it.23

She hurried to the small church of Saint Mary of the Porziuncola. Saint Francis’s friars were keeping watch there in prayer at the little altar, and they met her with lighted torches. There she put aside her fine clothes and her ornaments, and her hair was cut off. Her sister Beatrice later said that Saint Francis cut it himself before the altar. Saint Clare received the robe of penance and gave herself to Christ. She was about eighteen years old, and it was about the year 1211.24

Saint Francis did not keep her at the Porziuncola. He took her at once to the church of San Paolo, a house of Benedictine nuns, to stay there until God gave her another place. Her family learned where she had gone.25

Saint Clare’s Family Tries to Take Her Back

Her relatives condemned what she had done, and they banded together and ran to San Paolo. They tried force, harmful advice, and sweet promises, and they told her that such a poor life was unworthy of her birth.26

“But Clare took hold of the altar cloths and bared her shorn head. She declared that nothing would ever again separate her from the service of Christ. Her courage grew as her family’s war against her grew stronger.”27

— Legend of Saint Clare

Her sister Beatrice told the same story under oath. Beatrice said that Saint Clare would not agree to anything, would not let them take her out of the church, and would not go home with them. Ranieri had once begged her to marry, and he testified about this day too. He said that they let her stay when she showed them her shaven head.28 The Legend says the struggle went on for many days until her family gave up.29

A few days later she moved to the church of Sant’Angelo di Panzo. Saint Francis took her there with Brother Philip and Brother Bernard.30 At home she had left a young sister, Saint Agnes. The two sisters were very different. Still they loved each other dearly, and the parting hurt them both. Saint Clare begged God to let her sister follow her. Sixteen days after Saint Clare left home, Saint Agnes came to her. Saint Agnes said that she wanted to give herself wholly to God. Saint Clare embraced her with joy. “I thank God, sweetest sister,” she said, “that he has heard my worry for you.”31

The next day twelve furious men of the family came to Panzo. They had already given up on Saint Clare, so they turned to Saint Agnes and ordered her home. She refused to leave her sister. One of the knights struck her and kicked her and tried to drag her away by the hair. “Help me, dearest sister,” Saint Agnes cried. “Do not let me be taken from Christ the Lord.”32

The Legend tells what happened next. While the men dragged Saint Agnes down the hillside, Saint Clare threw herself to the ground and prayed with tears. Then the body of Saint Agnes seemed fixed to the ground, and the men could not carry her across a little stream. Her uncle Monaldo raised his arm to strike her, and a terrible pain seized his arm. At last Saint Clare came and asked her relatives to leave Saint Agnes in her care, and they went away bitter. Saint Francis cut Saint Agnes’s hair with his own hands.33

Saint Clare’s mind was not fully at rest at Panzo. On Saint Francis’s advice she moved to the church of San Damiano. Saint Francis had once worked hard to repair that church. While he was praying there, a voice had spoken to him from the wooden cross: “Francis, go and repair my house. As you see, it is completely destroyed.” Now Saint Clare settled in the same place and never again looked for another home. She would live in that small house for forty-two years.34

Saint Clare Becomes Abbess Against Her Will

Other women came to San Damiano. Pacifica had lived across the square from Saint Clare’s family, and she entered the religious life with Saint Clare. A woman named Felipa came four years later, because Saint Clare had helped her think about how Christ suffered and died on the cross to save all people. In time Blessed Hortulana entered the same Order, and Saint Clare’s sister Beatrice came to San Damiano too. Her niece Amada came on her advice. Saint Clare told Amada that she had asked God not to let the world deceive Amada.35

The sisters needed someone to lead them. The head of a convent of nuns is called an abbess. For three years Saint Clare refused that name and that office, because she wanted to be under others, not over them. Saint Francis kept pressing her until she gave in. Pacifica was there, and she said that he “almost forced her.” The Legend says that “fear, not pride,” rose in her heart.36

She led by serving. She poured water over the sisters’ hands and waited on them at table. With her own hands she cleaned the basins of the sick sisters. When she gave an order, she gave it “with much fear and humility,” and most of the time she preferred to do the work herself. At night she covered the sisters to protect them from the cold. If a sister’s robe was poorer than her own, she took it and gave the sister her better one.37

She also guarded their hearts. When she saw a sister tempted or troubled, she called her aside in secret and comforted her with tears. Sometimes she threw herself at the sister’s feet. While she was well, she got up for prayer at midnight and woke the sisters without a word. When she came back from prayer, the sisters were as happy as if she had come from heaven.38

The serving sisters were the ones who went out of the convent on its errands. When they came home, Saint Clare washed their feet and kissed them. One Thursday in Lent she was washing a serving sister’s feet, and she bent to kiss them as she always did. The sister could not bear such humility and pulled her foot away, and it struck Saint Clare in the mouth. Saint Clare gently took the foot again and kissed its sole.39 A sister named Agnes saw it happen. She was the daughter of a man of Assisi called Opportulo, and she had come to the convent as a little girl. She said that Saint Clare “rejoiced” at the blow.40

Saint Clare was hard on herself. For a long time she ate nothing at all on Mondays, Wednesdays, and Fridays, and on the other days she lived on bread and water. She slept on the ground with a stone for a pillow, and for a while her bed was a heap of vine cuttings. Under her robe she sometimes wore pigskin with the bristles turned in against her body. At other times she wore a shirt of knotted horsehair, tied on with rough cords.41

Saint Clare once lent the horsehair shirt to Sister Agnes of Opportulo for three days, and Agnes found it so rough that she could not bear it at all.42 The Legend tells of a sister who asked for the shirt and then gave it back after three days, more readily than she had asked for it.43

Her sisters watched all this with sorrow. They “grieved and lamented” over her long nights of prayer and her fasting, and Pacifica admitted that she had sometimes cried over it. At last Saint Clare made herself ill, and Saint Francis and the bishop of Assisi gave her an order. On her fast days she had to eat at least half a small loaf, about an ounce and a half of bread, and she obeyed.44

Through it all she stayed cheerful. Sister Felipa said that she was “always glad in the Lord” and was never seen upset. The Legend says that she seemed either not to feel her hardships or to laugh at them. Her courage reached further still. When news came that friars had been martyred in Morocco, she said she wanted to go there herself. This was before her long illness, and Sister Cecilia cried at the thought.45

The greater danger to her way of life came from people who loved her.

Saint Clare Refuses the Pope’s Offer

From the start, poverty was at the center of everything. Saint Clare had sold the inheritance that came to her and given all the money to the poor, keeping nothing for herself. She sold part of her sister Beatrice’s inheritance too. The Legend says she wanted to have nothing but the Lord Christ, and she would not let her daughters own anything else either. She often told them that the community would please God only when it was “rich in poverty.”46

The friars begged bread for the sisters, and sometimes they came home with whole loaves. Saint Clare met them with a reproach: “Who gave you these whole loaves?” She would rather receive the broken pieces.47

She wanted her Order to carry the name of poverty, so she asked Pope Innocent III for a privilege of poverty. A privilege was a special grant from the Pope. The Pope was delighted, and he said that no one had ever asked a pope for such a thing before. Then he wrote the first lines of her privilege with his own hand.48

Saint Francis had written the sisters a short way of life, and a little before he died he wrote to them again with his last wish. His words stand in her own Rule, the written way of life of her community.49

“I, little Brother Francis, want to follow the life and poverty of Jesus Christ our Most High Lord and of his most holy Mother, and to persevere in it until the end. And I beg you all, my ladies, and I advise you, to live always in this highest life and poverty. Watch yourselves well, so that you never leave it in any way because of anyone’s teaching or advice.”50

— Saint Francis to the sisters of San Damiano, in the Rule of Saint Clare

After Saint Francis died, Pope Gregory IX came to San Damiano himself. Pacifica saw and heard him try to give Saint Clare many things and buy property for the convent, but Saint Clare would never agree. Neither the Pope nor the bishop of Ostia could ever persuade her. In this struggle the Pope offered to free her from her vow, and she told him she never wanted to be freed from following Christ.51

In September 1228 Pope Gregory gave way and granted her a privilege of poverty in writing. Under it no one could ever force her to accept possessions.52 Sister Felipa said that Saint Clare honored that grant and guarded it with care, because she was afraid of losing it.53

In 1230 Pope Gregory forbade any friar to visit the convents of Saint Clare’s sisters without his permission. The sisters would now hear the friars preach less often, and Saint Clare sighed. “Let him take all the friars from us,” she said, “now that he has taken away the ones who gave us the food of the soul.” Then she sent every friar back to his minister, even the ones who begged bread for the sisters. When Pope Gregory heard of it, he quickly softened his order.54

Saint Clare could stand up to a pope with words, but the next danger came over the convent wall.

Enemy Soldiers Climb Into San Damiano

In those years the Church suffered much under the Emperor Frederick, and the valley of Spoleto was attacked again and again. Bands of his soldiers and Saracen archers laid waste to castles and besieged cities at his command. The Saracens in his army were descendants of Saracens who had once dominated Sicily.55 Saint Clare had already told her sisters what to do if enemies came. “If they come,” she said, “put me in front of them.”56

One day the army came against Assisi. Some Saracens climbed the wall of San Damiano and came down into the cloister, the enclosed court inside the convent. The sisters were terrified, and they went to their mother in tears.57

Saint Clare was ill, but she had herself brought to the door of the sisters’ dining hall. She ordered that the Blessed Sacrament be carried before her. It was the Body of our Lord Jesus Christ, kept in a little box of silver set in ivory. Two sisters held her up, Sister Francisca and Sister Illuminata of Pisa. Then Saint Clare lay on the ground and prayed with tears.58 The Legend gives her prayer.

“Does it please you, my Lord, to hand over to the pagans your defenseless servants, whom I have fed with your love? Lord, I beg you, protect these servants of yours. I cannot protect them now.” Then a voice like a little child’s sounded in her ears: “I will always protect you.” “My Lord,” she said, “protect this city too, if it pleases you, for it supports us for love of you.” And the Lord answered: “It will suffer troubles, but my protection will defend it.”59

— Legend of Saint Clare

Sister Francisca heard the voice herself, and she said it was a voice of wonderful sweetness. Then Saint Clare turned to her sisters. “Do not be afraid,” she told them. “I promise you that you will suffer no harm, now or in the future, as long as you obey God’s commandments.” The Saracens went away without doing harm to anyone. That evening Saint Clare called the two sisters who had heard the voice, and she ordered them to tell no one as long as she lived.60

Sister Francisca told the story under oath after Saint Clare’s death. She could not remember the year. She thought it was a Friday in September at about the hour of the midmorning prayer.61

Another time an imperial captain named Vitalis of Aversa brought a great army against Assisi. He swore he would not leave until he had taken the city. Saint Clare called her sisters. “We have received many good things from this city,” she said, “and so we must pray to God to protect it.” She told them to come to her at dawn. When they had gathered, she had ashes brought. She uncovered her shaved head and put plenty of ashes on it. Then she put ashes on the head of every sister and sent them all to pray. The next day Vitalis left with his whole army.62

The people of Assisi believed that her prayers had saved both the convent and the city. Her fame had already gone far beyond Assisi, and the Legend says it reached the palaces of duchesses and even the private rooms of queens.63

Letters to a Princess in Prague

Saint Agnes of Prague was a daughter of the king of Bohemia, and she could have married the emperor himself. Instead she chose poverty and founded a convent of Saint Clare’s Order in Prague.64 Saint Clare wrote to her from San Damiano. Four of those letters survive, and in them Saint Clare speaks in her own words.65

Saint Clare wrote her first letter in 1234. In it she said that the news of the princess’s choice had reached her and had spread “through nearly the whole world.” Saint Clare called that choice “a great and praiseworthy exchange.” It meant giving up the things of time for the things that last forever, and receiving a hundred for one.66

Soon Saint Agnes of Prague met the same pressure Saint Clare had met. Pope Gregory gave her convent the income of a rich hospital, and she wrote to Saint Clare for help.67 Saint Clare answered that the princess might receive advice that would hold her back from God’s call. She should respect the person who gave it and still not follow it. “Poor virgin, embrace the poor Christ,” Saint Clare wrote.68 In 1238 the Pope granted her convent a privilege of poverty too.69

Saint Clare wrote her third letter in 1238, and in it she taught the princess how to pray. “Place your mind in the mirror of eternity,” she wrote. Through contemplation the princess was to transform her whole self into the image of God. Contemplation is quiet prayer that looks at God with love.70 Saint Clare had once made herself ill by fasting, and now she gave the princess this advice.

“But our flesh is not bronze, and our strength is not the strength of stone. No, we are frail and open to every bodily weakness. So I ask you, dearest, and I beg you in the Lord, to draw back wisely and with discretion from the indiscreet and impossible strictness in fasting that I know you have taken up.”71

— Saint Clare, third letter to Saint Agnes of Prague, 1238

Nearly fifteen years passed before the last letter, and in it Saint Clare apologized for writing so rarely. She wrote that there were few messengers and that the roads were dangerous. Then she told the princess to look into a mirror every day, and the mirror was Christ. In that mirror shine “blessed poverty, holy humility, and love beyond words.” The mirror begins with poverty: the Lord “placed in a manger” and wrapped in poor little cloths.72

Near the end she wrote as a mother: “Remember your poor little mother.” Saint Clare wrote that she had inscribed the princess’s memory on the tablets of her heart, and that she held her dearer than anyone. Saint Clare’s sister Saint Agnes was at her side. Saint Clare wrote that her daughters sent greetings, above all “the most prudent virgin, Agnes, our sister.”73 She wrote that letter in 1253 on her deathbed, after nearly thirty years of illness.74

Saint Clare’s Long Years of Illness

The long illness began about twenty-nine years before her death, and often she could not get out of bed at all. Even then she would not be idle. She had herself raised up and propped with pillows, and she sat and spun linen thread. Her thread was made into fine cloth, and the sisters sewed the cloth into altar linens called corporals. The friars carried them to almost every church in the valley and the hills around Assisi, and Sister Francisca counted fifty pairs of them herself.75

One day she wanted a certain cloth, and there was no one nearby to bring it to her. The convent had a little cat, and it began to tug the cloth and drag it toward her. “Rascal,” Saint Clare said to the cat, “you don’t know how to carry it. Why are you dragging it on the ground?” As if it had understood her, the cat began to roll the cloth up so that the cloth would not touch the floor. Saint Clare told this story to Sister Francisca herself.76

She taught her sisters to look for God in everything. When she sent the serving sisters out of the convent, she told them to praise God at the sight of beautiful trees in flower and leaf. They were also to praise him for people and every other creature, “always, for all things and in all things.”77 She said that our Lord was nailed to the cross at noon, so she prayed especially at that hour.78

While the Pope was away at Lyons, she grew much worse. At that time a Benedictine nun of San Paolo had a vision. In it the nun and her sisters stood weeping at Saint Clare’s bed at San Damiano, and a beautiful woman at the head of the bed spoke to them. “Do not weep, daughters, for her who will conquer,” she said. “She cannot die until the Lord comes with his disciples.”79

In September 1252 Cardinal Rainaldo came to see her. He was the bishop of Ostia and the cardinal charged with protecting her Order, and he gave her Holy Communion. With tears she begged him to remember her soul and the souls of her sisters. Above all, she begged him to ask the Pope and the cardinals to confirm her privilege of poverty. He promised, and he kept his word.80

On Christmas night in 1252 she was too ill to get up. The sisters went to the chapel for the night prayer of the feast, called Matins, and she was left alone. She thought about the child Jesus, and it grieved her that she could not be there to praise him. “Lord God,” she sighed, “here I am, left alone with you in this place.”81

Then she began to hear the service at San Francesco, the friars’ church, about two kilometers away. She heard the friars sing the psalms and the responses, and she heard the organ, as if she were there. She told Sister Amada that she had also seen the manger of the Lord that night.82 In the morning her daughters came to her.

“Blessed be the Lord Jesus Christ, who did not leave me when you left me. By the grace of Christ I really did hear all the services that were sung last night in the church of San Francesco.”83

— Saint Clare to her sisters, Christmas morning, 1252

A few months later the Pope himself was on his way to Assisi.

The Pope Comes to Her Bed

In 1253 Pope Innocent IV and his cardinals moved from Perugia to Assisi. The Legend says that this fulfilled the nun’s vision. It explains that the Pope stands in the place of the Lord and that the cardinals are like his disciples. Pope Innocent came with his cardinals to San Damiano to see Saint Clare.84

He went to her bed and held out his hand for her to kiss. She kissed it gratefully and asked to kiss his foot as well. The Pope stepped up onto a wooden stool and held out his foot, and Saint Clare bowed her head and kissed it on top and underneath. Then she asked him to forgive all her sins. “I wish to God I had no more need of forgiveness,” he said, and he gave her absolution and his blessing.85

That same day she received Holy Communion from the head of the friars in that province. When everyone else had gone, she raised her eyes to heaven and joined her hands. With tears she spoke to her sisters. “Praise the Lord, my little daughters,” she said. “Today Christ has given me a gift so great that heaven and earth could not repay it. Today I have received the Most High himself, and I have seen his Vicar.”86

One wish remained. Cardinal Rainaldo had drawn up a Rule for her sisters in 1252, and poverty was at its heart. Saint Clare called all her sisters to her and urged them earnestly to keep the privilege of poverty. Sister Felipa said that Saint Clare longed to see the Rule confirmed in a papal bull, a formal letter sealed by the Pope. She wanted to kiss the bull one day and die the next.87

On 9 August 1253 Pope Innocent approved her Rule in a bull called Solet annuere. Two short notes on the top edge of the parchment ordered it done, and one scholar judged both notes to be in the Pope’s own hand. The Pope wanted the bull to reach her quickly.88 A friar came to San Damiano with the sealed letter, and Saint Clare took it with reverence and raised the bull to her lips. Sister Felipa said simply that “the next day” she “passed from this life to the Lord.”89

The Death of Saint Clare

Her last days were long, and for seventeen days she could take no food. Cardinals and bishops came to see her every day, and the people grew more devoted. Her daughters stood around her bed and wept night and day. Saint Clare had called her sister Saint Agnes back from Florence early that year, and now Saint Agnes begged her not to go and leave her behind.90 “It pleases God that I go, dearest sister,” Saint Clare answered. “But stop crying. You will come to the Lord soon after me. Before I leave you, the Lord will give you a great comfort.”91

A friar urged her to be patient in her long suffering, and in her answer she looked back across her whole life.

“Dearest brother, ever since I came to know the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ through his servant Francis, no suffering has troubled me. No penance has been hard, and no illness has been difficult.”92

— Saint Clare to a friar in her last illness, in the Legend of Saint Clare

She asked the priests and friars to read her the Passion of the Lord, the Gospel story of his suffering and death. Brother Juniper came, and the Legend calls him the Lord’s famous jester. Saint Clare was filled with fresh joy and asked him if he had anything new to say about the Lord. Two companions of Saint Francis stood near her. Brother Angelo comforted the others though he was crying himself. Brother Leo kissed her bed. She praised God for all his gifts and urged her daughters to keep the poverty of the Lord. Then she blessed everyone who had been kind to her, and all the sisters of her Order, present and to come.93

On the Friday before she died, Sister Amada was alone with her. “Do you see the King of glory, whom I am seeing?” Saint Clare asked her, and she asked it more than once.94 The Legend also records the words she spoke to her own soul.

“Then the most holy virgin turned to herself and spoke softly to her own soul. ‘Go out without fear,’ she said. ‘You have a good guide for your journey. Go out, for he who made you has made you holy. He has always protected you, and he loves you as tenderly as a mother loves her son. Blessed are you, Lord,’ she said, ‘for you have made me.’ One of the sisters asked her whom she was speaking to. Clare answered: ‘I am speaking to my blessed soul.’ And her glorious guide was not far away.”95

— Legend of Saint Clare

Sister Benvenuta di Diambra was sitting by the bed that Friday at nightfall, wide awake. Two years before, Saint Clare had healed her of sores that she had suffered for twelve years. Now Benvenuta saw a great crowd of virgins dressed in white come in through the door, with crowns on their heads. One was taller and more beautiful than all the rest, and Benvenuta called her the Virgin of virgins. On her crown was a golden ball like a censer, and its light seemed to fill the whole house. A censer is the vessel that holds burning incense. The tallest one covered Saint Clare with a cloth so fine that Saint Clare could still be seen through it. Then she bent her face over Saint Clare’s face, or perhaps over her breast, for Benvenuta could not tell which. After that they all vanished. Benvenuta told no one until she gave her sworn testimony.96

Sister Felipa said that all through her last night, Saint Clare counseled the sisters and preached to them. At the end she made her confession, because she was afraid that she had failed in some way in the faith she had promised at her baptism. Felipa said she had never heard a confession so beautiful. Sister Agnes of Opportulo was with her too, and Saint Clare asked her to say the prayer of the five wounds of the Lord. As far as anyone could hear, Saint Clare kept the Passion and the name of Jesus on her lips. Almost her last words to Sister Agnes came from the psalms: “Precious in the eyes of the Lord is the death of his saints.”97

Saint Clare died on 11 August 1253, the day after the feast of Saint Lawrence. Sister Benvenuta di Diambra remembered it as the Monday after her vision.98

The news shook the whole city, and men and women crowded to San Damiano until the town seemed empty. The mayor came with knights and armed men. They kept watch all that evening and all night so that they would not lose “the precious treasure” that lay inside.99

The next day the Pope came with his cardinals. When the friars began to pray the Office of the Dead for her, he stopped them and said they should pray the Office of Virgins instead. It seemed that he wanted to honor her as a saint before she was even buried. Cardinal Rainaldo answered that they should do what was usual, so the Mass for the Dead was said, and the cardinal preached on the vanity of the world. Then her body was carried to the church of San Giorgio in the city, with hymns and the shouts of the crowds. The body of Saint Francis had first been laid there too. Afterward people came to her tomb in crowds and blessed God. “She truly was a saint,” they said, and they asked her to pray for them to Christ.100

Saint Clare’s Sisters Testify Under Oath

Saint Clare had told her sister Saint Agnes that she would come to the Lord soon after her. Saint Agnes died on 16 November 1253, ninety-seven days after Saint Clare.101 By then the Church had already begun to examine Saint Clare’s life. On 18 October 1253 Pope Innocent IV wrote to Bishop Bartholomew of Spoleto. He told the bishop to find out the truth about Saint Clare’s life, her conversion, her life in religion, and her miracles. Everything was to be faithfully copied into a public record and sent to the Pope under the bishop’s seal.102

On 24 November the bishop came in person to San Damiano, and in the cloister Saint Clare’s sisters swore to tell the truth. Brother Leo and Brother Angelo were among those present. Over the next days twenty witnesses spoke. Each was asked how they knew what they said, when it had happened, and who else had been there. Pacifica told of the girl across the square. Beatrice told of the altar cloths, and Francisca told of the voice at the dining hall door.103

On 29 November the hearings moved to the church of San Paolo in Assisi, and Juan Ventura swore there. Long ago he had been the man-at-arms who saw a young girl hide her food for the poor. Now he told of something he had seen after her death, which he thought was in September. A man from beyond the mountains was carried to her tomb tied with ropes, raging or possessed, and at the tomb he was healed at once. Ventura did not know his name, because the man was not from those parts. He said that everyone in the square saw it and ran with the man to the tomb.104

Sister Felipa closed her testimony with a play on the saint’s name. She said that Saint Clare was “truly Clara” and had passed “to the clarity of the eternal light.”105 Her sisters had put on record what they saw, and now the decision belonged to the Pope.

The Pope Declares Her a Saint

By 1255 the Pope was Alexander IV. Before his election he had been Cardinal Rainaldo, the protector of her Order, and he had drawn up her Rule and asked the Pope to approve it. He had also preached at her funeral.106 The Legend says that he and the cardinals studied her life and her miracles, and all the Church leaders agreed. In 1255 the second anniversary of her death drew near. Then Pope Alexander declared her a saint in the main church of Anagni, and he ordered her feast to be kept throughout the Church.107 In his bull the Pope played on her name.

“Clare, bright with bright merits, shines brightly: in heaven with the brightness of great glory, and on earth with the splendor of lofty miracles.”108

— Pope Alexander IV, bull of canonization, 1255

She had died on 11 August. That day already belonged to Saint Rufino as the patron of Assisi, so her feast was first set for 12 August.109 The Pope also ordered her Legend to be written. Its author read the record of the inquiry, and he also went to the companions of Saint Francis and to her sisters.110

Her way of life had spread long before she died. Saint Agnes had gone to lead a convent near Florence in about 1219, and Pacifica spent a year at the convent of Spello to train the sisters there. A sister named Balbina spent a year and five months at Arezzo. In 1253 Pope Innocent wrote that her daughters had spread to almost every region of the world.111 Her Rule of 1253 said in its eighth chapter what she had said all her life.

“The sisters shall not take anything as their own, neither a house nor a place nor anything. … This is that summit of the highest poverty which has made you, my dearest sisters, heirs and queens of the kingdom of heaven.”112

— Rule of Saint Clare, chapter 8

People brought their needs to her tomb. The Legend tells of some who were healed there: a man blind for twelve years, a woman with a withered hand, a man with epilepsy, and others tormented by demons. Assisi remembered her as its protector. An ancient law of the city set a yearly procession for 22 June, when the city’s leaders, clergy, and people walked from the church of Santa Chiara to San Damiano. At a solemn Mass there they gave thanks that her prayers had saved their city. Artists usually show her holding the Blessed Sacrament, because of the day the soldiers came over the wall.113

Two of the documents she fought for survived her. Pope Gregory’s privilege of poverty of 1228 was kept in the nuns’ choir at Assisi. In 1893 the bull of her Rule was found at the convent of Santa Chiara, and it was placed in a reliquary in the nuns’ choir.114 She had once told a pope that she never wanted to be released from following Christ. In her last days another pope wrote that way of life into law, and she held the letter to her lips. Someone from her own time wrote a short note on the back of the faded parchment.115

“Blessed Clare touched this and kissed it out of devotion many, many times.”116

— Note on the back of the bull Solet annuere, 1253

A Note on the Sources

The oldest record of Saint Clare’s life is the sworn testimony of twenty witnesses, taken in November 1253. Its Latin text was not read for this life. The story follows a modern Spanish translation, and our English comes from that Spanish. The Legend of Saint Clare was written soon after her canonization and is traditionally ascribed to Thomas of Celano. Its author drew on the testimony and on her companions, so the two often agree. The Legend sets her flight on the night after Palm Sunday. Sister Benvenuta of Perugia remembered that Saint Clare entered religion on the Monday of Holy Week. Her birth year is Gilliat-Smith’s estimate, and the year she left home is counted from the witnesses’ “forty-two years.” Robinson placed her flight in 1212. The sources give no year for the soldiers at San Damiano or for the siege by Vitalis. Her Rule’s bull is dated 9 August, two days before her death. The Legend dates her canonization near the anniversary of her death, but copies of the bull carry later dates in 1255.

How This Life Was Researched

This life rests on five sources in three languages: English, Latin, and Spanish. The first is the sworn testimony of twenty witnesses from 1253. Two more are the Legend of Saint Clare in Latin and its English translation of 1910. The last two are a study of her life and Rule from 1914 and Saint Clare’s four letters to Saint Agnes of Prague in Latin. Every quotation from the Latin and the Spanish is our own translation. Every quotation from older English has been put into modern English.
