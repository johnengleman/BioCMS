Pope Gregory IX loved Saint Clare as a father loves his daughter. He told her that the times were hard and dangerous. He urged her community to accept some property, and he offered to give it himself. Saint Clare would not agree in any way. She was bound by a vow of poverty, a solemn promise made to God. The Pope had an answer for that. “If you fear your vow,” he said, “we release you from it.” Saint Clare answered, “Holy Father, in no way do I ever want to be released from following Christ.”1

Saint Clare had already turned down a great deal. She came from one of the noblest families of Assisi, and seven knights belonged to her family’s house. Her father and mother had hoped to marry her in splendid style to “great and powerful men.”2 She would not hear of it. One night she left her father’s house in secret and went to Saint Francis, who had chosen to be poor.3

For forty-two years she lived at San Damiano, a small church at Assisi. Women came there to share her life. Their way of life spread, and monasteries rose in “cities without number.”4 Pope Innocent III granted her community the right to own nothing at all. He told her that no one had ever asked a pope for such a privilege before. He was so glad of her request that he wrote the first lines of the grant in his own hand.5

Saint Clare guarded that poverty for the rest of her life. She wrote about it to Saint Agnes of Prague, a king’s daughter who had turned down an emperor to choose the same life. Late in her life she begged above all that the Pope confirm her privilege of poverty. Two years after her death, Pope Alexander IV declared her a saint.6

Saint Clare’s Mother Hears a Promise

Saint Clare was born about 1193 in Assisi, a city in the Italian region of Umbria. Her father was a knight named Favarone. Both sides of her family were of knightly blood, and they were rich in land and goods. Her mother was Blessed Hortulana. Though she had a husband and a household to care for, she gave as much of her time to God as she could. She had crossed the sea with other pilgrims to pray in the places where Christ had walked, and she came home full of joy. She also went to pray at the shrine of Saint Michael on Monte Gargano, and she visited the shrines of the Apostles in Rome.7

When Blessed Hortulana was expecting her child and the birth was near, she went into a church to pray. Standing before the cross, she begged the crucified Lord to bring her safely through the dangers of childbirth. Then she heard a voice speak to her:8

“Do not be afraid, woman. You will give birth safely to a light that will shine on the world more clearly.”9

Because of these words, Blessed Hortulana had her daughter named Clara at baptism. The name means bright or clear. Her mother hoped that the promised light would somehow shine out in her daughter’s life, as God willed. Years later, Saint Clare told this story to the sisters of her convent. One of those sisters had also heard it from Blessed Hortulana herself.10

Saint Clare learned the first truths of the faith from her mother. As a girl she loved the poor. Her father’s house was one of the greatest in the city, and it spent freely. When good food was set before her, she would say she was eating it. Then she hid it away and sent it to the poor in secret. Her companion Bona carried it to them many times. Before Saint Clare left home, she sent Bona on a pilgrimage to the shrine of Saint James at Compostela. Bona said that Saint Clare was full of grace and wanted others to share it. Once she also gave Bona money for the men working at Saint Mary of the Porziuncola, a church outside the city, so that they could buy meat. Saint Clare had no beads for counting her prayers to God, so she counted them on a little pile of stones.11

Under her soft and costly clothes she wore a hair shirt, a rough garment worn against the skin as a penance. A soldier who served in her father’s house remembered it as a rough white cloth. He also saw her fast and pray. She stayed at home and kept out of sight of the people who passed the house. When she sat with the household, she always wanted to talk about the things of God. Without seeking it, she began to win the praise of her neighbors.12

Saint Clare Refuses to Marry

Saint Clare was beautiful, and her family began to look for a husband for her. Her father was noble and powerful in the city, and so were the other men of his house. The family wanted a splendid match that would suit their rank. At the time she was about seventeen. Many of her relatives begged her to agree, but she never did.13

One man came and went freely in the house, because his wife was a relative of hers. He pleaded with Saint Clare many times to accept a husband. She would not even listen to him. Instead she preached to him “contempt of the world.” That old phrase means that the world and all it offers are worth nothing next to God. More than forty years later he told the story under oath.14

An early Life of Saint Clare was written a few years after her death. It tells how she held her family off. She let them think that she only wanted to wait a while before marrying. Meanwhile she gave her virginity to God. A neighbor of the family explained why no one could persuade her. She wanted to stay a virgin and to live in poverty.15

By then the name of Saint Francis was famous in Assisi. Saint Clare heard that he had chosen the way of poverty, and she decided in her heart to do the same. She longed to see him and to hear him speak. Saint Francis had heard of her goodness too. He wanted to speak with her and win her for God.16

They met many times and took care that no one should find out. Bona often went along with her. The two went in secret so that her relatives would not see them. Saint Francis always urged her to turn to Jesus Christ. So did Brother Philip, one of his followers. Saint Clare listened to them gladly and agreed with all the good things they told her.17 The early Life describes these visits:

“With only one trusted companion, the girl left her father’s house in secret. She often visited the man of God. His words seemed to her like fire, and his deeds seemed more than human.”18

Saint Francis showed her how empty the hopes of this world are and how its beauty deceives. He spoke to her of Christ as the Bridegroom who became man out of love. He urged her to keep herself for him alone. Saint Clare did not delay. She gave her consent, and from then on she put herself fully under Saint Francis’s guidance. After God, he was the one who directed her steps.19

Saint Clare Leaves Home at Night

Palm Sunday was coming. It is the Sunday before Easter, when the Church blesses palm branches and hands them out to the people. Saint Clare went to Saint Francis and asked him what she should do, and how. He told her to go to the blessing of the palms with everyone else, dressed in her finest clothes. On the night that followed, she was to go out from the world. She would turn the joy of the feast into sorrow for the sufferings of Christ.20

When Sunday came, Saint Clare went into the church with the crowd of women, bright in her festive clothes. As the others hurried forward to take their branches, Saint Clare was too modest to push ahead. She stayed in her place. Then the bishop came down the steps, went over to her, and put a palm into her hands. The early Life saw in this a sign of what was to come.21

That night she set out with a few companions she could trust. She did not want to leave by the usual door, because she was afraid someone would stop her. So she went to another door of the house. It had been barred shut with heavy logs and a stone column, and many men together could hardly have moved them. A woman who lived in the house at the time told how Saint Clare moved them alone, with the help of Jesus Christ, and opened the door. The early Life adds that her own strength amazed her. It was about the year 1211, and she was about eighteen years old.22

Leaving her home, her city, and her family behind, she hurried to Saint Mary of the Porziuncola. This was the place where Saint Francis and his company of poor men had begun their life together. That night the brothers were keeping watch in prayer at the little altar. They welcomed her with burning torches.23

There Saint Clare laid aside her ornaments. In the words of the early Life, she “gave a bill of divorce to the world.” In the Bible, a bill of divorce is the paper that ended a marriage. She was parting from the world for good. Her sister Beatrice later said that Saint Francis cut off her hair before the altar. Then Saint Clare received the plain robe of penance, worn by those who turn from sin to God. Saint Francis led her straight from there to the church of Saint Paul, a house of nuns, where she was to stay until God showed her another place.24

Back in Assisi, morning came to her father’s house. Many people saw the barred door standing open. They marveled that a young girl had been able to open it. Before long, her family would come looking for her.25
