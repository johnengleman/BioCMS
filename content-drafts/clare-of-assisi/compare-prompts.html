Saint Clare: two prompts, same facts

Opening and first three chapters. Notes are hidden here so the prose is easy to compare. A: the current long prompt (after copy edit and fact check). B: the new slim prompt, first draft (not yet copy-edited or fact-checked).
A — current prompt

Pope Gregory IX came in person to the convent of San Damiano at Assisi, and he came with an offer. Saint Clare was the abbess there, and he loved her like a father. The women in her care owned nothing at all and lived on bread and other scraps that the friars begged for them. The Pope thought the times were too dangerous for such a life. He wanted to give them property, and he was ready to buy it for them himself.

Saint Clare refused, and one of her sisters stood by and heard the Pope press her to accept. He believed it was her vow of poverty that held her back, so he offered to free her from it. “If you fear your vow,” he said, “we release you from it.”

“Holy Father,” Saint Clare answered, “in no way do I ever want to be released from following Christ.”

She was a knight’s daughter. Seven knights belonged to her family’s house in Assisi, and her parents had meant to marry her to “great and powerful men.” She had walked out of that house one night as a girl of about eighteen, and she had sold her inheritance and given the money to the poor. Then she had followed Saint Francis of Assisi into a life with nothing in it but Christ. Now a pope was offering her a little security, and she would not take even that.

Saint Clare held to that poverty for more than forty years. She led her sisters in one small house, and she never left it again. In her lifetime her way of life spread to “cities without number.” She asked one pope for the right to own nothing, and she defended it against the next. In letters to a king’s daughter in Prague she taught the same poverty. She was ill for nearly thirty years, and in her last year she was still asking the Church to protect her poverty. Two years after her death, the Pope declared her a saint.

Her mother had been promised a light before Saint Clare was even born.

Saint Clare’s Mother Hears a Promise

Saint Clare’s mother was Blessed Hortulana, and she was a pilgrim. She had crossed the sea to pray at the holy places, and she had gone to the shrine of Saint Michael on Monte Gargano and to Rome. Her neighbor Pacifica traveled with her. Only the town square lay between their two houses, and Pacifica often talked with Saint Clare while she still lived at home.

When Blessed Hortulana was expecting a child and her time was near, she went to a church to pray. She prayed before the cross and begged Christ to bring her safely through the danger of giving birth. Then she heard a voice.

“Do not be afraid, woman. You will give birth safely to a light that will shine on the world more clearly.”

— The voice heard by Saint Clare’s mother, in the Legend of Saint Clare

Blessed Hortulana took the words to heart. When the baby was baptized, she had her named Clara. She hoped the promised light would shine in her daughter. The name means clear and bright, and in English it is Clare. Years later Saint Clare told this story to her sisters herself. Blessed Hortulana told it too, and a sister named Cecilia heard it from her.

Saint Clare was born in Assisi in about 1193, and her father was a knight named Favarone. On both her father’s side and her mother’s, she came from one of the noblest families in the city. Her father’s household was among the largest in Assisi, and it spent money freely.

A man-at-arms named Juan Ventura lived in that house when she was a girl, and he saw what she did with her own food. She set it aside and hid it, and then she sent it to the poor. A woman named Bona lived with her, and Bona carried the food out for her many times. Saint Clare had no prayer beads, so she counted her prayers with a heap of pebbles. Under her soft and costly clothes she wore a little hair shirt. A hair shirt is a rough shirt worn next to the skin as a penance. Juan Ventura remembered a rough white cloth that she wore under her dress.

She stayed indoors and kept out of sight of the people who passed the house. When she sat with her family, she always wanted to talk about God. She was also beautiful, and so her family began to look for a husband for her.

Saint Clare Refuses to Marry

Her father, her mother, and her relatives all wanted a match worthy of her rank. A neighbor later said that they meant to marry her “splendidly, to great and powerful men.” She was about seventeen. Many relatives begged her to agree, but she never did.

One of them was Ranieri di Bernardo. His wife was her relative, so he came and went freely in the house. He begged her many times to accept a husband, and more than forty years later he told the story under oath. She would not even listen to him. Instead she preached to him “contempt of the world.” The neighbor gave the reason: nobody could persuade her, because she wanted to stay a virgin and “live in poverty.”

By then another man of Assisi had chosen poverty, and his name was becoming famous. He was Saint Francis. When Saint Clare heard what he had done, she decided in her heart to do the same. Saint Francis had heard of her as well, and he wanted to meet her. They met in secret, and they chose their times so that no one would know and no gossip would spread.

Bona often walked with Saint Clare to see him. Bona said that they went secretly so that Saint Clare’s relatives would not see them. Saint Francis always urged Saint Clare to turn to Jesus Christ, and a friar named Brother Philip urged her too. She listened to them gladly. The Legend of Saint Clare was written soon after she was declared a saint, and it describes those visits.

“With only one trusted companion, the girl left her father’s house in secret. She often visited the man of God. His words seemed to her like fire, and his deeds seemed more than human.”

— Legend of Saint Clare

Saint Francis spoke to her about Christ as a bridegroom and urged her to keep herself for Christ alone. She agreed without delay, and from then on she put herself wholly under the guidance of Saint Francis. As Palm Sunday drew near, she went to him and asked what she should do and how to do it.

Saint Clare Leaves Home at Night

Saint Francis gave her a plan for Palm Sunday, the Sunday before Easter. On that feast she was to dress up in her finery and go with everyone else to the blessing of the palms. On the night after the feast, she was to leave.

On Palm Sunday she went to church dressed for the feast, in the crowd of women. When the others pressed forward to receive their palm branches, Saint Clare stayed where she was. The Legend says that she held back out of modesty. Then the bishop came down the steps, went to her, and put a palm into her hands.

That night she set out with some trusted companions. She did not use the usual door, because she was afraid someone would stop her. Instead she went to another door of the house, which was barred shut with heavy logs and a stone column. Sister Cristiana lived in that house at the time, and she later said that many men could hardly have moved them. Saint Clare moved them alone, “with the help of Jesus Christ,” and opened the door. The Legend adds that her own strength amazed her. The next morning people saw the open door, and many of them marveled that a young girl had opened it.

She hurried to the small church of Saint Mary of the Porziuncola. Saint Francis’s friars were keeping watch there in prayer at the little altar, and they met her with lighted torches. There she put aside her fine clothes and her ornaments, and her hair was cut off. Her sister Beatrice later said that Saint Francis cut it himself before the altar. Saint Clare received the robe of penance and gave herself to Christ. She was about eighteen years old, and it was about the year 1211.

Saint Francis did not keep her at the Porziuncola. He took her at once to the church of San Paolo, a house of Benedictine nuns, to stay there until God gave her another place. Her family learned where she had gone.

B — slim prompt

Pope Gregory IX loved Saint Clare as a father loves his daughter. He told her that the times were hard and dangerous. He urged her community to accept some property, and he offered to give it himself. Saint Clare would not agree in any way. She was bound by a vow of poverty, a solemn promise made to God. The Pope had an answer for that. “If you fear your vow,” he said, “we release you from it.” Saint Clare answered, “Holy Father, in no way do I ever want to be released from following Christ.”

Saint Clare had already turned down a great deal. She came from one of the noblest families of Assisi, and seven knights belonged to her family’s house. Her father and mother had hoped to marry her in splendid style to “great and powerful men.” She would not hear of it. One night she left her father’s house in secret and went to Saint Francis, who had chosen to be poor.

For forty-two years she lived at San Damiano, a small church at Assisi. Women came there to share her life. Their way of life spread, and monasteries rose in “cities without number.” Pope Innocent III granted her community the right to own nothing at all. He told her that no one had ever asked a pope for such a privilege before. He was so glad of her request that he wrote the first lines of the grant in his own hand.

Saint Clare guarded that poverty for the rest of her life. She wrote about it to Saint Agnes of Prague, a king’s daughter who had turned down an emperor to choose the same life. Late in her life she begged above all that the Pope confirm her privilege of poverty. Two years after her death, Pope Alexander IV declared her a saint.

Saint Clare’s Mother Hears a Promise

Saint Clare was born about 1193 in Assisi, a city in the Italian region of Umbria. Her father was a knight named Favarone. Both sides of her family were of knightly blood, and they were rich in land and goods. Her mother was Blessed Hortulana. Though she had a husband and a household to care for, she gave as much of her time to God as she could. She had crossed the sea with other pilgrims to pray in the places where Christ had walked, and she came home full of joy. She also went to pray at the shrine of Saint Michael on Monte Gargano, and she visited the shrines of the Apostles in Rome.

When Blessed Hortulana was expecting her child and the birth was near, she went into a church to pray. Standing before the cross, she begged the crucified Lord to bring her safely through the dangers of childbirth. Then she heard a voice speak to her:

“Do not be afraid, woman. You will give birth safely to a light that will shine on the world more clearly.”

Because of these words, Blessed Hortulana had her daughter named Clara at baptism. The name means bright or clear. Her mother hoped that the promised light would somehow shine out in her daughter’s life, as God willed. Years later, Saint Clare told this story to the sisters of her convent. One of those sisters had also heard it from Blessed Hortulana herself.

Saint Clare learned the first truths of the faith from her mother. As a girl she loved the poor. Her father’s house was one of the greatest in the city, and it spent freely. When good food was set before her, she would say she was eating it. Then she hid it away and sent it to the poor in secret. Her companion Bona carried it to them many times. Before Saint Clare left home, she sent Bona on a pilgrimage to the shrine of Saint James at Compostela. Bona said that Saint Clare was full of grace and wanted others to share it. Once she also gave Bona money for the men working at Saint Mary of the Porziuncola, a church outside the city, so that they could buy meat. Saint Clare had no beads for counting her prayers to God, so she counted them on a little pile of stones.

Under her soft and costly clothes she wore a hair shirt, a rough garment worn against the skin as a penance. A soldier who served in her father’s house remembered it as a rough white cloth. He also saw her fast and pray. She stayed at home and kept out of sight of the people who passed the house. When she sat with the household, she always wanted to talk about the things of God. Without seeking it, she began to win the praise of her neighbors.

Saint Clare Refuses to Marry

Saint Clare was beautiful, and her family began to look for a husband for her. Her father was noble and powerful in the city, and so were the other men of his house. The family wanted a splendid match that would suit their rank. At the time she was about seventeen. Many of her relatives begged her to agree, but she never did.

One man came and went freely in the house, because his wife was a relative of hers. He pleaded with Saint Clare many times to accept a husband. She would not even listen to him. Instead she preached to him “contempt of the world.” That old phrase means that the world and all it offers are worth nothing next to God. More than forty years later he told the story under oath.

An early Life of Saint Clare was written a few years after her death. It tells how she held her family off. She let them think that she only wanted to wait a while before marrying. Meanwhile she gave her virginity to God. A neighbor of the family explained why no one could persuade her. She wanted to stay a virgin and to live in poverty.

By then the name of Saint Francis was famous in Assisi. Saint Clare heard that he had chosen the way of poverty, and she decided in her heart to do the same. She longed to see him and to hear him speak. Saint Francis had heard of her goodness too. He wanted to speak with her and win her for God.

They met many times and took care that no one should find out. Bona often went along with her. The two went in secret so that her relatives would not see them. Saint Francis always urged her to turn to Jesus Christ. So did Brother Philip, one of his followers. Saint Clare listened to them gladly and agreed with all the good things they told her. The early Life describes these visits:

“With only one trusted companion, the girl left her father’s house in secret. She often visited the man of God. His words seemed to her like fire, and his deeds seemed more than human.”

Saint Francis showed her how empty the hopes of this world are and how its beauty deceives. He spoke to her of Christ as the Bridegroom who became man out of love. He urged her to keep herself for him alone. Saint Clare did not delay. She gave her consent, and from then on she put herself fully under Saint Francis’s guidance. After God, he was the one who directed her steps.

Saint Clare Leaves Home at Night

Palm Sunday was coming. It is the Sunday before Easter, when the Church blesses palm branches and hands them out to the people. Saint Clare went to Saint Francis and asked him what she should do, and how. He told her to go to the blessing of the palms with everyone else, dressed in her finest clothes. On the night that followed, she was to go out from the world. She would turn the joy of the feast into sorrow for the sufferings of Christ.

When Sunday came, Saint Clare went into the church with the crowd of women, bright in her festive clothes. As the others hurried forward to take their branches, Saint Clare was too modest to push ahead. She stayed in her place. Then the bishop came down the steps, went over to her, and put a palm into her hands. The early Life saw in this a sign of what was to come.

That night she set out with a few companions she could trust. She did not want to leave by the usual door, because she was afraid someone would stop her. So she went to another door of the house. It had been barred shut with heavy logs and a stone column, and many men together could hardly have moved them. A woman who lived in the house at the time told how Saint Clare moved them alone, with the help of Jesus Christ, and opened the door. The early Life adds that her own strength amazed her. It was about the year 1211, and she was about eighteen years old.

Leaving her home, her city, and her family behind, she hurried to Saint Mary of the Porziuncola. This was the place where Saint Francis and his company of poor men had begun their life together. That night the brothers were keeping watch in prayer at the little altar. They welcomed her with burning torches.

There Saint Clare laid aside her ornaments. In the words of the early Life, she “gave a bill of divorce to the world.” In the Bible, a bill of divorce is the paper that ended a marriage. She was parting from the world for good. Her sister Beatrice later said that Saint Francis cut off her hair before the altar. Then Saint Clare received the plain robe of penance, worn by those who turn from sin to God. Saint Francis led her straight from there to the church of Saint Paul, a house of nuns, where she was to stay until God showed her another place.

Back in Assisi, morning came to her father’s house. Many people saw the barred door standing open. They marveled that a young girl had been able to open it. Before long, her family would come looking for her.
