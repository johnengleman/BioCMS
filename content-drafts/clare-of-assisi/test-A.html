Saint Clare Becomes Abbess Against Her Will

Saint Clare spent three years at San Damiano refusing to be called abbess, the sister who heads a house of nuns. She wanted to be under others, not over them. Saint Francis kept pressing her. Pacifica was in the room. She said afterwards that he "almost forced her" before Saint Clare agreed to govern the sisters. The office did not make her proud. Fear rose in her heart, not pride, and the higher others placed her, the lower she stood in her own eyes.1

She led by serving. She poured water over the sisters' hands and waited on them at table, and when a sister was sick she washed the seat she had used. Orders came hard to her. She gave them unwillingly, and most of the time she preferred to do the task herself. On cold nights, while the others slept, she went around and covered them.2

The serving sisters were those who went outside the convent on its errands. When they came home, Saint Clare washed their feet and kissed them. One Thursday in Lent she knelt before one of them and bent to kiss her foot. The sister could not bear it. She pulled her foot back, and it struck Saint Clare in the mouth. Saint Clare took the foot gently in her hands again and kissed the sole. Agnes of Opportulo saw it happen. She said that Saint Clare rejoiced at the blow.3

The same sister learned at first hand how hard Saint Clare was on herself. Under her robe Saint Clare sometimes wore a shirt of knotted horsehair, tied to her body with rough cords. Agnes asked to borrow it. She kept it three days and gave it back; it was so harsh, she said, that she could not bear it at all.4

Her fasting worried the sisters more. Through the two long fasting seasons of the year she lived on bread and water, and on Mondays, Wednesdays, and Fridays she ate nothing at all. The sisters grieved over it, and Pacifica admitted that she had wept more than once. At last Saint Francis and the bishop of Assisi ordered her to eat something every day: half a small loaf, about an ounce and a half of bread. Benvenuta of Perugia was there when Saint Francis gave the order, and she watched Saint Clare take a little bread and water out of obedience. Her face stayed glad through all of it. She seemed not to feel the hardship, or to laugh at it.5

In time she gave others the counsel she had needed herself. Years later she wrote to Saint Agnes of Prague, a king's daughter who had taken up the same life. She begged the princess to draw back from "the indiscreet and impossible strictness in fasting" she had begun, for "our flesh is not bronze, and our strength is not the strength of stone."6

Her mother followed her. Blessed Hortulana entered the Order her daughter had founded. She lived at San Damiano with the other sisters "in much humility," Pacifica said, and she died there. Her family had tried to pull Saint Clare out of this life and failed; the next to test it, with a father's love, would be a Pope.7

Saint Clare Refuses the Pope's Offer

Saint Clare had asked Rome for something unusual. She wanted a written promise that her sisters could never be made to own anything: a "privilege of poverty." Pope Innocent III said the request was unlike any other, and he wrote the first lines of the grant in his own hand.8

Later, Pope Gregory IX came in person to San Damiano. He loved her with a father's affection. The times were dangerous, he told her, and he urged her to accept some possessions, which he offered freely. She would not bend.9

He had an answer ready for the doubt he thought was holding her back. "If you fear your vow," he said, "we release you from it."10

"Holy Father," Saint Clare answered, "in no way do I ever want to be released from following Christ."11

Benvenuta of Perugia was present and heard the exchange. She said that neither Pope Gregory nor the bishop of Ostia could ever get Saint Clare to accept any property. Pacifica remembered that the Pope "wanted to give her many things." Felipa said that Saint Clare honored the privilege and kept it for fear of losing it. On 17 September 1228 Pope Gregory himself put the privilege of poverty in writing for San Damiano.12

That did not end the testing. The sisters depended on the friars, Saint Francis's brothers, for two kinds of food. Some begged for the house; others came to preach. Then Pope Gregory forbade any friar to go to the sisters' monasteries without his permission. Saint Clare grieved that her daughters would now hear sound teaching less often. She said with a sigh: "Let him take all the friars from us, now that he has taken away the ones who gave us the food of the soul." She at once sent every friar back to their superior, the begging brothers too. If she could not have the bread of the spirit, she would not keep the bread of the body. When the Pope heard of it, he softened his order at once and left the matter to the head of the friars. The next trial came not from Rome but over the convent wall.13

Soldiers Break Into San Damiano

In those years the Emperor Frederick II made war on the Church, and the valley of Spoleto "very often drank of the chalice of wrath." Bands of soldiers and Saracen archers, "numerous as bees," were posted by imperial command to lay waste castles and besiege cities. These Saracens were descendants of the Saracens who had once ruled Sicily and Calabria, and the Emperor had given them towns of their own.14

At San Damiano the sisters were afraid of what was coming: Tartars, Saracens, and other enemies of God. Saint Clare set about putting heart into them. She told them that if God was with them the enemies could not hurt them. They should trust in the Lord Jesus Christ, who would free them. She would answer for it herself that no harm came to them. "If they come," she said, "put me in front of them."15

One day the enemy struck at Assisi without warning, and the army was soon close to the gates. Some of the Saracens climbed the monastery wall and dropped down into the cloister, the enclosed part of the house. The sisters' hearts failed them. Their voices shook, and they went in tears to their mother. Saint Clare was gravely ill, but she rose from her bed and had the sisters called together. Then she had herself led to the door of the refectory, the room where the sisters ate, and set down facing the enemy. She asked for the small box in which the Body of Christ was kept, a silver case set in ivory, to be carried before her.16

There she lay face down on the ground before the Lord and prayed through her tears:

Does it please you, my Lord, to hand over to the pagans your defenseless servants, whom I have fed with your love? Lord, I beg you, protect these servants of yours. I cannot protect them now.17

Then a voice came to her, soft as a small child's: "I will always protect you." Then she prayed for the city too, which supported the sisters for love of God. The Lord answered: "It will suffer troubles, but my protection will defend it."18

She lifted her wet face and turned to the weeping sisters: "Do not be afraid. I promise you that you will suffer no harm, now or in the future, as long as you obey God's commandments." The boldness of the men in the cloister turned to fear. They climbed back over the walls they had scaled and left without touching anyone in the house.19

Assisi's danger returned. Another time the imperial army came against the city under its captain Vitalis of Aversa, a man hungry for fame and a brave fighter. He had stripped the land of trees and ruined the countryside, and he swore he would not withdraw until he had taken Assisi. The city was believed to be in immediate danger, and someone carried the news to Saint Clare.20

She sighed deeply and called her sisters together. "We have received many good things from this city," she told them, "and so we must pray to God to protect it." She told them to come to her at dawn. Very early the next morning they gathered around her, and she had ashes brought, the Church's old sign of sorrow and penance. Her head had lately been shaved. She uncovered it and told the others to uncover theirs. She heaped ashes on her own head first and then on the head of every sister in turn. Then she sent them to the chapel to beg the Lord with all their hearts for the city. That day the sisters fasted on bread and water, and some ate nothing. The next day the army broke up and fled in disorder, and its proud captain went away in spite of his oath. He did not trouble that land again, and not long afterwards he died by the sword.21

Of the voice that had answered her at the refectory door, almost no one knew. Two sisters had been holding Saint Clare up as she prayed: Francisca, and Illuminata of Pisa. Both heard it, and Francisca called it a voice "of wonderful sweetness." That same evening Saint Clare called the two of them to her and ordered them to tell no one as long as she lived. Francisca obeyed. She spoke of it only after Saint Clare's death, under oath. By then she could still say the month and the hour. It was September, she believed, on a Friday about the hour of Terce, the Church's mid-morning prayer. The year she could not say.22
