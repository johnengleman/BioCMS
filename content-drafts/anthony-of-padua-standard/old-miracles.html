This list gathers the healings, visions and other wonders attributed to St. Anthony of Padua, with their sources and their status. When Pope Gregory IX canonized him at Spoleto on Pentecost, 30 May 1232, the miracles examined at Padua were read aloud before the declaration; the first group below is what the early Latin Life records of them, the nearest thing to a list approved by the Church. The other accounts come from the early Latin Life, which a 1904 editor dated before 1249 and probably before 1240; from Jean Rigauld’s Life, written about 1293–1300 by a friar who questioned brothers who had known Anthony; from the collection of his miracles written down about 1367; and from later chronicles. Each entry says which. Stories that first appear in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries are gathered in their own group and marked as late. The list makes no claim of scientific proof, and it does not dismiss any account.

Miracles approved by the Church: the canonization, 1232

Healings read before the Pope at Spoleto, 30 May 1232

Within a month of Anthony’s death, Padua asked the Pope to canonize him, and Gregory IX had the reported miracles examined: by a commission at Padua (the bishop, the prior of St. Benedict’s and the Dominican prior Giovanni da Vicenza) and by the Bishop of Sabina. According to the early Latin Life, the miracles read out at Spoleto included the healing of nineteen lame people, of people with paralysis, of six blind, three deaf and three mute people, of two with epilepsy and two with fevers, and the raising of two people from the dead. An 1883 chronicle counts forty-five miracles judicially proved before the canonization, two of them raisings of the dead.

Status: approved: read out before Pope Gregory IX at the canonization, according to the early Latin Life. The individual cases (the list of forty-five) were not read for this page.

Source: Latin Life (attributed to Julian of Speyer), Acta Sanctorum, June, 13 June (1867 reprint), p. 204, https://archive.org/details/actasanctorum23unse

Source: H. J. Coleridge, ed., The Chronicle of St. Antony of Padua (1883), pp. 192–194, 207, https://archive.org/details/MN5128ucmf_2

Three more wonders from the canonization process

With the healings, the early Latin Life records three other wonders from the process: a woman who fell into the water and was not wet, a woman guarded by sparrows, and shipwrecked men who were saved. The names and places of these people were not in the passage read for this page.

Status: approved: the early Latin Life lists these three among the miracles approved at the canonization, apart from the further accounts it calls no less true but not so solemnly approved.

Source: Latin Life (attributed to Julian of Speyer), Acta Sanctorum, June, 13 June (1867 reprint), p. 204, https://archive.org/details/actasanctorum23unse

During Anthony’s life

A heretic of Rimini, thirty years in error

At Rimini, Anthony preached against the heretics the early Lives call Patarines. Jean Rigauld writes that he brought back to the Church “a noted heresiarch, named Bononillo, who had been for thirty years blinded by the pestilential mists of error.” The early Latin Life also tells of the conversion.

Status: reported in the early Lives. It is a conversion, not a physical wonder.

Source: Latin Life (attributed to Julian of Speyer), Acta Sanctorum, June, 13 June (1867 reprint), p. 200, https://archive.org/details/actasanctorum23unse

Source: Jean Rigauld, The Life of St. Antony of Padua (about 1293–1300), tr. 1904, p. 65, https://archive.org/details/LifeOfStAntonyOfPadua

The poisoned dish, northern Italy

Heretics in Italy served Anthony a dish of poisoned food. He made the sign of the cross over it and told them, “I do this not to tempt God, but to show my constant and earnest zeal for your salvation, and the triumph of the Faith.” He ate it and took no harm, and, Rigauld says, the men returned to the faith.

Status: reported in Rigauld’s Life, written 60–70 years after Anthony’s death; also in the Catholic Encyclopedia.

Source: Jean Rigauld, The Life of St. Antony of Padua (about 1293–1300), tr. 1904, pp. 55–56, https://archive.org/details/LifeOfStAntonyOfPadua

Source: N. Dal-Gal, “St. Anthony of Padua,” Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Catholic_Encyclopedia_(1913)/St._Anthony_of_Padua

The sermon to the fishes

When heretics openly mocked his preaching, Anthony went to the river and said, “Since you show yourselves unworthy to hear the Word of God, behold, I turn to the fishes, that your unbelief may be put to shame.” The fish gathered in crowds, lifted their heads out of the water and opened their mouths. Rigauld sets the story near Padua. An 1883 chronicle sets it at Rimini, at the mouth of the Marecchia, with the fish lined up by size, the smallest nearest the shore, and Anthony beginning, “My brothers, the fishes”; the Catholic Encyclopedia places it on the Brenta and says it was not at Padua. Baring-Gould thought the fish were “no doubt an addition” by people who missed the satire of a preacher turning from deaf men to fishes.

Status: legend. First written down 60–70 years after his death; the place is disputed.

The kneeling horse, or mule, and the Host

A heretic who denied that Christ was present in the Sacrament agreed to a test. He kept his animal without food for three days and then set oats before it, while Anthony held the Host, and the animal passed the oats by and knelt to the Host. Rigauld, who took the story from a small treatise on Anthony’s miracles, names no town and calls the animal a horse. The 1883 chronicle makes it a mule belonging to Bonvillo of Rimini; Baring-Gould sets it at Toulouse, where the mule breaks its halter as Anthony carries the Host to a dying man, and the Catholic Encyclopedia notes still other places, among them Bruges, in fourteenth-century legends. The kneeling mule became one of Anthony’s emblems in art.

Status: legend; the place and the animal differ between versions.

Francis at the door, chapter of Arles

While Anthony preached to a chapter of the friars at Arles, in Provence, on the title of the cross, a friar named Monaldo saw Francis, who was alive but far away in Italy, at the door. He “appeared at the door of the Chapter-room, raised in the air, with his arms stretched out in the form of a cross blessing the Friars.” Thomas of Celano mentions the event in his first Life of Francis (1228), Bartholomew of Trent tells it about 1240, and the early Latin Life of Anthony has it. The Bollandists guessed the year 1226.

Status: reported in the early Lives, with a named eyewitness in Rigauld.

In two places at once, Limoges, Holy Thursday

On the night of Holy Thursday, Rigauld writes, Anthony was preaching in the church of St. Pierre-du-Queyroix at Limoges when the hour came for him, as guardian, to read a lesson at Matins in the friars’ choir; he was there in the choir and read it, while he was also preaching in the town. Baring-Gould sets the story at Montpellier. The Catholic Encyclopedia counts it among the best-attested miracles of his years in France.

Status: reported in Rigauld’s Life, written 60–70 years later; the place is disputed.

The platform that fell at St. Junien

At St. Junien, in the Limousin, the platform Anthony was preaching from collapsed, as he had told the crowd it would, and no one was hurt.

Status: reported in Rigauld’s Life; also in the Catholic Encyclopedia.

The archbishop of Bourges in tears

At a synod of the clergy at Bourges, Anthony turned to the archbishop and said, “I speak to thee, mitre-bearer!” He rebuked him so sharply that the archbishop was moved to tears. Rigauld introduces the story as told to him by “Certain Friars, worthy of belief.”

Status: reported in Rigauld’s Life. It is a conversion of heart, not a physical wonder.

Source: Jean Rigauld, The Life of St. Antony of Padua (about 1293–1300), tr. 1904, pp. 67–68, https://archive.org/details/LifeOfStAntonyOfPadua

A novice freed from temptation, Limoges

A novice named Peter, at Limoges, was freed from temptation when Anthony breathed on him, Rigauld writes. The Catholic Encyclopedia gives two related stories: Anthony’s tunic freeing a monk from temptation, and his breath on a novice’s face confirming the young man’s vocation.

Status: reported in Rigauld’s Life and the Catholic Encyclopedia.

Cabbages carried through the rain, Brive

At Brive, where Anthony founded a house of the friars, a maid-servant carried cabbages to them through the rain and was not wet. Rigauld heard the story from Pierre de Brive, a canon of Noblac, who had it from his mother.

Status: reported in Rigauld’s Life, with a named witness at second hand; also in the Catholic Encyclopedia.

Source: Jean Rigauld, The Life of St. Antony of Padua (about 1293–1300), tr. 1904, pp. 53–54, https://archive.org/details/LifeOfStAntonyOfPadua

Rain held back from the crowd, the Creux des Arènes, Limoges

A crowd too large for any church had gathered to hear Anthony in an open space at Limoges called the Creux des Arènes. When the thunder began and the rain started, the people began to leave, and Anthony told them, “Fear not; do not move nor cease listening to the Divine Word, for I trust in Him who never suffers us to trust in vain that the rain will not touch you.” The rain fell all around them and not on them. Rigauld says friars who had been there were still alive when he joined the Order. Butler tells of a field in France and thunder held off, and the Catholic Encyclopedia of a square at Limoges.

Status: reported in Rigauld’s Life, from eyewitness friars.

A broken glass and spilt wine, Provence

At the house of a poor woman in a village in Provence, where Anthony was a guest, a glass was broken and the wine was spilt from the cask. Rigauld writes that the glass was made whole and the wine restored; the Catholic Encyclopedia says the cask filled again.

Status: reported in Rigauld’s Life; also in the Catholic Encyclopedia.

Source: Jean Rigauld, The Life of St. Antony of Padua (about 1293–1300), tr. 1904, pp. 49–50, https://archive.org/details/LifeOfStAntonyOfPadua

The stolen book of notes, Montpellier

While Anthony lectured to the friars on the Psalms, a novice stole the book he used in teaching. The collection of his miracles written about 1367 calls it a glossed psalter (psalterium glossatum); the 1883 chronicle and a 1911 biography call it his manuscript of notes on the Psalms. Anthony prayed for its return. On a bridge, the story goes, the fleeing novice was stopped by a terrible figure who ordered him to take the book back: a devil with an axe in the 1367 collection, “a man of savage countenance” with a drawn sword in the 1883 chronicle. The novice brought the book back. The chronicle calls it the only time Anthony asked such a grace for himself. The story is the root of his patronage of lost things.

Status: legend. First written down in the collection of his miracles about 1367, more than a century after his death, though the devotion may be older.

The Child Jesus with Anthony

A nobleman who was Anthony’s host saw the Child Jesus with him. In the Italian tradition this was Count Tiso at Camposampiero, in the last weeks of Anthony’s life; the 1883 chronicle sets Tiso’s vision in his own house at Padua, where Anthony lodged and Tiso saw the Child through the keyhole; Baring-Gould has the host watching through a chink in the wall of a house in the Limousin. The Catholic Encyclopedia names Camposampiero and Châteauneuf-la-Forêt and says the existing documents do not decide between them. Pope Benedict XVI described it as an apparition mentioned in several literary sources.

Status: tradition; the place is disputed. It was not found in the early sources read for this page.

The devil at his throat, Padua, Lent 1231

One night early in the Lent of 1231, the devil seized Anthony by the throat and would have strangled him. He called on the Virgin, a light filled the room, and the devil fled. The early Latin Life adds that Anthony told this himself, later, among friends.

Status: reported in the early Latin Life, as told by Anthony; Rigauld has it too.

The sins on the paper, Padua

A young man who wept so hard that he could not speak his sins in confession wrote them down instead. As Anthony read the paper, each sin on it was wiped out.

Status: reported in the 1883 chronicle; later tradition.

Source: H. J. Coleridge, ed., The Chronicle of St. Antony of Padua (1883), p. 100, https://archive.org/details/MN5128ucmf_2

Leonardo’s foot, Padua

A man of Padua named Leonardo confessed that he had kicked his mother, and Anthony told him, “The foot of him who kicks his mother deserves to be cut off.” Leonardo took the words literally and cut off his foot with a hatchet. His mother ran to Anthony and upbraided him, and Anthony joined the foot back to the leg “without wound or scar.”

Status: legend from the later Lives. The Catholic Encyclopedia says Franciscan writers attribute it to him.

Source: H. J. Coleridge, ed., The Chronicle of St. Antony of Padua (1883), pp. 100–101, https://archive.org/details/MN5128ucmf_2

The miser’s heart

Anthony was asked to preach at the funeral of a rich man, in Tuscany in the Basilica’s telling. He said, “That rich man is dead and buried in hell; but go to his treasures and there you will find his heart.”

Status: legend.

Other stories on the Basilica’s list

The Basilica of St. Anthony in Padua tells, among his miracles, of an infant who spoke, in a story of a nobleman’s jealousy; of a violent husband in Tuscany; of a soldier of Padua who stabbed his wife, and of her healing; of a glass belonging to a knight named Aleardino; and of a dead man who spoke, at Lisbon. Only the titles and openings of these accounts were read for this page.

Status: later Lives and popular tradition; not checked against the earliest Life.

Ezzelino da Romano at Verona

Baring-Gould and Butler tell how the tyrant Ezzelino da Romano humbled himself before Anthony at Verona, and the Bollandists print the story in the later collection of Anthony’s miracles. The Catholic Encyclopedia calls it “an apocryphal legend”: Anthony risked his life with the tyrant, it says, but may have failed.

Status: contested.

Late stories, sixteenth and seventeenth centuries

Stories added by Surius and Wadding

Writers of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, chiefly Laurentius Surius and Luke Wadding, added stories that are not in the early Lives: a boy at Lisbon raised from the dead to clear Anthony’s father of a charge; Anthony appearing in a court to clear his father of a debt; a capon turned to fish bones, and an owl served up as a capon; Anthony preaching in Italian with perfect ease though he was born far away; and Anthony appearing at night to sleepers to tell them to confess sins only God knew. The Bollandists print them in a supplement to the early Life.

Status: late; not found in the early Lives.

Source: Latin Life (attributed to Julian of Speyer), Acta Sanctorum, June, 13 June (1867 reprint), pp. 200–203 (supplement), https://archive.org/details/actasanctorum23unse

After Anthony’s death

The children of Padua, 13 June 1231

The friars meant to keep Anthony’s death quiet, but children ran through the streets of Padua crying, “The holy father is dead; St Antony is dead!”

Status: reported in the early Lives.

An appearance at Vercelli, 13 June 1231

On the day of his death, Anthony appeared at Vercelli to Abbot Thomas Gallo. In the 1883 chronicle’s telling, he said, “I have left my little ass near Padua and am going in haste to my own country,” and cured the abbot’s sore throat.

The house that could not be broken into, Arcella

While the people of Capo di Ponte and the friars quarrelled over Anthony’s body, people tried at night to break into the house at Arcella where it lay. The early Lives say they were “stupefied and struck with blindness” and could not get in.

Status: reported in the early Lives.

The first cures at the tomb, and Tuesdays

The cures began on the day Anthony was buried at the church of St. Mary in Padua, and the people of Capo di Ponte, who had fought to keep his body, were the first to come barefoot to the tomb. The 1883 chronicle adds that his body entered Padua on a Tuesday and that no sufferer who asked his help on a Tuesday failed to be cured. It gives this as the origin of the devotion of Tuesdays, and of the nine Tuesdays, to St. Anthony.

Status: the first cures are reported in the early Latin Life; the Tuesday tradition comes from the later chronicles.

Source: Latin Life (attributed to Julian of Speyer), Acta Sanctorum, June, 13 June (1867 reprint), p. 203, https://archive.org/details/actasanctorum23unse

A dream before the canonization

Some at the papal court objected that a year had not passed since Anthony’s death. The early Latin Life says that one of those who objected dreamed he saw the Pope consecrating a church and asking for relics for the altar, and a covered body whose relics were so precious that all present strove to take them; on waking, he found the envoys from Padua at his door. Rigauld calls the dreamer a cardinal. The 1883 chronicle adds that the body was incorrupt and fragrant, and that the cardinal, on meeting the envoys, said, “This is our dream and its interpretation.”

Status: legendary; the sources agree that a doubting cardinal had the dream, and differ only in detail.

The bells of Lisbon, 30 May 1232

At the moment the Pope pronounced the sentence of canonization at Spoleto, the 1883 chronicle says, the church bells of Lisbon began to ring by themselves.

An appearance at Coimbra

After the canonization, Rigauld heard from the custodian of St. Jacques, Anthony appeared to a canon of Holy Cross at Coimbra, the house where he had lived as a young canon, and told him of the death of the Queen of Portugal almost at the hour she died. Rigauld adds that the bed Anthony had slept in there had become an object of the greatest veneration.

Status: reported in Rigauld’s Life, at second hand.

A voice from the tomb and the end of Ezzelino’s rule

Ezzelino da Romano ruled Padua from 1237. In the 1883 chronicle, a friar named Bartolomeo Corradini heard a voice from Anthony’s tomb promising that the city would be freed “on the octave of my feast.” On 20 June, a Tuesday, the army of the Pope and Venice defeated Ezzelino’s nephew Ansidisio, in the late 1250s.

Status: reported in the later chronicles.

Source: H. J. Coleridge, ed., The Chronicle of St. Antony of Padua (1883), p. 197, https://archive.org/details/MN5128ucmf_2

The tongue found incorrupt, 1263

When the body was moved to the new church in 1263, with St. Bonaventure, the Minister General, presiding, the flesh had fallen to dust, but the tongue was found fresh and of a lively red colour. Bonaventure kissed it and said, “O blessed tongue, which always didst bless the Lord, and cause others to bless Him, now does it appear plainly how highly thou wert esteemed by God!” The tongue and vocal organs have been on permanent display in the Chapel of Relics of the Basilica since the examination of 1981, and Padua keeps a Feast of the Tongue. The sources disagree about the day in 1263 (8 April, or 18 February).

Status: a relic reported by the Basilica and the Franciscan Order. No Church document read for this page declares it a miracle.

A cardinal’s life preserved, 1350

At the translation of 1350 a cardinal named Guy (Guy de Boulogne in the Basilica’s account, Guy of Montfort in the 1883 chronicle) gave a silver urn and a reliquary for the saint’s jaw. The Basilica says he had been cured of the plague through Anthony’s intercession; the chronicle says his life had been preserved.

Status: reported by the Basilica and the later chronicles.

Source: Basilica of St. Anthony, Padua, “Expositions,” https://www.santantonio.org/en/node/578

Source: H. J. Coleridge, ed., The Chronicle of St. Antony of Padua (1883), p. 201, https://archive.org/details/MN5128ucmf_2

A Jesuit at the reliquary of the tongue

In the seventeenth century a Jesuit, Ignatius Martini, kissed the reliquary of the tongue and repented of the vain use he had made of his own.

Status: popular tradition, from the 1883 chronicle.

Source: H. J. Coleridge, ed., The Chronicle of St. Antony of Padua (1883), pp. 199–200, https://archive.org/details/MN5128ucmf_2

Lost things found, sixteenth and seventeenth centuries

The chronicles record things found through Anthony’s help: a manuscript of the theologian Ambrosius Catharinus, a sacristan’s thurible, and money belonging to King Charles II. Catholics invoke him especially for the recovery of things lost, a devotion tied to the story of the stolen book. The responsory “Si quaeris miracula,” sung in his honour, is attributed by the Catholic Encyclopedia to Julian of Speyer and by the 1883 chronicle to Bonaventure.

Status: popular tradition.

Source: H. J. Coleridge, ed., The Chronicle of St. Antony of Padua (1883), pp. 213–214, https://archive.org/details/MN5128ucmf_2

Source: N. Dal-Gal, “St. Anthony of Padua,” Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Catholic_Encyclopedia_(1913)/St._Anthony_of_Padua

Fire and storm in Padua, 1749 and 1756

When fire broke out in 1749, the chronicle says, none of the people on the burning roof was hurt and the saint’s altar was untouched. In a storm in 1756 the lead roof of the city hall fell, and no one was injured.

Status: popular tradition, from an eighteenth-century chronicle.

Source: H. J. Coleridge, ed., The Chronicle of St. Antony of Padua (1883), pp. 202–203, https://archive.org/details/MN5128ucmf_2
