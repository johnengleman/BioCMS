# Anthony of Padua: notes, focus own-words
Researcher: own-words agent, 2026-10-01. Tier A. Other agents cover witnesses and church-miracles.

## 1. Identity (own-words view only)
- Fernando Martins de Bulhoes, b. Lisbon c. 1195, d. Arcella near Padua, Fri 13 June 1231; Franciscan name Anthony [S1; S4 p.91]. Canonized 1232; Doctor of the Church 1946 by Pius XII, title "Doctor Evangelicus" [S1].
- Tradition: Catholic (Latin). Feast 13 June (Roman calendar) [S1; S4].
- Gregory IX called him "Ark of the Covenant" (Woodcock) / "Ark of the Testament" (Benedict XVI) [S2 p.~66; S1]. Our English: use "Ark of the Covenant" or the Latin "arca testamenti" after checking.

## 3. Timeline (own-words items only)
- c. 1195 b. Lisbon [S1].
- 1220 relics of five Franciscan martyrs of Morocco at Coimbra; he leaves the Augustinian canons; takes name Anthony [S1].
- 1222 Forli ordination: Dominicans refuse to preach; the minister orders Anthony, who spoke "with simple words" then amazed all. Tradition says text was "Christus factus est pro nobis obediens usque ad mortem" (Phil 2:8). First sermon. Woodcock marks it "says tradition" [S2 pp.21-22]. S1 also says the superior chose him in "somewhat casual circumstances".
- 1222-23 Bologna: first lector of the friars. Francis's letter [S2 p.30-31; S4 p.259 note]. NEW for popular English pages: the letter is only known from the Liber miraculorum (c. 1367) and scholars are divided on authenticity [S4 p.259 note 1].
- Rimini: sermon to the fishes (legend) [S2 pp.29-30].
- c. 1224-27 Montpellier: lectures on the Psalms with written notes; stolen psalter legend [S2 pp.34-35; S4 pp.259-260].
- 1226 (an "important critic" in S2) writing of the first sermon cycle at Limoges [S2 p.~66 n.2]. Conflict: S2 elsewhere says he began the Sunday Sermons in 1230 at Padua.
- 1230 Rome: Gregory IX hears him, calls him "Ark of the Covenant"; Cardinal-Bishop of Ostia (later Alexander IV) urges him to write down his sermons [S2 p.~68].
- Autumn 1230 to winter: at Padua, writes Sermons on the Saints (Festival sermons); work half done at death [S2 p.69; S7]. Preaches Lent 1230 daily and writes the first volume (Sermones dominicales) [S2 p.~66].
- 13 June 1231 dies at Arcella, aged 36, joining in the Penitential Psalms "to the end" [S2 p.91]. Children of Padua cry "Our blessed Father is dead" [S2 p.92].
- 1232 canonized; 1946 Doctor of the Church [S1].

## 4. Character (from his own words and sermon style)
- A nature watcher. Walks the vineyards near Padua and draws sermons from vines, spiders, cranes, doves, lilies [S2 pp.77-80].
- A fierce critic of the rich, usurers, and bad priests and bishops; spares his own Church nothing [S2 pp.81-83].
- Practical and gentle in one voice: the same sermons that scourge usury say the wounds of Christ are the dove's nest [S2 pp.79-80].
- Wrote notes, not finished speeches: Woodcock judges the sermons "cannot be judged from their written records" and says he probably wrote from memory [S2 p.76]. Neale calls them "Sermon Notes" [S5].
- He called his body his "little ass" (asinello) after Francis [S2 p.~66 n.1; p.104 story].

## 5. Quotations (marked: rights; Latin checked or not)
Rules: English is the 1911 translation of Woodcock (public-domain) unless marked. Latin lines are OCR from S3; spelling normalized in brackets where I corrected long s. Locator format: sermon, folio of S3.

Q1. Vine. EN (S2 p.77-78): "Man's soul is a vine, ... for to bring forth fruit it must be carefully and ceaselessly cultivated. ... The vine left to itself becomes the wildest of all growing things. Its wood is useless, except to burn, when it is dry." Rights: public-domain. Latin checked in S3, Feria VI Hebd. II in Quadr., fol. LXXXVIII (djvu line ~8192-8206): "Moraliter vinea ista est homo vel anima ... quia sicut vinea indiget cura multa si debeat fructum facere, sic homo &c. Et sicut vinea super omnia ligna statim efficitur silvestris si non colitur, sic homo ... Item vinea non fructifera nihil valet nisi ad comburendum." Match is close but Woodcock's English is looser and adds "man's soul is a vine" which I did not find word for word. Status: confirmed in substance; use with care.
Q2. Purple of the poor. EN (S2 p.82): "How many rich men of our day are clad in purple - that is in stuffs dyed with the sweat and blood of the poor, because the clothes they wear are woven out of theft, larceny, usury, and illegitimate gain? ... But the garment dyed with the blood of the poor shall be the prey of eternal flames." Latin checked in S3, Feria V Hebd. II, fol. LXXXV (djvu lines 7807-7810; OCR): "O quot induuntur hodie purpura, panno tincto sudore et sanguine pauperis; quia induuntur rapina, furto, usura, illicito lucro: sed in veritate omnis violenta praedatio erit cum tumultu ... vestimentum mixtum sanguine." Rights: public-domain. Conflict: Woodcock footnote says "Fer. 6, Dom. I, Quadr."; the Latin I found is under Thursday of the second week (Dives and Lazarus). Woodcock's "larceny" is not in the Latin I read ("rapina, furto, usura, illicito lucro").
Q3. Usurer worse than Judas. EN (S2 p.82): "The usurer ... is worse than Judas. That traitor, having sold the Blood of his Divine Master, brought back to the priests and princes the thirty pieces he had received, but the usurer guards and keeps his unjust gains." Rights: public-domain. Latin: not found (no locator in S2: "elsewhere"). Status: English only, locator missing.
Q4. Almsgiving as well bucket. EN (S2 p.82): "Thus when the bucket of almsgiving is let down into the well of riches it makes their possession purer to the conscience and more agreeable to God." Rights: public-domain. Latin: S3 has "Puteus ... mundana concupiscentia" (fol. ~LXXX) but I did not confirm the bucket passage. Status: English only.
Q5. Spider and the heart. EN (S2 p.78, "Sermon in Septuagesima"): "the devil spreads his web like a spider! The spider begins from the outside and gradually works inwards with thousands of threads, sitting to watch in the centre. ... for is not the heart the source of life?" Rights: public-domain. Latin: not in S3 (not a Lent sermon). Status: English only; Latin needs the Sunday sermons.
Q6. Cranes. EN (S2 p.79, Sermon Dom. IV post Trin.): "Be merciful, like the cranes ... When a flock of these birds makes a long flight one flies in front and ... with its cries encourages the others. ... So point out by your good example the way of Truth to those that know it not, help on those who are slow, soothe those who are too hasty ... bear one another's burdens." Rights: public-domain. Latin not checked.
Q7. Dove in the cleft of the rock. EN (S2 pp.79-80): "Be as the dove who makes her nest in the deepest cleft of the rock. This cleft, in which the soul should hide itself, is the wound in the side of Jesus Christ. ... the wound in His side leads to the Heart ... they are the virtues of our Saviour ... humility, gentleness, poverty, patience, mortification. The world despises them as useless, but it is with these that we shall build our nest, deep in the Rock, in the Heart of Jesus." Rights: public-domain. Latin not checked. Best passage for the site: shows the person.
Q8. Lilies of the field. EN (S2 pp.78-79, Dom. XV post Trin.): "Hermits flourish in the desert, ... monks stand like flowers in the cloister garth, protected from the great heat of the sun, but the penitent lives and thrives and blossoms in the field of the world." Rights: public-domain. Latin not checked.
Q9. Priests who sue. EN (S2 p.83, Sermon on Peter and Paul): "Tell me, ye priests, is it in the prophets or in the Gospel, in the Epistles of St. Paul or the Rules of St. Benedict and St. Augustine, that you find these disputes, these lawsuits, these intrigues for transitory and perishable things?" Rights: public-domain. Latin not checked.
Q10. Absent bishops. paraphrase (S2 p.83): he compares absentee bishops to idols with eyes that see not, with "hands to gather money, but not to touch the wounds of Christ". The last phrase is Woodcock's quotation; Latin not checked.
Q11. Last judgment. EN (S2 p.84, Sermon II, Dom. II Quadr.): "Whither will the sinner flee; where will he hide? Flight is impossible; he cannot conceal himself! To appear before God is unbearable terror." Rights: public-domain. Latin not checked. Woodcock adds that a modern critic thinks this sermon may have inspired the Dies Irae; treat as opinion.
Q12. Francis's letter. Latin (S4 p.259 n.1): "Carissimo meo Fratri Antonio Frater Franciscus salutem in Christo. Placet mihi quod sacram theologiam legas fratribus, dummodo per hujusmodi studium spiritum non extinguas, sicut in regula continetur. Vale." EN (S2 p.30): "I desire that you instruct the brothers in Sacred Theology, provided that this study does not overcome in them the spirit of holy meditation and devotion according to the prescriptions of the Rule." Rights: public-domain. Status: authenticity disputed (S4). Note this is a letter TO Anthony, not by him. Anthony wrote no known letters.
Q13. Last words reported. EN (S2 p.91): "It is not necessary, Brother, for I have the Unction within my soul. Neverthe-less, it is well to receive it, and I will gladly do so." Rights: public-domain. Source in S2 is Rigauld (a later Life). Not checked against the early Life (witnesses agent should check Assidua/Rigaldina).
Q14. Benedict-quoted lines (modern translation, Holy See, copyright; quote one at most, or paraphrase): (a) charity is the soul of faith (Sermones Dominicales et Festivi II, p.37); (b) Christ hangs before you "so that you may look at the Cross as in a mirror" (III, pp.213-214); (c) if you preach Jesus he melts hard hearts (III, p.59) [S1]. Latin not checked: the critical edition is under copyright and the 1521 Lent text does not contain them. Mark paraphrase.
Q15. The four attitudes of prayer, in Anthony's Latin as listed by Benedict: obsecratio, oratio, postulatio, gratiarum actio [S1]. Rights: facts-only for the list (Latin words are quotable).

## 6. Scenes
1. 1222 Forli. Dominicans decline to speak; the minister turns to the kitchen friar; he starts with simple words and the room grows still [S2 pp.21-22].
2. Rimini. Heretics laugh; he walks to the river and preaches to the fishes; the crowd watches (legend) [S2 pp.29-30].
3. Montpellier cell door bursts open; a novice with the stolen psalter, trembling [S2 pp.34-35; S4 pp.259-260].
4. Lent 1230 Padua. Priests cannot hear all the confessions; he forgets to eat until evening [S2 p.81].
5. Winter 1230: a sick friar writes at night the Sermons on the Saints [S2 p.69].
6. Arcella, 13 June 1231: he asks for the Penitential Psalms, then dies; the children run through Padua crying [S2 pp.91-92].
7. Vineyards at Padua: he walks them and turns what he sees into sermons [S2 p.77].

## 7. Miracles
Not my focus. One own-words item: the stolen psalter (status: legend, first written in Liber miraculorum 1367 per S2 p.35) [S2 pp.34-35; S4 p.259].

## 8. Other

### Works
- Sermones dominicales (Sundays), Sermones in solemnitatibus / festivi (Sermons on the Saints/Festivals). Mixed order: Woodcock puts first volume at Limoges 1226 (disputed) or 1230 Padua; Festival sermons winter 1230-31 [S2; S7; S1]. Critical edition of the Latin: Padua 1979 (copyright). Old edition: Locatelli, Padua 1895 [S2 appendix].
- Lent sermons exist in old printed editions (Paris 1521; Venice 1575), now on archive.org [S3; S3b].
- "Sermones in psalmos" published by Azzoguidi, Bologna 1757, attributed to him; the editors in Kerval say this is a link people made to the "psalterium glossatum" [S4 p.259 n.2]. Authorship unverified; do not quote.
- "Concordantiae morales": ascribed to him; Neale translated them 1856 [S5]. Authorship to check.
- He wrote the sermons as outlines, with long chains of Bible texts and "arbitrary" etymologies (Woodcock's appendix: Thamar = amara, commutata, palma) [S2 p.105-108].
- Anthony's method in short: the four senses of Scripture (literal, allegorical, moral, anagogical), per Benedict XVI [S1].

### Popular prayers wrongly credited to him (flags)
- The rhyme "Tony, Tony, turn around, something's lost and must be found": described by EWTN as "an old couplet"; it is not his and not in any source I read as old. Origin date unknown [S6]. Do not use as his.
- Reverent invocation "The sea obeys, and fetters break ... while treasures lost are found again" - EWTN attributes the words to Julian of Spires (Speyer) [S6]. Conflict: popular pages credit Anthony; Julian is a Franciscan composer of his office. Need Latin "Si quaeris miracula" text and an authorship source from a book or Latin text to confirm (not found in my reads).
- Anthony's patronage of lost things begins from the stolen psalter story, first recorded in Liber miraculorum c. 1367, though a devotion may be older [S2 p.35; S4 pp.259-260].
- St Anthony's Bread, Tuesday devotions, the "Miraculous Responsory" are later devotions: not checked here.
