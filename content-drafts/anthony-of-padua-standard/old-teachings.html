Anthony left no letters and no treatise for ordinary readers. What survives is his Latin sermons: a cycle for the Sundays of the year; an unfinished cycle for the feasts of the saints, which the Cardinal-Bishop of Ostia asked him to write in 1230 and which he worked on at Padua that winter; and sermons for Lent printed in the sixteenth century. They are notes for preachers rather than finished sermons, built from long chains of Bible texts, and his 1911 English biographer warned that they “cannot be judged from their written records.” The sermon passages below are in that biographer’s English (C. M. Antony, 1911). For two passages, the vine and the purple of the poor, our researchers checked the Latin of a 1521 printed edition; the other passages have not yet been checked against the Latin. In 1946 Pope Pius XII named him a Doctor of the Church, the Evangelical Doctor, for the richness of these sermons. Two popular texts are not his: the rhyme “Tony, Tony, turn around,” whose origin is unknown, and the responsory “Si quaeris miracula,” which the Catholic Encyclopedia gives to Julian of Speyer and an 1883 chronicle to Bonaventure.

Sermons in vines, spiders and cranes

Anthony drew his sermons from the world around him: a vine, a spider’s web, a flock of cranes, the lilies of the field. His 1911 biographer pictures him walking the vineyards around Padua, though the sermons themselves do not say where he stood. In a Lenten sermon for the Friday of the second week of Lent, which survives in the old Latin (“Moraliter vinea ista est homo vel anima”), the vine is the soul.

“Man’s soul is a vine, … for to bring forth fruit it must be carefully and ceaselessly cultivated. … The vine left to itself becomes the wildest of all growing things. Its wood is useless, except to burn, when it is dry.”

— Anthony of Padua, Lenten sermon, in C. M. Antony’s English (1911)

Elsewhere the spider becomes the devil, who “spreads his web like a spider” and works inward toward the heart, and the cranes become a picture of a community: one flies in front and calls to the others, and so Christians should “point out by your good example the way of Truth to those that know it not, help on those who are slow, soothe those who are too hasty.” Anthony had spent eight years at Coimbra reading, and the early Lives say his memory served him in place of books; in his sermons that learning goes out into the fields.

The purple of the poor

Anthony preached hard against the ways men grew rich at others’ expense: theft, usury and dishonest gain. Fine clothes bought with such money, he said, were dyed in the blood of the poor. The Latin of this Lenten passage survives in the old printed editions: “O quot induuntur hodie purpura, panno tincto sudore et sanguine pauperis.”

“How many rich men of our day are clad in purple — that is in stuffs dyed with the sweat and blood of the poor, because the clothes they wear are woven out of theft, larceny, usury, and illegitimate gain? … But the garment dyed with the blood of the poor shall be the prey of eternal flames.”

— Anthony of Padua, Lenten sermon, in C. M. Antony’s English (1911)

The usurer, he said, was worse than Judas, who at least brought the thirty pieces back. The remedy was to give: “when the bucket of almsgiving is let down into the well of riches it makes their possession purer to the conscience and more agreeable to God.” He lived this in the pulpit of Padua. In the Lent of 1231, the early Life says, usurers gave back what they had taken, and on 15 March of that year the city passed a law to protect debtors, a copy of which survives in a museum in Padua.

Shepherds who go to law

Anthony spared his own Church nothing. He compared bishops who stayed away from their people to idols, with “hands to gather money, but not to touch the wounds of Christ,” and he asked priests who sued for property where they had learned such things.

“Tell me, ye priests, is it in the prophets or in the Gospel, in the Epistles of St. Paul or the Rules of St. Benedict and St. Augustine, that you find these disputes, these lawsuits, these intrigues for transitory and perishable things?”

— Anthony of Padua, sermon on Sts. Peter and Paul, in C. M. Antony’s English (1911)

He could say such things to a bishop’s face. At a synod at Bourges, Rigauld was told, he turned to the archbishop with the words “I speak to thee, mitre-bearer!” and rebuked him until the archbishop wept.

A nest in the wound of Christ

The most tender of his surviving passages tells the soul where to live. Like a dove that nests in the cleft of a rock, the soul should hide in the wound in Christ’s side, which leads to His Heart, and build its nest there out of the virtues the world despises.

“Be as the dove who makes her nest in the deepest cleft of the rock. This cleft, in which the soul should hide itself, is the wound in the side of Jesus Christ. … The world despises them as useless, but it is with these that we shall build our nest, deep in the Rock, in the Heart of Jesus.”

— Anthony of Padua, sermon, in C. M. Antony’s English (1911)

The virtues he names are humility, gentleness, poverty, patience and mortification. He had practised them long before he preached them: at the hermitage of Monte Paolo he hid his learning, washed the pots and the friars’ feet, and lived on bread and water. He died at Arcella looking upward, and when the friars asked what he saw, he answered, “I see my Lord.”

Holiness in the field of the world

Anthony did not think holiness belonged only to hermits and monks. In a sermon on the lilies of the field, he gave each its place, and the last and the best place went to ordinary penitents living in the world.

“Hermits flourish in the desert, … monks stand like flowers in the cloister garth, protected from the great heat of the sun, but the penitent lives and thrives and blossoms in the field of the world.”

— Anthony of Padua, sermon for the 15th Sunday after Trinity, in C. M. Antony’s English (1911)

He preached that way to the crowds of Padua in 1231, out in the fields because no church could hold them: enemies were reconciled, prisoners set free, and so many came to confess that the priests of the city could not hear them all.

Where will the sinner hide?

Anthony was gentle and severe at once, and the early Latin Life says his hearers felt love and fear together. He spoke of the judgment of God without softening it.

“Whither will the sinner flee; where will he hide? Flight is impossible; he cannot conceal himself! To appear before God is unbearable terror.”

— Anthony of Padua, second sermon for the 2nd Sunday of Lent, in C. M. Antony’s English (1911)

His 1911 biographer mentions the opinion of a modern critic that this sermon may lie behind the hymn Dies Irae. The early Life says famous preachers trembled under his words and hid their faces behind a hand or a sleeve.

Prayer and the Word

Anthony read Scripture in its four senses: the literal, the allegorical, the moral and the anagogical. He taught four attitudes of prayer, which he named in Latin obsecratio, oratio, postulatio and gratiarum actio. In his general audience on Anthony in 2010, Pope Benedict XVI drew out other lines from the sermons: that charity is the soul of faith; that Christ hangs on the cross before us so that we may look at it as into a mirror; and that preaching Jesus softens hard hearts. Those lines are given here in paraphrase, because the modern translations are under copyright. Anthony himself did what he taught: he preached every day of the Lent of 1231, and he spent that winter, though sick, writing his sermons down for other preachers.
