At Pentecost in 1221 the friars of Saint Francis held their general meeting at Assisi. Brothers had come to it from many provinces of the young Order. When it was over they were sent back to their places in every direction. One friar was left over. He came from Portugal. He was weak and ill, and he had never meant to set foot in Italy. He had sailed to Africa to die for Christ, but a long illness had stopped him there. On the voyage home the wind had driven his ship to Sicily. Now the meeting had ended, and nobody had any work for him. An early Life of Saint Anthony says it in a single line: “Only Anthony was asked for by no one, for he seemed as useless as he was unknown.”1

The Church now counts that friar among its Doctors, the small company of saints whose teaching it holds up for the whole world. Pope Pius XII gave him the title in 1946 and called him the Evangelical Doctor, because his sermons draw so constantly on the Gospels. An English biographer thought he was perhaps the first friar of Saint Francis to teach theology to the brothers. A city changed its laws at his request, so that a man ruined by debt would no longer spend his life in prison. Pope Gregory IX had known him, and he named him a saint less than a year after he died. For most people since then he has simply been the saint you ask when something is lost.2

The sick friar at Assisi knew none of this. He had wanted something else, and he had wanted it with his whole heart. His story begins in Lisbon, near the western doors of a great church of the Virgin Mary.3

Saint Anthony Leaves Home for Coimbra

He was born about 1195 in Lisbon, a city that the early Life places “at the farthest ends of the earth”. His parents lived near the cathedral, which was built in honour of the Mother of God. The body of the martyr Saint Vincent rested inside it. His parents were young when he was born, and he was their first son. They named him Fernando at his baptism. When he was a boy, they gave him to that same church to learn his letters and good conduct. So the Virgin's church was his first school, and from his earliest years the Mother of God was his teacher. As he grew up, he did not give way to the pleasures of the world and the flesh. He was already making up his mind to serve God alone.4

Looking at the world around him, he saw how quickly it passed away. He understood that all it offered was the craving of the flesh and of the eyes, and the pride of life. About 1210 he went to the house of Saint Vincent just outside the city and took the robe of the Augustinian canons. Canons lived together under a rule and sang the Church's prayers in choir each day. They also cared for the souls of the parishes in their charge, and they were bound in a special way to study. To enter, he left his father and mother and gave up every right to inherit from them. What he longed for there was peace and calm of soul.5

He did not find it there. His family and friends came to see him again and again, and their visits broke the peace of his heart. They were dear to him, and still he decided to go. After nearly two years he asked to leave Lisbon for Holy Cross at Coimbra, another house of the same Order. Like Abraham, he wished to go out from his country and his kindred. His superior let him go only with difficulty. One early writer says it was because of the seriousness of his character. Another says it was because everyone loved him.6

At Coimbra he found the quiet he had wanted. The brothers there soon knew him as a man who sought inner peace, and as a “zealous lover of the Cross”. Moving there did him so much good that no one could blame it on a restless heart. The Spirit was already drawing him toward the study of the Scriptures, as if to prepare him for what lay ahead. In them he learned to weed out faults and plant virtues in his own soul first. He would need that skill later in the souls of others. He also armed himself with the teaching of the Fathers, the great Christian writers of the first centuries. In this way he could build up the faith and answer the errors against it.7

God gave him such light in his studies that “his memory served him in the place of books”. He spent eight years in that house, given to obedience, prayer, and study. By the end of them he knew the Bible by heart, and the best passages of the Fathers as well. He was a learned canon in a quiet cloister, and he might have stayed there all his life.8

Saint Anthony Becomes a Friar

In those years the new Order of Saint Francis was spreading across Europe. Many of its friars longed to die for Christ. They sailed to the lands of the unbelievers. They hoped either to bring them to the faith or to win the palm that marks a martyr's victory, “for love of Christ who died for us”. In 1220 news came from Morocco. Five of those friars had preached Christ there in public, and a savage king had put them to the sword. Wonders followed their deaths. Their reward was Christ, the bread of life who came down from heaven. A great prince of Portugal named Pedro brought their relics home. He spread the story of their sufferings everywhere, and he said that their merits had saved him from great dangers.9

The news reached the young canon at Holy Cross, and it did not reach him in vain. He was “like a war-horse ready for the day of battle”. He could not stop thinking of the wrong done to Christ and of the death of the martyrs, and he took their suffering into himself with a wonderful compassion. Unless he too went out to meet the tyrant's fury and won the same palm for Christ, he counted himself as doing nothing at all.10

Now only one thing seemed worth doing: to give his life back to Christ, who had died for him. The fear of the sword did not weaken him. Instead the fire of a perfect love changed him. With an eager mind he worked out what he must do, and he decided to take the robe of the men who had died. Their way of life might lead him more surely to the contest he longed for. His resolve was simple: “I will take their robe, and I too will go to the fight.” He was already what his later biographer Jean Rigauld would call him: “a martyr of desire”.11

The way opened at the monastery door:

“One day he was turning over in his mind how best to carry out his plan. Then some poor Friars Minor, who had settled near Coimbra, came to the monastery to beg alms for Christ's poor. When he saw them, he could hold himself back no longer. He took them aside, opened to them the longing of his heart, and begged them most humbly to take him into their Order.”

— Jean Rigauld, Life of Blessed Anthony, about 130012

The poor friars were overjoyed to see a new sheep coming into their fold. They set a day and went away thanking God. He asked his superior for leave, and once again he got it only with difficulty. On the day they had set, the friars came back and put their robe on him there in the monastery. It was cut in the shape of a cross. He wore a cross now, he who “so ardently desired to be crucified for Jesus Christ”. The robe was tied at the waist with a cord, the sign of obedience. One day obedience would decide his whole life.13

Leaving cost him something. As he went out with the friars, one of the canons spoke to him in the bitterness of his heart. This canon loved him dearly and grieved more than the rest. “Go, go,” he said. “Perhaps you will become a saint.” The new friar answered with the simplicity of a dove: “When you hear that I am a saint, surely you will give thanks to God.” Then he left Holy Cross, the house where he had first seen the robe of the Friars Minor. He left behind the men he had lived beside for eight years.14

The friars lived at a small place near Coimbra called Saint Anthony. When he reached it, he asked to be called Anthony from then on. He wanted the people who came looking for him to lose his trail. Under a name nobody knew, he could turn away their visits more easily. He was giving up a name often borne by the sons of kings and nobles in that country. Fernando was the name his parents had given him at the font. Now he set it aside for good.15

The robe was his now, and the new name. All that was left was the fight.16

Saint Anthony Seeks Martyrdom in Morocco

He could not rest. The king of Morocco was still raging against Christ, and the young friar was eager for martyrdom. Very soon he asked his superiors for leave to go among the unbelievers. There he would preach the faith openly, and by their hands he would offer himself as a sacrifice “to Christ who died for us on the altar of the cross”. His superiors gave him leave. Saint Anthony crossed the sea to the land of the Saracens, as Christians then called the Muslims of North Africa.17

He arrived. Then everything went wrong. A long and very serious illness took hold of him. He had come to preach Christ in the open, and now he could do none of it. Nothing he wished for was going well, and he understood that he was not going to succeed. He had crossed the sea to give his whole life to Christ in a single act. Instead he lay sick, far from home. In the end necessity forced him to turn back toward a Christian land.18

He took ship for Spain. The winds were against the ship, and it came to land in Sicily instead. There he saw that he had been “cheated of his plan completely”. He had wanted to give everything to Christ in a single hour. Now he was ill on an island he had never meant to reach, and his plan lay in ruins.19

In Sicily some friars told him that the general meeting of the whole Order would soon be held at Assisi. Weak and sick as he was, he got there as best he could.20

Saint Anthony Hides at Monte Paolo

That was how he came to Assisi, where no one asked for him. Yet God had closed the door to Morocco on purpose. The King of kings had decided something different from what human sense would choose. The wisdom of Christ orders all things sweetly, and it was keeping him for greater things.21

At Assisi he said nothing about his learning, and nothing about any other way he might be useful. Instead he went to Brother Gratian, who led the friars of Romagna in northern Italy. He humbly asked Brother Gratian to request him from the head of the Order, and he begged to be taken in and trained in the life of the Rule. Brother Gratian received him kindly and took him north.22

Saint Anthony asked for a place of solitude. Brother Gratian sent him to the hermitage of Monte Paolo, near the town of Forlì. God was at work in this. In solitude the young friar would grow in humility. Through prayer and study he would gather a store of seed to scatter later in his preaching. At Monte Paolo he found what he had wanted for a long time: a cell in a crypt, apart from the other friars and fit for prayer. One of the brothers had made it ready for himself, and he gave it to the newcomer.23

He kept his learning out of sight, for a man who carries treasure openly on the public road invites robbers. So for a long time he lived as a simple man among simple men, hiding the light of God's grace under the look of a man who had never studied. To be taken for ignorant seemed better to him than to be puffed up with pride. The lowest work was the work he looked for: the plates and the kitchen pots, and the feet of the friars, which he washed and then kissed. For about two years he read nothing but his breviary, the book of the Church's daily prayers. Illness and his lowly work left room for nothing else. Yet nothing he had learned at Coimbra was lost.24

His heart was still full of zeal for the house of God. But by God's will he had already been denied his great desire once. After that he did not dare to reach for honour again by his own choice. He had given himself to God, and he left it to God to bring him out.25

So in the crypt he lived for God alone:

“There he led a solitary life, as far as he was allowed. There he strengthened his spirit against temptations through holy meditation, and he set himself firm in the love of God. There he kept the night watches alone in prayer. There he gave himself wholly to God's plan and fixed the anchor of a most firm hope on high. There too he wore his body down with so much fasting on bread and water that sometimes, when he had to go back to the friars at the evening hour, his step wavered from weakness, and those who were there saw that he could not hold himself up.”

— An early Life of Saint Anthony, written in the 1230s26

Saint Anthony Is Ordered to Preach

He stayed hidden a long time. About 1222 his superiors sent some friars to Forlì to be ordained as priests, and he went with them. A few Dominican friars had come for the same ordination. They belonged to the Order of Preachers, the new Order of Saint Dominic. The brothers gathered in the evening for their talk together. The Minister who led the friars of that place asked the Dominicans to give the brothers a word of encouragement. By God's will, they all refused. Each said he had not prepared anything. The Minister asked his own friars, and they made the same excuse.27

Then the Minister turned to Saint Anthony. He knew nothing of the young friar's learning, but he felt the Spirit pressing him. He begged Saint Anthony earnestly, and then he ordered him with authority to tell the friars whatever the Holy Spirit gave him. The friars thought him better at washing kitchen pots than at explaining the mysteries of Scripture. Only very rarely had he let slip a few words that sounded like a scholar's.28

He refused with all his strength:29

“Anthony would much rather have been a pupil than a teacher. He protested that he was unfit and unprepared. He did all he could to avoid what seemed to him an ambitious act, until the formal command laid on him left him no choice. … He began, in the fear of God, with simple words. But heavenly grace lit his mind, and his memory served him in place of books. As he went on, his words rose higher, and he explained the deep mysteries of Holy Scripture so clearly, and spoke so powerfully, that his hearers were astonished. … They said to one another, ‘No one ever spoke like this man.’”

— Jean Rigauld, Life of Blessed Anthony, about 130030

He had not been able to resist the man who gave the order. In the end he gave way against his will, and the Lord did the rest. He had prepared his soul, and so the Lord governed his tongue. That evening the Lord took the lamp that had been hidden so long under a basket, and he set it on a lampstand. The friars were filled with joy. From then on they honoured the wisdom that God had hidden in this man. They honoured just as much the humility that had hidden it.31

The news soon reached the head of the whole Order, who gave Saint Anthony the office of preacher and made him go out in public. The young friar had been tested first as a poor man among the poor. He had not snatched at this honour before God called him. His life and his death would both show that the call was God's. He left Monte Paolo, the hermitage where Brother Gratian had let him hide. The fight he had wanted for so long came to him at last, and it would be fought with words.32

Saint Anthony Preaches in Italy and France

He exchanged the quiet of the hermitage for a kind of work he had never known. At Monte Paolo he had watched over his own soul. Now he went far and wide through the cities and fortified towns for the good of others. In northern Italy the heretics called Patarines taught that the world was ruled by two powers, one good and one evil, and Rimini was one of their strongholds. There he answered them with fitting arguments and brought very many back to the full Christian faith. One of their leaders had lived in the darkness of that error for thirty years. Saint Anthony led him into the light of the truth, and the man kept the Church's commands until he died.33

About this time he began to teach theology to the brothers at Bologna, in the friars' own house rather than at the city's famous university. Saint Francis had not at first meant his brothers to study theology, but he had changed his mind. The friars have handed down a short letter that Saint Francis sent about this work. “To my dearest Brother Anthony, Brother Francis sends greeting in Christ,” it begins. “It pleases me that you teach sacred theology to the brothers, provided that by such study you do not put out the spirit, as the Rule says. Farewell.”34

About 1224 the Order sent him over the mountains into France. He led the friars at Limoges and in the whole Limousin district. He founded their first house at Brive, and for a time he taught theology at Montpellier, where he lectured on the Psalms. Though he was their superior, he wanted to be taken for the lowest of the brothers. On the road he gave way in everything to the friar who walked with him.35

On those roads he carried nothing, yet God saw to it that he and his companions never went without what they needed. Bread and water were his food when he fasted, and he wore an iron chain next to his skin. Often his feet trembled so much that they could hardly hold him up. Fastened in this way to the cross of penance with Christ, he lived a kind of martyrdom that lasted his whole life.36

Prayer was the heart of it all. One night in the Limousin he was praying in the chapel after Compline, the last prayer of the day. Some friars ran to him there in tears. By moonlight they had seen a band of men tearing up the ripe grain of a good friend of the friars. He calmly told them to leave it alone and go on with their prayers, since the enemy only wanted to disturb their rest and break off their prayer. At daylight they went over the field and found that not a stalk had been touched.37

In the pulpit he was “gracious and severe” at once, so that his hearers felt love and fear rise in them together. He gave each hearer what that person could take in, and no one went away without some good. But he rebuked powerful men so sternly that famous preachers who heard him trembled at his courage. They went red with shame at their own timid hearts. Wishing themselves far away, they hid their faces behind a hand or a sleeve.38

Once he preached at a meeting of the clergy at Bourges, with the archbishop among his hearers. Suddenly Saint Anthony turned to him and called him by the tall headdress of his rank: “I speak to you, mitre-bearer!” Then he rebuked the archbishop for faults hidden in his conscience. He quoted the Scriptures against them so clearly that the archbishop was moved to tears. When the meeting was over, the archbishop took him aside and humbly opened to him the wounds of his conscience. From that day the archbishop was more faithful to God, and he became a friend to the friars.39

This was the fight he had been given. He had once thirsted so eagerly for “the cup of suffering” that he sailed to Africa to meet a tyrant's fury. Now, for the sake of the truth, he gave way to no one, however great, and the fear of death did not move him. With wonderful firmness he stood up even to the tyranny of powerful men.40

Another time in France an immense crowd gathered to hear him on a wide plain called the Creux-des-Arènes. The temples of the old pagan gods had once stood there. No church could hold so many. He had begun to preach, and the people were listening closely. Then thunder rolled and lightning flashed across the clouds, and the rain began to fall. The people were afraid, and they started to break up and leave. He calmed them and spoke quietly:41

“Do not be afraid. Do not move, and do not stop listening to the word of God. I trust in the One who never lets us trust in vain, that the rain will not touch you.”

— Saint Anthony, in Jean Rigauld's Life of Blessed Anthony42

The people obeyed and stayed where they were. God held the rain back from them, and all around the crowd it poured down in torrents. Saint Anthony went on preaching as long as he wished. When he had finished, the people looked at the drenched ground around them. Then they looked at the dry earth under their own feet. They praised the power of God, who is wonderful in his saints.43

About 1226 the friars of Provence gathered at Arles for their chapter meeting, and he preached to them. His subject was the title that Pilate had set over the cross: “Jesus of Nazareth, King of the Jews”. He spoke of it gently and warmly. Saint Francis was still alive then, but far away in Italy. As Saint Anthony preached, Saint Francis appeared at the door of the chapter-room. He was raised in the air with his arms stretched out in the form of a cross, and he blessed the friars. Only one of the brothers saw him with his own eyes, a friar of proven virtue named Monaldo.44

Saint Francis came, as if to approve the sermon and to show the hearers what they should imitate. He marked his sons with the sign of the cross. It was fitting that he came to that sermon. Saint Francis had carried the cross of Christ before all the friars. And from the day he entered the Order, Saint Anthony had longed for the sufferings of the cross. He “still continued to desire” them, even while he preached about them to others. The martyr he had meant to be lived on in him.45

In 1226 Saint Francis died, and the friars lost the father of their Order. To Saint Anthony he had been a father too, the one who had blessed the friars at Arles while Saint Anthony preached on the cross. After the news reached him, Saint Anthony left France and set out again for Italy.46

All of Padua Comes to Hear Saint Anthony

In 1230 the friars of the whole Order met again at Assisi. It was the year they moved the body of Saint Francis to its new tomb. At that meeting Saint Anthony was freed from every office of government over the friars. He was given leave to preach wherever he chose. Nine years earlier no one at Assisi had wanted him. Now he left Assisi free to carry the word of God anywhere.47

Pope Gregory IX had heard him preach and was struck by how well he knew the Scriptures. He gave him a name of his own: the Ark of the Covenant, after the sacred chest that held the tablets of God's law. He also called him a storehouse of the Holy Scriptures. The cardinal bishop of Ostia asked him for something more. Saint Anthony had already written out his sermons for the Sundays of the year. The cardinal wanted the sermons for the saints' feast days added to them. Saint Anthony chose to do this work in Padua. The people there had shown him their sincere devotion before, while he was writing his Sunday sermons.48

Padua was a rich city with a young university. Many of its people borrowed money to pay for luxury and show. The usual rate was twenty-eight in every hundred a year, and fifty or sixty was not rare. A debtor who could not pay could be seized and lose everything he owned. Then he could be banished, or kept in prison for life.49

He spent the whole winter of 1230 in a small cell in Padua, writing, and preaching now and then. Already a sick man, he drove his tired body hard. Lent would begin on 5 February 1231. For those forty days of fasting before Easter he made a plan: he would give the whole season to preaching every day and hearing confessions.50

Then one night near the start of Lent he lay down to sleep, worn out from his work. The enemy of the human race wanted to stop the good he was about to do. The enemy seized Saint Anthony by the throat and squeezed so hard that he would have choked him, if God's will had not held him back. At once Saint Anthony called on the name of the glorious Virgin. She had taught him when he was a child, and now he begged her to go on protecting him. He opened his eyes and found himself free. The whole cell was filled with a bright light from above. The enemy of light could not bear it and fled.51

He kept to his plan. The people of Padua were so eager to hear him that each day a church had to be named where he would preach. Soon no church could hold them, and he moved out into the open fields. There the Spirit filled him to overflowing, and he poured the rain of saving teaching on people who were thirsty for it. They longed to hear his gentle words. The whole city came, day after day, and the bishop came with his clergy. People streamed in from the towns, castles, and villages around. Some hurried out at night with lamps lit, each trying to take a good place early. Delicate, well-off men and women put aside their fine clothes and sat there. They waited humbly until the end for the grace that came down each day. When he spoke, it was like this:52

“Everyone listened so closely while the servant of the Lord was speaking that among thirty thousand people or more you could hardly hear a murmur or a sound. The stallholders shut their shops and did not dare to sell anything at all until the sermon was over and each person went back to his own work.”

— An early Life of Saint Anthony, written in the 1230s53

When a sermon ended, everyone pressed in to touch him. Some cut small pieces from his robe to keep as relics. Anyone who managed to speak to him counted it a great gift. Coming and going, he was often afraid of being crushed. Only a strong band of young men around him kept him safe.54

And he was ill through all of it. Illness stayed with him all that Lent, and his heavy body weighed on him. Saint Francis had called the body the “little ass”. To the last Saint Anthony would not ease the hard Rule to spare his own. None of it kept him from the pulpit, from hearing confessions, or from giving advice to those who came. He ran the whole course of the forty days and gathered for the Lord a rich harvest of souls. The Lord was making his servant's goodness known, because he meant soon to glorify him before everyone. Then the people would honour him all the more, for they had seen his holiness with their own eyes.55

He did not spare the moneylenders. His written Lenten sermons have hard words for those who grew rich on usury, the lending of money at interest that the Church forbade. In one, on the rich man and the beggar Lazarus, he wrote:56

“O how many people today are dressed in purple, in cloth dyed with the sweat and blood of the poor, because they are dressed in robbery, theft, usury, and dishonest gain.”

— Saint Anthony, Lenten sermon on the rich man and Lazarus57

About 1292 a very old man was walking home from Rome with a friar. He told the friar that he had known Saint Anthony:58

“I was a robber by trade. I belonged to a gang of twelve; we lived in the forest and robbed everyone who passed. We had heard of Anthony's preaching, but we could not believe what people said about the power of his words. They compared him to a flaming torch and called him a second Elijah. So all twelve of us went in disguise to hear him. … As soon as his burning words reached our ears, we were filled with bitter sorrow for our sins. The good Father heard our confessions one after another, gave each of us a fitting penance, and forbade us ever to go back to our old life. … As for me, he gave me the penance of going twelve times on pilgrimage to the tombs of the Apostles, and I am now on my way back from Rome for the twelfth time.”

— An old pilgrim, speaking to a friar about 1292, in Rigauld's Life59

The old man wept as he told it, and they walked on together. He said that some of the twelve had gone back to their old life and soon died terrible deaths, as Saint Anthony had warned. Those who kept faith had died in peace. He himself hoped for the eternal life that Saint Anthony had promised to those who turned from their sins.60

In Padua that Lent, bitter enemies made peace. Men held for years in prison were set free. Moneylenders gave back their interest, pledges were returned, and debts were forgiven. Public sinners changed their lives. Many said that Saint Anthony had come to them in a vision and warned them. So many came to confession that the priests of the city could not hear them all.61

In his sermons he warned the people of Padua against the reckless spending that drove them into debt. He also went to the men who ruled the city, and he used all his influence to change the law on debt. On 15 March 1231 Padua passed a new law. The law itself says it was made at the request of “Blessed Anthony”. From then on, a citizen who gave up all his goods could no longer be kept in prison for life or driven into exile for debt. Easter was eight days away, and he was still preaching every day.62

Saint Anthony Prays in a Walnut Tree

One day that spring he was on a hill with a travelling companion, looking out over the plain of Padua and the city. His spirit filled with joy. He began to praise the city highly, and he said that it would soon be adorned with great honour. Both early biographers took his words as a sign that he knew what was coming. The early Life says he foresaw the glory that waited for him. Only a few years later its writer could already point to the crowds of pilgrims who came to Padua because of him. Rigauld says he knew that he would soon die.63

As harvest time drew near, he saw the people busy in the fields. He decided to stop preaching for a while so that they could gather their crops. The constant talk of people in the world had also worn him out. He left the noise of the city for a quiet place about ten miles north of Padua, where the friars had a small house. It was called Camposampiero, the Field of Saint Peter, and it seemed fitting that he should rest there. Saint Peter holds the keys of heaven, and he would soon open its gates to this tired friar.64

The lord of that land was a nobleman named Tiso. He received Saint Anthony with great devotion and treated him with every kindness. A few days later Tiso built him a cell with his own hands. He chose a pleasant spot planted with trees, not far from the friars' house. There he set the cell up in a walnut tree whose branches spread out wide from a single trunk. Rigauld says it stood on a height so that the friar might be nearer to heaven. Saint Anthony gladly took it as his own.65

Up there among the branches he gave himself to meditation and prayer. His true home was already in heaven. In those last days he lifted himself above the earth, to make his spirit clean of every earthly stain. Rigauld pictures him joined in prayer with the choirs of angels.66

He had often preached about a hiding place like that. One of his favourite pictures was the dove:

“Be like the dove that makes her nest in the deepest cleft of the rock. This cleft, where the soul should hide itself, is the wound in the side of Jesus Christ. … The wound in his side leads to his Heart. … They are the virtues of our Saviour: humility, gentleness, poverty, patience, and self-denial. The world despises them as useless. But it is with these that we shall build our nest, deep in the Rock, in the Heart of Jesus.”

— Saint Anthony, from his sermons67

One day at the hour of the meal he came down from the tree to eat with the friars. Suddenly a violent illness gripped him. He lay down on a bed and soon lost all his strength. As the sickness grew worse, he felt that he could not last long. The friars of that small house were poor, and he did not want to be a burden to them. So he asked to be taken back to Padua.68

The friars begged him not to leave them. They held out as long as they could. In the end they gave in, much against their will. With tears and cries of grief they placed their father in a carriage and let him go.69

Saint Anthony Dies at Arcella

Near the city a friar met the carriage on the road. He saw how ill Saint Anthony was. Because of the crowds in Padua, he urged him not to go on to the friars' house in the middle of the city. Saint Anthony should turn aside instead to Arcella, just outside the walls. In a small house there, friars served the Poor Ladies, the nuns of Saint Clare. Saint Anthony agreed. He reached Arcella “exhausted in body but courageous in spirit”, and there he rested for a little while.70

The illness grew, and soon everyone could see that he was dying:

“After he had made a holy confession and received absolution, he began to say the hymn of the Blessed Virgin, ‘O gloriosa Domina’. Then he lifted his eyes and gazed straight upward for some time. As he gazed so intently, he was asked what he saw. He answered, ‘I see my Lord.’ As was the custom, the friars brought the oil of anointing. He said to them, ‘I have this anointing within me. You do not need to do this for me; even so, it pleases me, and it is good for me.’ He received the anointing with devotion, and with the friars he sang the psalms of repentance and finished them to the end. After this he lived a little while longer, about half an hour, and so, in the hands of those who stood around him, he died, like one gently falling asleep.”

— An early Life of Saint Anthony, written in the 1230s71

It was Friday, 13 June 1231. He was about thirty-six years old. “O gloriosa Domina” means “O glorious Lady”. His last prayers began with her, the Mother of God. With that hymn he asked the Queen of heaven herself to let him in, for she is the bright gate of heaven. She had taught him as a boy in Lisbon, and she had saved him on that night in Lent. Before the hymn he had received Holy Communion, the Body of the Lord, to go with him on his way. While he was still in the body, he was given to see his Lord. Then the good Jesus led his soul into the joy of heaven.72

He had sailed to Morocco to die for Christ, and he had been turned back. Now he died on a Friday, the day Christ suffered. He had not been able to become like Christ by martyrdom, and so he was made like the suffering Christ on the day of his death.73

The friars who were with him grieved bitterly. The Poor Ladies wept when the news was brought to them. It was right to rejoice because he had entered heaven. It was right also to weep for the man who had turned so many sinners from their evil ways. Then the friars made a decision. They knew how the people of Padua loved him, and they feared what the crowds would do. They meant to keep his death quiet.74

Padua Fights Over Saint Anthony's Body

They could not. Before anyone outside the house had heard, groups of children went running through the city. They were shouting, “The holy father is dead! Saint Anthony is dead!” The news ran at once into every corner of Padua and filled the ears of all the people.75

Then the city fought over him. Saint Anthony had chosen to be buried at the friars' church of Saint Mary in Padua. But the men of the district of Capo di Ponte ran to the house with a crowd of armed men. They would not let the friars touch the body. The friars went to the bishop. He ordered the Podesta, the governor of the city, to help them. The men of Capo di Ponte would not give way even to him. The Poor Ladies had not been able to keep Saint Anthony while he lived. Now they sent secret messengers to the leaders of the city, asking to keep his body.76

At midnight the crowd tried three times to break into the cell. They were struck dazed and blind. The doors stood open, and still they could not get in. Next day people from the villages pressed in with belts, necklaces, and rings to be touched to the body. The June heat was rising, so the friars laid the body in a wooden chest in a shallow grave. At once a rumour spread that it had been stolen. The people came running with swords and clubs, and they did not stop until they had seen for themselves.77

The head of the friars arrived that evening. Next morning the Podesta set a fine of a hundred pounds on anyone who used violence. On the fourth day the bishop ruled for the friars, because Saint Anthony had chosen his grave. The Podesta had a bridge of boats built across the river, so that the body would not have to pass through Capo di Ponte at all. The men of Capo di Ponte smashed it, and people on both sides took up arms.78

Both the friars and the Poor Ladies were afraid. They called on God to calm the storm. God himself quieted the tumult and changed the hearts of the people. The Podesta called the council together and kept the ringleaders apart in another part of the city for the day. God had let the storm come first, so that the light of grace afterward would shine all the brighter.79

On the fifth day the bishop and his clergy came to the cell in procession. The Podesta came with the nobles and the knights, and a crowd beyond counting came with them. It was Tuesday, 17 June. They lifted the body from the grave, and noblemen took turns to carry it. With hymns and a great glow of lights they brought him through the city to Saint Mary's. There the bishop said Mass, and they buried him. He had been dead five days in the summer heat. Yet there was no smell of death about him, only a sweet scent, as if to draw others to follow in his steps. Rigauld says the crowds had run through the streets not for a coffin but for “a pearl of great price”. As a small child he had begun his lessons in a church of the Virgin. Now he was laid to rest in another.80

Miracles began at his tomb that same day. The sick who could touch it were healed at once. Those who could not get near it for the crowd were healed outside, in front of everyone. The first to come were the men and women of Capo di Ponte, who had fought for him with swords. They came barefoot and threw themselves down at the grave. There they confessed their fault with such sorrow that all who saw them were moved.81

After them the whole city came in processions. The bishop came with his clergy and the religious houses. The teachers came with their students, and the trades came in their companies. Many walked barefoot. The candles they carried were so tall that they had to be cut down before they could stand inside the church. Some were decorated with lilies, grapes, and leaves made of wax. Some had to be left burning outside in the square. Day and night the people came, through heat and cold. Soon pilgrims were arriving from as far away as Hungary and Germany.82

Padua wanted the whole Church to honour him, and soon. Before a month had passed, the city sent envoys to the pope. The students of the university wrote their own letter as eyewitnesses. Pope Gregory IX had the miracles examined by the bishop of Padua and the priors of two religious houses. Witnesses came forward, and the proven miracles were written down. Two cardinals who were then in the region wrote to the pope as well. Some at the papal court objected that a whole year had not yet gone by since his death. In the end they all gave their consent.83

The papal court was staying at Spoleto, a hill town in central Italy. There the pope stood in the cathedral on the feast of Pentecost, 30 May 1232, and heard the approved miracles read aloud. Nineteen lame people had walked, and six blind people had seen. Two of the dead had been raised. Then the pope stretched out his hands to heaven. He called on the name of the Holy Trinity and wrote the friar from Portugal among the saints.84

At Pentecost in Assisi eleven years before, no one had asked for him. Now the words that the canon had spoken in bitterness at Coimbra had come true.85

Why the Church Remembers Saint Anthony

Pope Gregory IX set the feast of Saint Anthony on 13 June, the day of his death, and Padua kept it for the first time two weeks later. In his letter for the canonization, the pope wrote that he had known the man himself. In Lisbon an old tradition held that Saint Anthony's mother was still alive then. She lived to see her son honoured on the altars of the Church before she joined him in heaven.86

Then there was Tiso of Camposampiero, the nobleman who had built the cell in the walnut tree. He had been a soldier. Stirred by Saint Anthony's sermons, he gave up soldiering and the honours of the world and put himself and all he had in Saint Anthony's hands. He lived as a lay follower of Saint Francis until he died on the last day of January 1234.87

In 1232 the friars began a great church over Saint Anthony's tomb. His written sermons cover the Sundays of the year, the feasts of the saints, and Lent. They are long outlines packed with texts of Scripture rather than finished speeches. In 1946 Pope Pius XII declared him a Doctor of the Universal Church. The pope dated his letter 16 January, the day the friars keep the feast of the five martyrs of Morocco. Those were the men whose deaths had first made Saint Anthony long to die for Christ.88

The favour most people ask of him is a small one: to find what they have lost. Many trace the custom to a story about a book of his own. While he was teaching at Montpellier, a novice stole his book of notes on the Psalms and ran away with it. Saint Anthony prayed earnestly to have it back. On a bridge the runaway met a man with a savage face and a drawn sword, who ordered him to return the book. The novice turned back. He threw himself at Saint Anthony's feet with many tears and gave the book back. He received far more than the pardon he begged for. By Saint Anthony's prayers he stayed in the Order to the end of his life.89

The people of Padua gave him a day of his own. His body had been carried into the city on a Tuesday, and from then on Tuesday was his day. In time the custom grew of praying to him on nine Tuesdays in a row. The friar Julian of Speyer wrote the prayers and hymns for his feast in 1233, two years after his death. One of them begins “Si quaeris miracula”, which means “If you seek miracles”. It sings that the sea and chains give way, and that young and old ask for lost things and receive them. Every Tuesday the friars of Padua still sing it in his basilica.90

Saint Bonaventure Opens Saint Anthony's Tomb

In 1263 the new church was ready to receive him. Saint Bonaventure was then the head of the whole Order. He came to Padua to move the body to the high altar and to stir up the people's devotion. When the shrine was opened, the flesh had turned to dust and the bones lay loose. But the skin of the head, the hair, and the teeth were whole, and so was the tongue. It was as fresh and red as if he had only just been buried. This was the tongue “which had spoken in every place the word of truth”. Saint Bonaventure took it out with reverence and showed it with great joy to all who were there. Then he kissed it and spoke to it:91

“O blessed tongue, you always blessed the Lord and made others bless him. Now it is plain how highly God valued you.”

— Saint Bonaventure, Padua, 126392

He ordered it placed in a precious case of its own. It is still kept in Padua, in Saint Anthony's basilica.93

A Note on the Sources

The early Life quoted here is the Latin Life that the Bollandists printed in the Acta Sanctorum for 13 June. The 1904 editor of Rigauld took it for the Life by Julian of Speyer, written before about 1240. According to the Messenger of Saint Anthony, Julian built on the first Life of 1232. A friar of Padua wrote that first Life, and the bishop of Lisbon helped him with the early years. The notes give the early Life's paragraph numbers and the pages of the 1867 reprint. The Bollandists set later material from Surius and Wadding after paragraph 12, so the pages jump. Jean Rigauld wrote between about 1293 and 1300 and says that his accounts came from friars who had known Saint Anthony. He names Friar Monaldo as the eyewitness at Arles, and he heard the rain story from friars who had been in the crowd. Saint Anthony himself told of the night attack in Lent, among friends. Bartholomew of Trent, who wrote about 1240 and says he saw and knew him, also tells of his wish to win a martyr's crown among the Saracens. Pope Pius XII's letter of 1946 says that Saint Anthony came to Assisi and recognised Saint Francis there with great joy. The two early Lives do not tell of a meeting. The letter of Saint Francis on theology and the story of the stolen book are first found in a book of the friars' miracles from about 1367. Scholars are divided on whether the letter is his. Pope Pius XII quotes another form of it, in which Saint Francis calls him “my bishop”. The years 1195, 1210, and 1221 are Bollandist estimates, and so is about 1226 for the Arles chapter. The eight years at Coimbra rest on the Chronicle of 1883, and the years 1222 and 1224 come from Woodcock. Tiso's later life comes from Scardeone, a Paduan writer of the 1500s quoted by the Bollandists. The basilica credits the responsory “Si quaeris” to Julian of Speyer, and the 1883 Chronicle gave it to Saint Bonaventure. The sermon on the rich man and Lazarus was checked in the Paris printing of 1521. Woodcock thought Saint Anthony could not have preached the written sermons just as they stand, and that most likely he wrote them from memory. For the dove passage we have only Woodcock's English of 1911.

How This Life Was Researched

Three researchers drew on more than twenty sources in Latin and English. Among them are two early Lives, one written in the 1230s and one written about 1300 from the memories of friars who had known Saint Anthony. Others are his own Lenten sermons in the Paris printing of 1521 and the Chronicle of Saint Anthony of Padua in its English edition of 1883. An English Life of 1911 and the papal letter of 1946 are among them too. For this version the writer went back to the saved texts of the Latin Life, Rigauld's Life, the Chronicle, and the 1911 Life, and checked each scene against them. The research marked several details as missing from the usual English accounts. Among them are the night attack in Lent 1231, his heavy build, and the giant candles of the first processions.
