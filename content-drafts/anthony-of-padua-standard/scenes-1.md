# Anthony of Padua: scene cards, group 1 (SC-01 to SC-05)

**Sources read in this session** (keys follow the outline; all public-domain):
- **W-S1** = Vita auctore anonymo valde antiquo (Julian of Speyer, per the 1904 editor), Latin, in Acta Sanctorum, June vol. 23 (Paris/Rome 1867), 13 June, pp.198-200. Read from the archive.org OCR, `archive:actasanctorum23unse`. The OCR has scan errors. Where a quotation below differs from the raw OCR, I corrected only clear letter errors, and I list them in each card.
- **W-S2** = Jean Rigauld, Life of Blessed Antony, English, CTS 1904 (ed. Andrew Egan), pp.31-44. Read from `archive:LifeOfStAntonyOfPadua`. Copied exactly. The Latin original of Rigauld is not online, so each plain line below is my own modern English of the 1904 text, checked against Julian's Latin where Julian has the same passage.
- The Assidua (Vita prima) in Latin is not in the sources I could reach. Everything "per the Assidua" in the outline therefore comes only through Julian and Rigauld.

## SC-01 — Leaving home, about 1210-1212
- Status: full
- When and where: The Bollandists put his arrival at St Vincent's in 1210 and call this an estimate [W-S1 note e p.200]. St Vincent's is a house of Augustinian canons just outside Lisbon. He then moves to Holy Cross (Santa Cruz) at Coimbra, another house of the same Order.
- Who was there: his "dear friends and relatives" (visitors, unnamed); his Superior at St Vincent's (unnamed). Julian names no one.
- What happened, in order:
  1. After a boyhood in the church of the Virgin (the cathedral, where the body of St Vincent lies), he goes to the Augustinian house near Lisbon and takes the habit [W-S1 p.198; W-S2 pp.31-33].
  2. Rigauld adds that he left his father and mother and gave up all right to inherit from them [W-S2 p.33].
  3. The frequent visits of relatives and friends disturb his peace of mind [W-S1 p.198; W-S2 p.34].
  4. After "nearly two years" he moves to Holy Cross, Coimbra [W-S1 p.198].
  5. His Superior gives leave only with difficulty [W-S1 p.198; W-S2 p.34].
  6. At Coimbra he studies the Fathers; his memory serves him "in the place of books" [W-S2 p.34; W-S1 p.198].
- Words spoken: none in the sources.
- Inner life: his wish is for "peace of soul" and quiet of mind. Julian: "ad optatam mentis quietudinem obtinendam" (to gain the peace of mind he wanted). Rigauld: he wished, "like another Abraham", to leave his country and kindred [W-S2 p.34].
- Testimony (Julian, W-S1 p.198, Latin, public-domain):
  "Ubi dum pacem pectoris ejus importuna carorum amicorumque frequentia perturbaret; peractis ibidem ferme duobus annis, ad sanctae Crucis de Conimbria, aliud scilicet ejusdem Ordinis monasterium transvolavit; ad quod tamen, ob morum ipsius gravitatem, vix licentiam sui Superioris obtinuit."
  (OCR corrected: "carorum amicorura" to "carorum amicorumque"; "feriiie" to "ferme"; "sanctse" to "sanctae".)
  plain: "But the constant coming of dear friends disturbed the peace of his heart. After nearly two years there, he flew off to Holy Cross at Coimbra, another house of the same Order. Because of the seriousness of his character, he hardly got leave from his Superior to go."
  Julian then says the move brought him such progress that it could not be blamed on a restless heart: "ut non multo post vir Dei sapientiae spiritu plenus esset" (so that soon the man of God was full of the spirit of wisdom).
  Rigauld, W-S2 p.34: "But, because the frequent visits of his relations and friends hindered him from obtaining the peace and calm of soul which he so much desired, he wished, like another Abraham, to go forth out of his country and from his kindred. It was not without difficulty that he obtained permission from his Superior, for he was beloved by all on account of his great sanctity and amiability."
  plain: He wanted a calm soul, and his visitors stopped it. So he asked to go far away, as Abraham did. His Superior agreed with difficulty, because everyone loved him.
- What changed: he leaves home and family for a quieter house; the move that lets him study.
- Not in the sources: his age on entering (no source gives it; the Bollandists give 1195 for birth and 1210 for arrival, both estimates). No scene of a farewell, no named visitor, no word of what his family said. No reason for the move beyond the visits. No tears. Julian's phrase about "gravity of character" could mean the Superior hesitated because Fernando was serious and valued; Rigauld reads it as "beloved by all". The sources do not settle which.
- Sources: W-S1 p.198 and note e; W-S2 pp.31-34.

## SC-02 — Coimbra, 1220
- Status: full
- When and where: 1220 (Bollandists, from the date of the martyrdom) [W-S1 note g p.200]. Holy Cross at Coimbra, at the monastery door; the friars' hermitage near Coimbra is called St Antony (later Olivais) [W-S1 p.198 and note l].
- Who was there: Fernando; "some poor Friars Minor" from near Coimbra (Rigauld) [W-S2 p.36]; one of the canons who loved him; the Infante Don Pedro (Julian: "Petrus Infans") who brings the relics.
- What happened, in order:
  1. Friars have been killed at Morocco. The Bollandists name five: Berard, Otto, Peter, Adjutus, Accursius, killed 1220 [W-S1 note g p.200].
  2. The Infante Pedro carries their relics from Morocco. He tells the story of their passion and says their merits saved him from great dangers [W-S1 p.198; W-S2 pp.35-36].
  3. The news strikes Fernando "like a war-horse ready for the day of battle, or an elephant excited by the sight of blood". He decides to take the habit of those who died, and to follow their life, to reach the same crown [W-S2 p.36]. Julian has only the elephant.
  4. One day the poor friars come to the monastery to beg alms. Fernando takes them aside, opens his heart, and asks to be received [W-S1 p.198; W-S2 pp.36-37].
  5. They are glad and set a day. He gets his Prelate's leave "etsi difficulter" (though with difficulty). They come back on the day and put the habit on him in the monastery [W-S1 p.198; W-S2 p.37].
  6. As he leaves with the friars, a canon, who says he grieves more than the others, speaks in bitterness of heart. Fernando answers [W-S1 p.198; W-S2 p.38].
  7. He reaches the friars' place, which is called St Antony, and asks to be called Antony from then on. His reason (Julian): so that "pious caution" would mislead those who sought him, and he could turn away their pressing visits more easily under an unknown name. Rigauld agrees: he hoped by an unknown name to escape the frequent visits, and he also gave up a name common among the sons of kings and nobles in that country [W-S1 p.198; W-S2 p.40].
- Details: his new habit is "shaped like a cross", with a cord at the waist (Rigauld's own comment, W-S2 p.37).
- Words spoken:
  - Fernando, on the martyrs, W-S2 p.36: "I will take their habit," he said, "and so will I, too, go to the fight." plain: I will wear their habit, and I will go into the fight as they did. (Rigauld frames it as a resolve; it is not said to anyone in particular.)
  - The canon, Julian Latin: "Vade, inquit, vade, quia forsitan Sanctus eris." plain: Go, go; maybe you will become a saint.
  - Fernando, Julian: the OCR reads "Cura Sanctiim laulieris, Deum utique colluu- dabis"; I read it as "Cum Sanctum audieris, Deum utique collaudabis" (a reconstruction; check against another copy before quoting the Latin). plain: When you hear that I am a saint, you will surely praise God together with me.
  - Rigauld, W-S2 p.38: "Go, I tell you, go. You will perhaps become a saint." Fernando: "When you hear that I am a saint, surely you will give thanks to God."
- Inner life: Julian, W-S1 p.198: he turns "the wrong done to Christ and the death of the Martyrs" into compassion in himself and thinks he does nothing unless he too faces the tyrant's fierceness and wins the same palm.
- Testimony (Rigauld, W-S2 pp.36-37, English, public-domain):
  "One day, while the servant of God was revolving in his mind how he could best carry out his project, some poor Friars Minor, who had settled in the neighbourhood of Coimbra, came to the monastery to beg alms for Christ's poor. When he saw them he could contain himself no longer ; he took them aside, revealed to them the longing of his heart, and entreated them most humbly to receive him as a Friar into their Order."
  plain: One day he was thinking how to carry out his plan. Then some poor friars who lived near Coimbra came to the monastery to beg alms. When he saw them, he could hold back no longer. He took them aside, told them what he longed for, and asked them very humbly to take him into their Order.
- What changed: he is a friar, called Antony, and he now has leave to seek martyrdom.
- Not in the sources: the number of begging friars. The outline notes and the first notes say "five poor friars". That is wrong. Julian's OCR "Cuinque Fratres" is a scan of "Cumque Fratres" (and when the friars), and Rigauld says "some". The five are the five martyrs. No sermon, no procession scene, no crowd at the relics, no weather. No account of the canon's face or voice beyond "bitterness of heart". Fernando's "habit" words are not said to the begging friars.
- Sources: W-S1 p.198, notes g, h, l p.200; W-S2 pp.35-40.

## SC-03 — Morocco and the storm, 1220-1221
- Status: thin (the sources are short; they give no days, ports, or names)
- When and where: after the habit; Morocco ("terra Saracenorum"); the sea; Sicily.
- Who was there: Antony alone is named. No companion appears in the two early Lives.
- What happened, in order:
  1. He asks for and obtains leave to go among the infidels and preach, offering himself as a victim [W-S2 p.41; W-S1 p.198].
  2. He crosses to the Saracens' land [W-S1 p.198 "ad terram Saracenorum transivit"]. Rigauld: "He arrived, indeed, among the Saracens, but his efforts to obtain the much coveted palm of martyrdom were vain" [W-S2 p.41].
  3. "Gravi nimis et diutino languore correptus": a very heavy and long illness; nothing he wanted could be done [W-S1 p.198]. Rigauld: "Brought down by a long and painful illness, he understood that he was not to succeed" [W-S2 p.41].
  4. By necessity he decides to return to Christian lands, and takes ship for Spain [W-S1 p.198-199; W-S2 p.41].
  5. "Ventis non secundis flantibus": with unfavourable winds he lands in Sicily and sees he is cheated of his plan [W-S1 p.199].
  6. The General Chapter at Assisi is soon to meet. When the friars tell him, he goes, weak and ill [W-S1 p.199; W-S2 pp.41-42].
- Testimony (Julian, W-S1 p.198-199, Latin, public-domain):
  "Nam gravi nimis et diutino languore correptus, nihil secum pro voto prosperum agi perspexit; donec ipsa necessitate compulsus, ad partes fidelium remeare disposuit. Cumque navigando ad redeundum in Hispaniam iter arriperet, contigit ut in partes Siciliae ventis non secundis flantibus applicaret, et sic penitus a proposito se fraudatum conspiceret."
  (OCR corrected: "secura" to "secum", "prosperura" to "prosperum", "sic" to "et sic" retained as printed.)
  plain: A very bad and long illness seized him. He saw that nothing he wished for was going well. In the end the need forced him to decide to go back to the lands of the faithful. As he sailed for Spain, it happened that unfavourable winds drove him to Sicily. There he saw that his plan had been taken from him completely.
- Words spoken: none. Julian gives no spoken word.
- Inner life: Rigauld says God "decided otherwise" and meant him for greater things: "the eternal wisdom of Christ ... disposed otherwise: he was intended and reserved for greater things" [W-S2 p.41]. Julian: "de quo Rex regnantium Dominus aliud a sensu humano decrevit" (the King of kings decided otherwise than human sense) [W-S1 p.198]. These are the authors' comments, not Antony's words.
- What changed: the plan to die for Christ fails; he is left sick on the wrong shore with a chapter to attend.
- Not in the sources: how long the illness lasted ("diutinus" only), what the illness was, where he stayed, any day or port in Morocco, any preaching, any meeting with Moroccans or with a ship's crew, how long the voyage took. Where in Sicily: Julian says only "partes Siciliae". The Bollandists note Wadding gives Tauromenium (Taormina) and Cornegius gives Messina; both are late and uncertain [W-S1 note m p.200]. Julian does not say that someone took him in. No storm scene: Julian says "winds not favourable" and Rigauld "contrary winds". The Bollandists also report Cornegius's claim that Francis appeared to him in Lisbon; that is late, one line only [W-S1 note i p.200].
- Sources: W-S1 pp.198-199 and notes i, m; W-S2 pp.41-42. Bartholomew of Trent (W-S1 p.196) was not re-read, the notes give it.

## SC-04 — Pentecost chapter, 1221 (Assisi)
- Status: full
- When and where: the General Chapter at Assisi. The Bollandists give Pentecost, 30 May 1221 (an estimate), with Brother Elias as vicar for the General [W-S1 note n p.200].
- Who was there: Antony; Brother Gratian, who "then ruled the friars of Romagna" (Julian: "Fratribus Romaniolae praeerat"); the friars of many provinces. The Bollandist note says nothing puts Francis in the room.
- What happened, in order:
  1. Antony hears of the Chapter and goes, though he is "debilis et infirmus" (weak and ill) [W-S1 p.199; W-S2 p.42].
  2. The Chapter ends; the friars go off to their different places [W-S1 p.199].
  3. "Solus Antonius a nullo petebatur", because he was unknown, so he seemed useless [W-S1 p.199].
  4. With no word of learning or any use he could offer, he goes to Gratian and asks humbly that Gratian ask the Minister General for him, take him in, and train him in the discipline of the Rule [W-S1 p.199; W-S2 p.42].
  5. Gratian "benigne" (kindly) receives him and takes him to Romagna [W-S1 p.199]. Rigauld adds "with the permission of the Father-General" [W-S2 p.42].
- Words spoken: none quoted. Julian gives Antony's request only in reported speech.
- Testimony (Julian, W-S1 p.199, Latin, public-domain):
  "Soluto igitur ex more Capitulo, Fratribusque ad sua circumquaque loca dimissis; solus Antonius a nullo petebatur; quia, sicut erat ignotus, ita et inutilis videbatur. Nulla ergo de se vel litteraturae, vel cujuscumque alterius utilitatis habita mentione, ad Fratrem Gratianum, qui tunc Fratribus Romaniolae praeerat, devotus accessit; rogans humiliter, ut ipsum a Ministro generali petitum colligeret, collectumque disciplinis regularibus erudiret."
  (OCR corrected: "circumquaque" for "circuraquaque", "dimissis" for "diraissis", "Romaniolae" from "Romanio- Ise", "disciplinis" from "discipiinis".)
  plain: When the Chapter ended as usual and the friars were sent off to their places everywhere, only Antony was asked for by no one, for he was as unknown as he seemed useless. He said nothing of his learning or any other use, and went humbly to Brother Gratian, who then led the friars of Romagna. He begged Gratian to ask the Minister General for him, take him in, and train him in the discipline of the Rule.
  The one-line "plain" of the hook: "Only Antony was asked for by no one."
- What changed: Gratian takes him; he goes to Romagna.
- Not in the sources: Gratian gives no recorded words; Julian says only that he took him "kindly". No scene of Antony waiting, standing, or watching others leave. No meeting with Francis (no source says Antony met him; the Bollandists say Francis was in the Order but do not say he was present). No crowd count, no weather, no mention of the sick man's appearance beyond "weak and ill". Julian does not say the number of friars or that they were "all sent" by a minister's list; that is the outline's wording. "Ignotus" and "inutilis" are Julian's, so these are the author's judgment of why, not Antony's thought.
- Sources: W-S1 pp.198-199 and note n p.200; W-S2 pp.41-42.

## SC-05 — The hermitage of Monte Paolo, 1221-1222
- Status: full
- When and where: after the Chapter; the hermitage of Monte Paolo (Julian: "eremum Montis sancti Pauli"), in Romagna, near Forlì (the Bollandists, note p). A cell in a crypt.
- Who was there: Antony; Brother Gratian, who sends him; one friar who had made the cell ready for himself and gave it up (Julian, W-S1 p.199); "those who were there" as witnesses of his weakness. No names.
- What happened, in order:
  1. Antony asks Gratian for a place of solitude. Gratian sends him to the hermitage [W-S1 p.199; W-S2 p.42]. Rigauld: Gratian hoped he might give himself to silence, prayer, devotion, and study and "lay up a store of choice seeds to sow broadcast, hereafter, by his preaching" [W-S2 p.42].
  2. He finds a long-wished-for cell in a crypt, set apart from the friars and fit for prayer. He gets it from a friar who had prepared it for himself [W-S1 p.199].
  3. He lives a solitary life "as far as was allowed", strengthens himself with holy meditation against temptation, keeps night vigils alone in prayer, and is fed on bread and water [W-S1 p.199].
  4. He so wears his body down that, "according to those who were there", at the hour of the collation, when he was to go back to the friars, his step wavered and he could not hold himself up [W-S1 p.199].
  5. For a long time he lives "simplicem inter simplices" (a simple man among simple men), and hides the light of grace "sub indocti specie" (under the look of an unlearned man) [W-S1 p.199].
  6. Later, by God's arrangement, his fame grows "in the community of the simple" [W-S1 p.199].
- Details: crypt cell; bread and water; night vigils; the weakness. Pots and feet: Rigauld says of his humility in general (not tied to Monte Paolo) that "he washed the plates and other kitchen utensils, and also the feet of the Friars which he then devoutly kissed" [W-S2 p.44]. At Forlì, shortly after, Julian says he was better practised in washing kitchen utensils than in explaining the divine words [W-S1 p.200].
- Words spoken: none.
- Inner life: Julian's narration only: "in divino se amore firmavit" (he grounded himself in divine love), "se totum dispositioni divinae commendans, sursum firmissimae spei anchoram contutavit" (committing himself wholly to God's plan, he fixed the anchor of firm hope above). These are the writer's words about him, not Antony's.
- Testimony (Julian, W-S1 p.199, Latin, public-domain):
  "Illic solitariam, quantum licuit, vitam duxit; illic sacris meditationibus contra tentationes spirituum roborans, in divino se amore firmavit: ibi nocturnis vigiliis solus in oratione perstitit; ibi se totum dispositioni divinae commendans, sursum firmissimae spei anchoram contutavit: ibi etiam cibo panis et aquae tanta corpus abstinentia maceravit, ut, testibus iis qui aderant, hora collationis aliquando rediturus ad Fratres, nutante prae nimia debilitate vestigio, semetipsum supportare non posset."
  (OCR corrected: "solitariara" to "solitariam", "tentationes spiritura" to "tentationes spirituum", "firmissimaj" to "firmissimae", "ab^tinentia njace-" to "abstinentia maceravit", "nutante prae nimia", "dispnsitioni" to "dispositioni".)
  plain: There he led a solitary life as far as was allowed. There he grew strong against the temptations of the spirits through holy meditation, and set himself firm in the love of God. There he kept the night watches alone in prayer. There he gave himself wholly to God's plan and held the anchor of a most firm hope above. There too he wore his body down with so much fasting on bread and water that, as those who were present said, when he was about to go back to the friars at the hour of the evening meal, his step wavered from great weakness and he could not hold himself up.
- What changed: his learning stays hidden; he is known as a simple friar until the ordination at Forlì.
- Not in the sources: how he spent each day beyond "meditation, vigils, prayer" and "study" (Rigauld). No book, no Scripture text, no dialogue, no named witness. No scrubbing of pots at Monte Paolo itself: pots and feet are Rigauld's general account of his humility. The crypt's size, light, cold, and how far it lay from the friars are not given. No date: "multo tempore" (a long time) is Julian's only measure. The outline's "1221-1222" is an estimate. "Collationis hora" is the evening meal (collation); the plain text says "evening meal" on that reading, which I took from the usual meaning of the word and not from the source.
- Sources: W-S1 p.199 and note p p.200; W-S2 pp.42-44.
