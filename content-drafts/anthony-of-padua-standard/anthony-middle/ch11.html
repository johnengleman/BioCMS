Why the Church Remembers Saint Anthony

Pope Gregory IX set the feast of Saint Anthony on 13 June, the day of his death, and Padua kept it for the first time two weeks later. In his letter for the canonization, the pope wrote that he had known the man himself. In Lisbon an old tradition held that Saint Anthony's mother was still alive then. She lived to see her son honoured on the altars of the Church before she joined him in heaven.87

Tiso of Camposampiero, the nobleman who had built the cell in the walnut tree, had been a soldier. Stirred by Saint Anthony's sermons, he gave up soldiering and the honours of the world and put himself and all he had in Saint Anthony's hands. He lived as a lay follower of Saint Francis until he died on the last day of January 1234.88

In 1232 the friars began a great church over Saint Anthony's tomb. His written sermons cover the Sundays of the year, the feasts of the saints, and Lent. They are long outlines packed with texts of Scripture rather than finished speeches. In 1946 Pope Pius XII declared him a Doctor of the Universal Church. The pope dated his letter 16 January, the day the friars keep the feast of the five martyrs of Morocco. Those were the men whose deaths had first made Saint Anthony long to die for Christ.89

The favour most people ask of him is a small one: to find what they have lost. Many trace the custom to a story about a book of his own. While he was teaching at Montpellier, a novice stole his book of notes on the Psalms and ran away with it. Saint Anthony prayed earnestly to have it back. On a bridge the runaway met a man with a savage face and a drawn sword, who ordered him to return the book. The novice turned back, threw himself at Saint Anthony's feet with many tears, and gave the book back. He received far more than the pardon he begged for. By Saint Anthony's prayers he stayed in the Order, and he died a holy man.91

The people of Padua gave him a day of his own. His body had been carried into the city on a Tuesday, and from then on Tuesday was his day. In time the custom grew of praying to him on nine Tuesdays in a row. In 1233, two years after his death, the friar Julian of Speyer wrote the prayers and hymns for his feast. One of them begins “Si quaeris miracula”, which means “If you seek miracles”. It sings that the sea and chains give way, and that young and old ask for lost things and receive them. Every Tuesday the friars of Padua still sing it in his basilica.90
