# Fact sheet: Saint Anthony of Padua (checked 2026-10-06, heart draft of the biography)

The teachings essay and the miracle list must use these names, dates, numbers, and wordings exactly. "n." means the note number in the current `biography.html` (93 notes). Where a later writer finds a source that disagrees, list the conflict for the checker. Julian page numbers are plus or minus one page (OCR order); the paragraph numbers are firm.

## Names and titles

- **Saint Anthony of Padua.** Baptized **Fernando** (Latin "Fernandus"; Rigauld "Ferdinand"). Took the name Anthony at the friars' place near Coimbra called Saint Anthony (n. 15; Early Life n. 4; Acta p. 200 note l: later Olivais). Called "of Lisbon" and "of Padua" (Pius XII greets both: "Rejoice, happy Portugal; O happy Padua, rejoice"; this greeting is no longer in the biography). Never "Anthony the Great". Always "Saint Anthony" in our own sentences; inside a quotation the source's own words stand.
- Born Lisbon, about **1195** (a Bollandist estimate, from "thirty-six years" at death); parents near the cathedral (n. 4). He was their first son (Rigauld p. 31). The Latin says "venerable" parents; "just before God" is Rigauld. Never give a father's name or rank from the Latin notes (Martim de Bulhões is in the Bollandist note b; it is not used).
- Augustinian canons: the house of Saint Vincent near Lisbon, about **1210** (an estimate), nearly two years; then Holy Cross at Coimbra, **eight years** (Coleridge p. 29; not in Julian or Rigauld). Orders: the canons are "Canons Regular of Saint Augustine".
- **The five martyrs of Morocco**, 1220: Berard, Otto, Peter, Adjutus, Accursius (Acta p. 199 note g). The text says "five" and names none. Prince **Pedro** (the Infante Don Pedro; Latin "Petrus Infans") brought their relics home. He was "a great prince", not "the king's son".
- **Brother Gratian**, minister of the friars of Romagna (Rigauld p. 42; Julian n. 5). **Monte Paolo**, a hermitage near Forlì (Woodcock p. 20; Coleridge). No source says what became of Gratian.
- Forlì, ordination: about **1222** (Woodcock p. 28). The "Minister of the place" is unnamed. The Dominicans present are unnamed.
- **Monaldo**: the friar who saw Saint Francis at Arles (Rigauld p. 66; Coleridge p. 81). One person seated saw it (Julian n. 12 "cujusdam").
- **Tiso** of Camposampiero, a nobleman (Julian n. 29). Camposampiero = "Campo San Pietro", the Field of Saint Peter, about ten miles north of Padua (note f, p. 206). Died the last day of January **1234** (Scardeone, Acta note g). Plain name, no title.
- **The cardinal bishop of Ostia** (Hugolino was Gregory IX; the Ostia cardinal of 1230 is a Bollandist puzzle). Say "the cardinal bishop of Ostia"; never name him.
- Popes: **Gregory IX** (knew him; canonized him; named him "Arca Testamenti"); **Pius XII** (Doctor, 16 January 1946, *Exulta, Lusitania felix*). Plain titles: "Pope Gregory IX", "Pope Pius XII".
- **Saint Bonaventure**, head of the whole Order in 1263; opened the tomb. **Saint Francis**, **Saint Clare** (the Poor Ladies of Arcella are her nuns), **Saint Dominic** (his Order of Preachers), **Saint Vincent** (martyr; his body in the Lisbon cathedral).
- **Julian of Speyer**: the Latin Life ("Vita auctore anonymo valde antiquo") is his in the 1904 Rigauld editor's view, written before about 1240; the responsory *Si quaeris miracula* is his by the basilica's account (1233). Say "the early Life". Coleridge gave *Si quaeris* to Saint Bonaventure (a conflict, stated in the Note on the Sources).
- **Jean Rigauld**, Franciscan, later bishop of Tréguier; wrote between about 1293 and 1300 (Rigauld Intro).
- **Capo di Ponte** (Latin "Capite-pontis"; Rigauld "Head of the Bridge"): the district. **The Podesta**: the governor of the city. Padua; **Arcella**: the small friary of the friars who served the Poor Ladies, just outside the walls. The friars' church: **Saint Mary** in Padua.
- Places: Lisbon, Coimbra (Holy Cross), Morocco, Sicily (the port is uncertain, note m), Assisi, Forlì, Monte Paolo, Rimini, Limoges, Brive, Bourges, Arles, Montpellier, Bologna, Padua, Camposampiero, Arcella, Spoleto.

## Dates and numbers

- Pentecost **30 May 1221**, the general chapter at Assisi (Bollandist estimate, note n; Woodcock p. 18 agrees: it opened 30 May). He had come from Africa by way of Sicily.
- Death: **Friday 13 June 1231** at Arcella; about **thirty-six** years old (Julian n. 31; Acta note c). The children cried "The holy father is dead! Saint Anthony is dead!" (n. 75). Buried **Tuesday 17 June 1231**, the fifth day (Julian n. 37: "sub die transitus sui quinto"; Coleridge p. 191: a Tuesday; basilica page: Tuesday 17 June).
- Canonized **Pentecost, 30 May 1232**, Spoleto cathedral, by Gregory IX; less than a year after death. Feast fixed on **13 June**. Lameness: **nineteen** lame made to walk, **six** blind, **two** dead raised (Julian n. 43). Padua kept the first feast two weeks later (Coleridge p. 195).
- Doctor of the Church: **16 January 1946** (Pius XII). Title "Evangelical Doctor". The date is the feast of the Franciscan protomartyrs.
- Basilica begun **1232**; body moved to the high altar **1263** (Saint Bonaventure); the tongue found whole.
- Arles chapter: "about 1226" (Bollandists: 1226 or a little earlier). Saint Francis died **1226** (Woodcock p. 28; not an estimate). Coleridge puts the chapter in autumn 1226, three weeks before Saint Francis died. The text says only "about 1226".
- General chapter at Assisi, **1230** (Saint Francis's body moved; he was freed from all office, Julian n. 26; Rigauld p. 73). Nine years after the first.
- France: "about 1224" (Woodcock p. 28: "almost certain" in 1224; p. 33 "end of 1223 or early 1224").
- Padua, winter **1230–31**: Lent began **5 February 1231** (Ash Wednesday, note c); law of **15 March 1231**; Easter **23 March 1231** (eight days after the law). The night attack: "near the start of Lent" (Julian n. 26), a night he told of later among friends.
- Interest rates in Padua: **28** in every 100 a year, **50 or 60** not rare (Woodcock p. 70). The law: a citizen who gave up all his goods could no longer be kept in prison for life or exiled for debt; "at the request of Blessed Antony" (Woodcock p. 71).
- Crowds: "thirty thousand or more" (Julian n. 27). Candles: so tall they were cut down to stand inside the church; "sixteen men to carry one" is Coleridge p. 190 and is not used.
- Robber: a gang of **twelve**; **twelve** pilgrimages to the tombs of the Apostles; the old man told it about **1292** (Rigauld p. 74).
- Camposampiero: "about ten miles north of Padua" (note f). The cell: in a **walnut tree** whose branches spread from one trunk (Julian n. 29). Rigauld alone says it stood on a height to be nearer heaven.
- Fine on violence: **a hundred pounds** (Julian n. 35). The fourth day, the bishop's ruling; the fifth day, the burial.
- Tiso died 31 January 1234. "Eleven years" from Pentecost 1221 to Pentecost 1232.
- "How This Life Was Researched": **three** researchers; **more than twenty** sources in **Latin and English**. Eight years at Coimbra rest on Coleridge; 1222 and 1224 on Woodcock.

## Approved wording of quotations (plain modern English; our translation or modernization)

1. The opening line (n. 1; Julian n. 5 "solus Antonius a nullo petebatur; quia, sicut erat ignotus, ita et inutilis videbatur"): "Only Anthony was asked for by no one, for he seemed as useless as he was unknown."
2. "at the farthest ends of the earth" (n. 4; Julian n. 1, "in extremis terrae finibus").
3. "zealous lover of the Cross" (n. 7; Rigauld p. 34). "his memory served him in the place of books" (n. 8; Rigauld p. 34; Julian "ut memoria pro codicibus uteretur").
4. "for love of Christ who died for us" (n. 9; Rigauld p. 35). "like a war-horse ready for the day of battle" (n. 10; Rigauld p. 36). "the cup of suffering" (n. 40; Julian n. 9 "calicem passionis").
5. His resolve (n. 11; Rigauld p. 36; no "said"): "I will take their robe, and I too will go to the fight." Rigauld on him: "a martyr of desire" (n. 11).
6. Rigauld, the friars at the monastery door (block quote, n. 12; pp. 36–37): "One day he was turning over in his mind how best to carry out his plan. Then some poor Friars Minor, who had settled near Coimbra, came to the monastery to beg alms for Christ's poor. When he saw them, he could hold himself back no longer. He took them aside, opened to them the longing of his heart, and begged them most humbly to take him into their Order."
7. The robe (n. 13; Rigauld p. 37): "so ardently desired to be crucified for Jesus Christ".
8. The canon and Saint Anthony (n. 14; Julian n. 4; Rigauld p. 38): "Go, go. Perhaps you will become a saint." / "When you hear that I am a saint, surely you will give thanks to God."
9. "to Christ who died for us on the altar of the cross" (n. 17; Rigauld p. 41). "cheated of his plan completely" (n. 19; Julian n. 5 "penitus a proposito se fraudatum").
10. Monte Paolo (block quote, n. 26; Julian n. 6): "There he led a solitary life, as far as he was allowed. There he strengthened his spirit against temptations through holy meditation, and he set himself firm in the love of God. There he kept the night watches alone in prayer. There he gave himself wholly to God's plan and fixed the anchor of a most firm hope on high. There too he wore his body down with so much fasting on bread and water that sometimes, when he had to go back to the friars at the evening hour, his step wavered from weakness, and those who were there saw that he could not hold himself up." Never "temptations of the spirits".
11. Forlì (block quote, n. 30; Rigauld pp. 45–46; two ellipses): "Anthony would much rather have been a pupil than a teacher. He protested that he was unfit and unprepared. He did all he could to avoid what seemed to him an ambitious act, until the formal command laid on him left him no choice. … He began, in the fear of God, with simple words. But heavenly grace lit his mind, and his memory served him in place of books. As he went on, his words rose higher, and he explained the deep mysteries of Holy Scripture so clearly, and spoke so powerfully, that his hearers were astonished. … They said to one another, 'No one ever spoke like this man.'"
12. "gracious and severe" (n. 38; Julian n. 9 "gratiosus pariter et severus").
13. Saint Francis's letter (n. 34; Kerval p. 259 n. 1; also Acta, Liber Miraculorum n. 20): "To my dearest Brother Anthony, Brother Francis sends greeting in Christ. It pleases me that you teach sacred theology to the brothers, provided that by such study you do not put out the spirit, as the Rule says. Farewell." (Pius XII's form begins "Antonio Episcopo meo": "my bishop".)
14. Poverty and riches (n. 36; Rigauld pp. 52–53): no longer quoted in the biography (the "Foxes have holes" and "thorns" quotations were dropped on 2026-10-07). If a later text uses them, take the wording from Rigauld pp. 52–53 and have it checked. The biography tells only that on the road he carried nothing and God saw to it that he and his companions never went without what they needed (n. 36).
15. The field at night (n. 37; Rigauld pp. 57–58): now reported speech, not a quotation. He calmly told the friars to leave it alone and go on with their prayers, since the enemy only wanted to disturb their rest and break off their prayer. At daylight not a stalk had been touched. Do not quote his words.
16. Bourges (n. 39; Rigauld p. 67): "I speak to you, mitre-bearer!"
17. Arles (n. 45; Rigauld p. 66): "still continued to desire" (the cross).
18. The rain (block quote, n. 42; Rigauld p. 71; Woodcock quotes it too): "Do not be afraid. Do not move, and do not stop listening to the word of God. I trust in the One who never lets us trust in vain, that the rain will not touch you." Never "and the rain will not touch you".
19. Padua crowd (block quote, n. 53; Julian n. 27): "Everyone listened so closely while the servant of the Lord was speaking that among thirty thousand people or more you could hardly hear a murmur or a sound. The stallholders shut their shops and did not dare to sell anything at all until the sermon was over and each person went back to his own work."
20. Lenten sermon, Lazarus (n. 57; Latin, fol. 85): "O how many people today are dressed in purple, in cloth dyed with the sweat and blood of the poor, because they are dressed in robbery, theft, usury, and dishonest gain."
21. The robber (block quote, n. 59; Rigauld pp. 74–75): "I was a robber by trade. I belonged to a gang of twelve; we lived in the forest and robbed everyone who passed. We had heard of Anthony's preaching, but we could not believe what people said about the power of his words. They compared him to a flaming torch and called him a second Elijah. So all twelve of us went in disguise to hear him. … As soon as his burning words reached our ears, we were filled with bitter sorrow for our sins. The good Father heard our confessions one after another, gave each of us a fitting penance, and forbade us ever to go back to our old life. … As for me, he gave me the penance of going twelve times on pilgrimage to the tombs of the Apostles, and I am now on my way back from Rome for the twelfth time."
22. The law (n. 62): "Blessed Anthony" (Woodcock p. 71; the statute's own words, in the quotation).
23. The dove (block quote, n. 67; Woodcock pp. 79–80, English only; Latin not checked): "Be like the dove that makes her nest in the deepest cleft of the rock. This cleft, where the soul should hide itself, is the wound in the side of Jesus Christ. … The wound in his side leads to his Heart. … They are the virtues of our Saviour: humility, gentleness, poverty, patience, and self-denial. The world despises them as useless. But it is with these that we shall build our nest, deep in the Rock, in the Heart of Jesus."
24. Arcella (n. 70; Rigauld p. 79): "exhausted in body but courageous in spirit".
25. The death (block quote, n. 71; Julian n. 31): the full block quote as in the biography, from "After he had made a holy confession and received absolution" to "he died, like one gently falling asleep". Saint Anthony's words: "I see my Lord." / "I have this anointing within me. You do not need to do this for me; even so, it pleases me, and it is good for me."
26. "O gloriosa Domina" means "O glorious Lady" (n. 72).
27. Rigauld (n. 80): the crowds ran "not for a coffin but for 'a pearl of great price'".
28. The tongue (n. 91; Rigauld p. 76): "which had spoken in every place the word of truth". Saint Bonaventure (block quote, n. 92; Coleridge p. 198): "O blessed tongue, you always blessed the Lord and made others bless him. Now it is plain how highly God valued you."
29. Pius XII (n. 88): the biography now gives only the date, 16 January 1946, the feast of the five martyrs of Morocco. The greeting "Rejoice, happy Portugal; O happy Padua, rejoice" and the antiphon "O doctor optime" ("O best of teachers") are no longer in the biography. Si quaeris: "If you seek miracles" (n. 90).
30. Tiso (n. 87; Acta note g): the tomb words are no longer in the biography. He is told as a former soldier, "stirred by Saint Anthony's sermons" (Scardeone "incensus"), who gave up soldiering and the honours of the world, put himself and all he had in Saint Anthony's hands, lived as a lay follower of Saint Francis, and died on the last day of January 1234.

## Disputes, told in the text

- Why his superior at Lisbon let him go only with difficulty: "the seriousness of his character" (Julian n. 2) or "everyone loved him" (Rigauld p. 34). Both are given.
- The Arles chapter is "about 1226"; the Francis vision there is told as the Church tells it (Julian n. 12; Rigauld pp. 65–66).
- The letter of Saint Francis: "the friars have handed down" a letter; whether it is his is in the Note on the Sources.
- The hill: "the early Life says he foresaw the glory"; "Rigauld says he knew he would soon die" (n. 63).
- Not in the text, on purpose: the Infant Jesus in his arms; the fish, the mule, the poisoned dish, and the kneeling horse (all dropped on 2026-10-07; even the Rimini line no longer names the dish and the horse); the Ezzelino story; "perhaps first to teach theology" is "an English biographer thought" (Woodcock pp. 30–31).

## Added by the middle revision (2026-10-07)

- Note numbers renumbered 1–93 (was 95). New notes: 21 (God closed the door to Morocco; Early Life n. 5; Rigauld p. 41) and 40 (the "cup of suffering"; Early Life n. 9).
- Rimini (n. 33): the Patarines taught two powers, one good and one evil (Woodcock p. 25); Rimini was one of their strongholds (Woodcock p. 25); a leader of thirty years in error was led to the truth and kept the Church's commands until death (Early Life n. 10; Rigauld p. 65). Names: not used (Bonovillus, Bononillo, Bonillo vary).
- Saint Francis's death (n. 46): "After the news reached him, Saint Anthony left France and set out again for Italy" (Woodcock pp. 28, 50). No source gives words of his.
- The lost book (n. 89): the novice "gave the book back", and "By Saint Anthony's prayers he stayed in the Order to the end of his life" (Coleridge p. 72 "persevered in religion, and died in the odour of sanctity"; Woodcock p. 34 asks to be received again into the Order). The Coleridge form (a man with a drawn sword on a bridge) is told, not Woodcock's (the devil at a flooded river).
- Brive: "founded their first house" (Rigauld p. 54: "the first Convent of Friars Minor").
- Bourges (n. 39): "I speak to you, mitre-bearer!" is unchanged; the headdress is glossed before the word.
