This list gathers the works God has done through Saint Anthony of Padua, as the Church remembers them. They are healings, visions, and other wonders, from the oldest witnesses to the chronicles of later centuries. Each account names who told it. Canonization is the Church's declaration that a person is a saint. The first group is what the early Latin Life says was read out at his canonization in 1232. That Life was written before 1249 and is credited to Julian of Speyer. A manuscript from Ancona names many of the people healed. It is printed in the Acta Sanctorum. Many other accounts come from that Life and from Jean Rigauld's Life. Rigauld wrote between about 1293 and 1300 after questioning friars who had known Saint Anthony. Others come from a collection of his miracles written about 1367 and from later chronicles. Stories first written down from the 1500s to the 1700s have their own group. Each account is told as its source tells it.

Miracles approved by the Church: the canonization, 1232

Many healings read to the Pope, Spoleto 1232

Within a month of Saint Anthony's death, the people of Padua asked Pope Gregory IX to make him a saint. The Pope had the cures examined by the Bishop of Sabina and by a commission at Padua. The early Latin Life lists the cures read out at Spoleto on 30 May 1232:

  • nineteen lame people healed
  • five people with paralysis and five with hunchbacks healed
  • six blind people, three deaf people, and three mute people healed
  • two people with epilepsy and two with fevers healed
  • two people raised from the dead

When the miracles had been read, the Pope raised his hands to heaven and called on the name of the Holy Trinity. Then he wrote Saint Anthony into the list of the saints.

Status: approved by the Church and read before Pope Gregory IX at the canonization. The commission at Padua had three members: the bishop, the prior of St. Benedict's, and the Dominican prior Giovanni da Vicenza. Rigauld gives the same counts. An 1883 chronicle counts forty-five miracles proved before the canonization, two of them raisings of the dead. The appendix of the Acta Sanctorum prints many of these cases by name from a manuscript at Ancona.

A woman falls into water and stays dry

The early Latin Life tells of a woman who fell into water and called on Saint Anthony. Everyone who pulled her out was soaked. She alone came out dry in her clothes and on her body, saved through Saint Anthony.

The Ancona manuscript tells it in full. The woman came from Monselice and was traveling toward Padua with her husband and other women. He had promised to take her on pilgrimage to Saint James. To cool her joy, he said he had changed his mind. After a long silence she said that she would throw herself into the river in the name of Jesus Christ and Saint Anthony if he did not keep his promise. He did not believe her and called her foolish. She called on the name of Saint Anthony and threw herself headfirst into the water. The women with her ran to the river and pulled her out. Their own clothes were soaked, and they wrung much water out of them. She was found on the bank without a thread of her clothing wet.

The writer of the manuscript says that he counts her act as foolishness. Yet he believes that Saint Anthony could win this for her from God. He knew that Saint Anthony was a friend of simplicity. In his view, Saint Anthony kept the simple woman unharmed in the middle of the waves.

Status: approved by the Church. The early Latin Life lists it with the miracles read out at the canonization. It gives no place or date. Rigauld tells it as well. It is told in full in the Ancona manuscript, which places the woman at Monselice.

Sparrows leave a woman's crop alone

The early Latin Life tells of a woman who was set to guard a crop of millet. She wanted to visit Saint Anthony's tomb. So many sparrows were there that she did not dare to leave. She promised to visit the tomb nine times. After that the sparrows left the millet untouched.

The Ancona manuscript names her Tumita of Trimegone. She longed to visit Saint Anthony's tomb after she heard of his wonders, but she could not go. She had been set to guard her millet from the sparrows until harvest. One day she came to the fence around the millet and made a vow to Saint Anthony. She promised to visit his tomb nine times if he kept the sparrows off the millet. At once the whole flock of birds left the fence in a single swarm. She could not see one sparrow left on the willows around the millet.

Status: approved by the Church. The early Latin Life lists it with the miracles read out at the canonization. It gives no place or date. Her name and village come from the Ancona manuscript.

Shipwrecked men reach harbor after a vow

The early Latin Life tells of men in a shipwreck at sea who had no hope of saving themselves. They made their confession and promised themselves to Saint Anthony. A ray of light went ahead of them where they could see it. It led them quickly to a safe harbor. Rigauld says the friars sing of it in the hymn of the Saint's Office. The hymn calls him a sign of salvation to shipwrecked sailors, whom he guided by a ray of light.

The Ancona manuscript gives the details. About twenty-six men and women boarded one ship at Saint Hilary to sail to Venice. At the hour of night prayer they reached the lagoon near Saint George of Alga. A very great storm rose, and they tried to take shelter. A whirlwind carried them to places they did not know. A darkness fell with wind and rain. It was so thick that they could hardly see one another, and they gave up hope of surviving. They made their confession, and a priest on board gave them absolution. Then they wept and cried out and promised themselves to God and Saint Anthony.

As soon as they made the vow, the storm calmed around the ship. Darkness still lay on them, and no one knew where they were or where to go. Then a light came out of the ship itself and shone. It went ahead of the sailors and passengers, who wept for joy. It led them to the place called Saint Mark the Little near Venice, and they were safe. They had been saved from death through the merits of Saint Anthony. When they arrived, the light that had gone ahead of them vanished.

Status: approved by the Church. The early Latin Life lists it with the miracles read out at the canonization. It gives no place or date. Rigauld tells it as well. It is told in full in the Ancona manuscript.

A Poor Clare is cleansed in this life

The early Latin Life tells of a sister of the Poor Clares. Saint Clare founded this order, the Order of Poor Ladies. The sister was very afraid of the fire of purgatory. Purgatory is the cleansing that the Church teaches comes after death. She asked through Saint Anthony to be cleansed in this life instead. Then very hard sufferings tormented her. At the prayer of the other sisters she was freed from them through Saint Anthony.

The Ancona manuscript tells it at more length. Her name was Oliva, and she was a serving sister of the Poor Ladies. Saint Anthony's body was still unburied when she came and kissed his hands. Among other things she asked that God give her at once every punishment she had earned by her sins and keep nothing to punish later. After her prayer she went back into the monastery. At once great pain tormented her whole body. She could not bear it, and her cries disturbed the other sisters. At night she recovered a little.

The next day she slipped into the dining hall with the other sisters and sat at table. The pains seized her again, so that she could not eat. She rolled about on the table and cried out like a woman in labor. At the abbess's order she was carried to the infirmary. There she prayed again and again to Saint Anthony for relief. Then she remembered that she had a small piece of his tunic hidden away. She had it brought and laid it on her body. At once all her pain left her.

Status: approved by the Church. The early Latin Life lists it with the miracles read out at the canonization. It gives no place or date. It is told in full in the Ancona manuscript, which names the sister.

A boy is healed of a neck swelling

The early Latin Life tells of a boy with a dangerous swelling of the neck. His mother made a vow for him to Saint Anthony, and the boy was healed. Later his mother neglected the vow. The same illness struck him again. The vow was made again and kept. Then he was freed.

Both the Ancona manuscript and the collection of about 1367 name the boy Henry of Padua. His neck had swollen, and he suffered for fifteen days. His mother vowed to carry a wax neck and head to the Saint's tomb. She took him to the tomb, and he was healed as soon as he got home. Then she put off paying the vow. His neck swelled again, and the old pain came back. His mother knew her guilt and was sorry. She sent the promised wax head and neck to the tomb. The pain left him at once, the swelling went down, and in a few days he was fully healed.

Status: approved by the Church. The early Latin Life lists it with the miracles read out at the canonization. It gives no place or date. The Ancona manuscript and the collection of about 1367 name the boy.

A knight's glass does not break

The early Latin Life tells of a knight who had been a heretic from his youth. He sat at a table and heard people tell of Saint Anthony's miracles. He mocked them. Then he threw the glass cup in his hand to the ground from a height. He said, “If Anthony keeps this glass whole, I will believe that he is a saint.” The glass struck stone and stayed whole. The knight gave up his errors at once and began to believe in Christ.

In the Ancona manuscript the knight is named Aleardinus of Salsaterra. It says he had been caught in heresy from his youth. After he heard of Saint Anthony's wonders, he came to Padua one day with his wife and household. At a meal the others spoke of the miracles and said that Saint Anthony was a saint of God. Aleardinus emptied the glass cup in his hand and said, “If this cup stays unbroken, I will admit that everything else you tell me about him is true.” He threw it from the seat where they were dining onto the floor. The glass struck the stone and stayed whole. Many people standing in the street nearby saw it.

Moved to sorrow for his sins, the knight ran to the glass and picked it up. He hurried with it to the friars and told them everything that had been said and done. Then he made a devout confession and accepted the penance they gave him with humble devotion. After that he held faithfully to Christ and preached his power with great firmness.

Status: approved by the Church. The early Latin Life lists it with the miracles read out at the canonization. Rigauld tells it about 1300 and says the friars sing of it in a hymn of the Saint's Office. The Basilica of Saint Anthony tells it of a knight named Aleardino da Salvaterra at Padua. In that telling the tiles broke instead of the glass. The knight's name and the scene in full come from the Ancona manuscript.

A cleric who mocked the inquiry is healed

The early Latin Life tells of a cleric who laughed at the inquiry into the miracles. A very grave illness struck him. He made a vow to Saint Anthony and was healed. Taught by his own experience, he became an open witness to Saint Anthony's holiness.

In the Ancona manuscript the cleric is named Guidoctus of Anguillara. He laughed in secret at the witnesses who were giving evidence about Saint Anthony's miracles in the Bishop of Padua's chamber. That night a very strong pain seized his whole body, and he was sure that his death was near. He thought himself unworthy of mercy. He went humbly to his mother and begged her to make a vow to the Saint for him, so that he might be healed through her faith. She did it, and he was well before dawn. The man who had laughed at the witnesses of the truth became a public witness himself, and he proclaimed the glory of Christ.

Status: approved by the Church. The early Latin Life lists it with the miracles read out at the canonization. It gives no place or date. Rigauld tells it about 1300 and says the friars sing of it in a hymn of the Saint's Office. The cleric's name and the full telling come from the Ancona manuscript.

A hunchbacked woman stands straight, Padua 1231

The Ancona manuscript tells of a woman named Cuniza. She came to the grave on the day Saint Anthony's body was laid in the church of the Mother of God. A huge hump on her shoulder bent her so low that she could walk only with wooden crutches called ferulae. She stayed a little while in prayer, lying down before the tomb. At once her shoulder became level, and everyone watching saw the hump vanish. She left her crutches behind and went home standing straight.

Ricarda, bent for twenty years, walks from the tomb

The Ancona manuscript tells of Ricarda, a woman of Padua whom almost every citizen knew. For twenty years her legs had been withered and so twisted that her knees were stuck to her chest and her feet to her buttocks. One day she came on her low stools to the Saint's tomb to beg with the other poor people. Heavy sleep came over her, and she dozed a little with her head bowed. A voice cried out while she rested: “Thanks be to God, for she is freed!” She woke and saw a girl who had come with a hump and was now healed. Many people went with the girl as she left.

Ricarda got up to go in to the tomb herself. Near the church doors a boy of seven appeared and went ahead of her. He joined his hands and invited her in: “Come, come, in the Lord's name, for he will free you.” She followed him, dragging herself to the church threshold. At the doorway the boy disappeared. She went on to the enclosure around the tomb.

As she prayed, two lumps shaped like eggs burst out near her thigh. Fluid ran under her skin, and the lumps moved down to the bottom of her feet. They made a sound like clapping hands, and many people heard it. Her legs had been dried up for twenty years. Now they were stretched out before everyone's eyes. Her skin loosened, and living flesh grew back to her old size. She left her stools behind and walked home with firm steps through the middle of the city. The citizens who knew her were amazed.

Status: approved by the Church. The Ancona manuscript lists it with the miracles brought forward at the canonization and read before Pope Gregory IX.

Maria, hurt at the Brenta, is healed

The Ancona manuscript tells of a woman named Maria. She had followed her father's mares to the river Brenta and sat down under a walnut tree. A dark man came out of the river and walked straight to her. He picked her up in his arms and quickly carried her to another walnut tree nearby. There he wanted to do violence to her and threw her to the ground. He left her so broken that her chest was humped, one knee was drawn up, and her hip was twisted. Her father led her home.

For five years and more she stayed bent like this. Then she was carried to the tomb of Saint Anthony and was restored to her former health through his merits. While she was being made well, she felt a hand move gently between her flesh and her bones. Her limbs had long been worn out with pain, and the hand soothed them more sweetly than any ointment.

Status: approved by the Church. The Ancona manuscript lists it with the miracles brought forward at the canonization and read before Pope Gregory IX.

Gilla feels a hand lift her at the tomb

The Ancona manuscript tells of Gilla, a woman who had been bent for eight years. Her left leg was withered and its sinews were drawn up, so she could not set her feet on the ground. Her husband Marcoaldus brought her on horseback and laid her before the tomb. She bowed down in prayer, and a great distress came over her. She could not bear the heat that seized her, and men carried her out to breathe cooler air. A little later they brought her back, and she asked for mercy at the tomb. She felt a man's hand touch her belly and try to lift her body. When she opened her eyes to see who had touched her, she saw no one near. Then she understood that she had felt help from God. She stood up at once by herself, left her crutches, and went away glad and healthy with her husband.

Agnes can eat again after lying on the tomb

The Ancona manuscript tells of a girl named Agnes. For three years her whole body had lost its strength from an illness called atrophy. One day she was carried to the tomb and laid on it. She was in great pain and seemed close to death. The pain eased a little. Then she called to her mother, who was near. She said that she was so hungry she could swallow a whole loaf. Her mother took her home glad and gave her bread at once. The girl kept it down. Her dried-up body recovered its old moisture.

Caesaria of Venice is healed beside the church wall

The Ancona manuscript tells of Caesaria, a woman of Venice. For two years and more one of her hands had been drawn up, and her left foot had been twisted sideways. When the sweat left her, she got up. Her hand and foot were healthy. She gave God due thanks and went away glad.

Saint Anthony speaks to Colangria in the night

The Ancona manuscript tells of Colangria of Montagnana. She had lost the use of her body for a year and a month. Through the Saint's merits she felt no pain at all, and she stayed well from then on.

Eight lame or bent people are healed

The Ancona manuscript tells of these eight people, one after another:

  • Federicus came from the county of Concordia. He had fallen carelessly from the church of Pulcigno. His back was drawn up, and he could move only with crutches. He came to the tomb and made a vow. He got his health back, left his crutches, and walked home upright while everyone who had seen him marveled.
  • Prosdocima was the widow of Mainerius of Nocrite. Her hand and both her feet were drawn up. She was carried to the tomb in a wooden tub. When she was lifted onto the chest, her feet stood straight before everyone and were fully restored. Her hand trembled a little and began to open and stretch. The keepers of the chest told her to close it and open it again.
  • Margarita of Padua seemed to fall from a height one night in her sleep. When she woke, her neck was twisted, and her left hand and foot were so shortened that she could hardly touch the ground with the tips of her toes. One day she was set on the Saint's tomb. At once her neck straightened and her head went back to its right place. Her hand and foot were restored, and she came down freed through the merits of Saint Anthony.
  • Albertus was a boy of eleven. His left foot had been twisted from birth. His father often tied pieces of wood to it to straighten it. Whenever they were untied, it sprang back into the same twist. One day his mother came to the chest with him and prayed. The boy stayed there a little while and sweated heavily. The keepers of the chest gave him back to his mother, and he walked home with his soles on the ground.
  • Massaguerra of Sacile's right leg had been drawn up for two years, and his foot hung down. He came to the chest on crutches and began to sweat heavily. The pain was too much to bear, and he drew back a little. The people near him ran up and lifted him as he tried to stand. At once the sinews of his foot stretched out in front of everyone. He left his crutches and hurried home. The whole neighborhood ran to meet him. They wept, rang the bells, and gave thanks to God and Saint Anthony.
  • Tridentinus was a young man with a bone growing out of his spine. For five years he had walked hunched over with his hands stretched to his knees and a crutch to hold him up. He came to the Saint's tomb. His mother had full faith and had him lifted a little over the chest. At once the hump went down, and he came down from the chest standing upright. He left his crutch and went home with his mother, glad and giving praise.
  • Veridolus had broken kidneys and a hump. His head hung almost to the ground, and he went about on low stools. His mother led him to the tomb. At once such great pains pressed on him that he sweated all over. When the sweat eased a little, he straightened his back in front of those standing by. Through the Saint's merits the hump went down and vanished.
  • Venetiana of Treviso had carried a hump on her chest for two years and more. It was shaped like a loaf of bread, and she bent her head down to her knees when she had to move. She came to the tomb and prayed there for three days. She was restored to the health she longed for. The hump went down, she lifted her head, and she left giving thanks to God and Saint Anthony.

Status: approved by the Church. The Ancona manuscript lists it with the miracles brought forward at the canonization and read before Pope Gregory IX.

Six speechless or trembling people are healed

The Ancona manuscript tells of these six people, one after another:

  • Bartholomew of Pieve di Sacco had been mute since childhood. For four years he had also lost the use of his whole body to paralysis. He made a vow and came to the tomb. He kept on praying there with full faith. His tongue was loosed, and he gave praise to God. He had been carried there on a man's back, and he left on his own feet.
  • Micholeta had been mute for eleven years and said nothing at all. Her whole body had lost its strength. She heard people tell of the wonders done through Saint Anthony, and she had herself carried to his tomb. She lay before the chest and begged for mercy with full devotion. Through his merits she left healed.
  • A man from Friuli grieved that his tongue could no longer do its work. His mother led him to the chest of the Saint. He stood devoutly before the tomb and got back the speech he had lost.
  • Maria came from the county of Ferrara. For four years her whole body had been weak, and her head shook all the time. She came to the Saint's burial place and stayed in devout prayer. Suddenly her sinews began to grow firm, and the shaking of her head stopped. She stood upright and steady on her feet, with many people around her. Fully healed, she praised God and went home.

Ten people regain sight or hearing at the tomb

The Ancona manuscript tells of these ten people, one after another:

  • A German woman named Carolina had been blind in both eyes for seven years. She came devoutly to the tomb and prayed before the chest for a short time. She received her sight from heaven and went away glad and giving praise.
  • Theodoric was a friar of the Order from the land of Apulia. For two years he had been without the sight of his left eye. He came humbly to the Saint's tomb and stayed some time at Padua with the friars. There he begged earnestly at the tomb for his healing. At last he received the sight he wanted, and he went back to his country glad and giving praise.
  • A girl named Auriema had been blind for a year and a half. She was carried to the tomb to be healed. She laid the cloth that covered the chest on her blind eyes. Through the Saint's merits her eyelids opened at once, and she received the sight she had longed for.
  • Leonardus of Corniclano had been wholly blind in one eye for three years. The other eye was so dim that he could tell people he knew from strangers only by their voices. He came to the tomb to be healed and prayed there for a short time before the chest. He left with the sight of both eyes restored.
  • Flos de Gemma of Loreto had been blind in her left eye for seven years. She came to the chest of Saint Anthony and received the sight she had wished for. She gave thanks to God and went home with joy.
  • Alexia had lost the use of both eyes for five years. She got back the sight she had lost for so long at the tomb of the man of God, through his merits.
  • A woman of Padua named Michalotta had the falling sickness, an old name for epilepsy. The illness was so severe that she lost her sight, and she was often close to death. Her mother carried her to the tomb. Michalotta was lifted a little onto the chest, and her eyes opened at once. She got back the sight she had lost. From then on the falling sickness never troubled her.
  • A man called Rolandus Bulgarus had been deaf for twenty years because of a very grave illness of the head. He stood devoutly before the Saint's tomb and was restored to his former health. He went home glad.
  • Leonardus of Venice had been wholly deaf for four years because his ears were blocked. One day he came to the tomb with devotion and asked for help. He got back his hearing at once.
  • Menicus had been deaf for two years. He came humbly to the burial place of Saint Anthony to ask for health. While he stood there asking for mercy, he was fully restored. He left glad and giving praise to God.

A drowned girl lives again after a vow

The Ancona manuscript tells of a woman in the county of Padua who went to a neighbor's house to get fire. On her way home she found her little daughter floating on her back in a ditch full of water and mud. The girl had followed her. The mother cried out and pulled her drowned girl from the pool. She laid her on the edge of the ditch. Many people ran up to see the sad sight. One of them held her up by the feet with her head down and tried to make her bring up the water. The girl had no voice and no feeling. Her lips and cheeks were tight, and everyone gave up hope.

At last the mother made a vow. She promised to bring a wax image to the tomb of Saint Anthony if he would give her daughter back to her alive. As soon as she had said it, everyone standing there saw the girl move her lips. Someone put a finger in her mouth, and she at once brought up the water she had drunk. Through the merits of Saint Anthony the warmth of life came back to her, and she lived.

A buried young man lives, Monopoli

The Ancona manuscript tells of a young man who was digging earth near the friars' house at Monopoli, a city of Apulia. Then the bank above him fell and crushed him. A boy saw him buried and ran crying out to the young man's mother. She struck her face with her hands and tore her hair. She ran in tears to the friars' house and cried out, “Run, brothers, run! Help me, for the earth has covered my son!” The friars ran with hoes and other tools and began to dig where they had seen the earth fall. His mother kept calling out, “Saint Anthony, give me back my son!”

The diggers found his feet first. They were blue from the weight of the earth. Then they found his buttocks, bruised by the earth. Others dug with spades and made three holes in the cap he wore on his head. When they pulled the young man from under the earth, he was alive but badly shaken. The friars asked how he had stayed alive under the fallen earth. He answered, “Blessed Anthony held his hand over my throat.” Many who stood there heard this and gave due thanks to God and Saint Anthony.

Saint Anthony eats poison and is unharmed

Rigauld writes that heretics in an Italian province invited Saint Anthony to dinner. They served him a dish of deadly poison. The Holy Spirit showed him their plan, and he reproved them gently. He made the sign of the cross over the dish. He said, “I do not do this to test God, but to show my steady and earnest eagerness for your salvation and for the triumph of the faith.” Then he ate all of it, and God kept him from harm. The heretics came to the Catholic faith. Rigauld writes that God preserved him from danger because his poverty was so dear to God. The 1883 chronicle sets this dinner at Rimini.

A hungry animal kneels before the Host

A heretic refused to believe that Christ is really present in the Host. The Host is the consecrated Bread of the Mass, the Body of Christ. He proposed a test. He would keep his horse without food for two days and then offer it oats in the public square. Saint Anthony would stand on the other side and hold the Body of the Lord. The horse was kept without food as agreed. On the third day it was led into the square. Saint Anthony stood there and carried the Body of Christ with reverence in a ciborium, the covered cup that holds the Host. The hungry horse left the oats. It walked to the Body of Christ and knelt before it. It stayed on its knees until Saint Anthony let it rise. Rigauld writes that the sight freed the heretic from the darkness of error.

Status: told in Rigauld's Life about 1300. Rigauld took it from a small treatise on Saint Anthony's miracles and names no town. The 1883 chronicle tells it at Rimini with a mule owned by Bonvillo and kept without food for three days. It says the miracle was repeated at Toulouse with a heretic named Guialdo. The collection of his miracles written about 1367 and the Basilica of Saint Anthony tell the Toulouse form. In that form Saint Anthony says Mass in a small chapel and then carries the Host to the square. Baring-Gould also sets it at Toulouse and has the mule break its halter as Saint Anthony carries the Host to a dying man. The Catholic Encyclopedia of 1913 names Rimini and says fourteenth-century tellers place it at Toulouse and Bruges. Baring-Gould says the kneeling mule became one of his emblems in art.

A novice named Peter is freed from temptation

Rain does not fall on Saint Anthony's crowd

A novice returns Saint Anthony's stolen book

The collection of Saint Anthony's miracles written about 1367 says that he was teaching theology to the friars at Montpellier. A young friar in training left the Order at night and took a valuable book of the Psalms that Saint Anthony used in his teaching. Saint Anthony was very sad and prayed. Status: first written down in the collection of about 1367. The collection calls the figure the devil and the book a glossed psalter. A glossed psalter is a book of the Psalms with explanations. A nobleman sees the Child with Saint Anthony

A nobleman who was Saint Anthony's host saw the Child Jesus with him. In the Italian tradition this was Count Tiso at Camposampiero. Stories told of Saint Anthony in later centuries

Stories told by Surius and Wadding

Laurentius Surius and Luke Wadding told these stories in the 1500s and 1600s. The Bollandists print them in a supplement to the early Life:

  • a murdered boy at Lisbon is raised from the dead to clear Saint Anthony's father of a charge
  • Saint Anthony appears in a Lisbon court and clears his father over royal money
  • the bones of a capon he ate turn into fish bones
  • a roast owl set before him by heretics tastes like capon
  • Saint Anthony preaches in Italian with perfect ease though he was born far away
  • Saint Anthony appears at night to sleepers and tells them to confess sins that only God knew

A man left for dead is raised, Naples 1675

The Bollandists tell of Antonio Tortamano of Montemurro in the kingdom of Naples. He was very devoted to Saint Anthony. In April 1675 he went to Ferrandina to buy linen. On the evening before, he ate supper at a friend's house with two other guests. They had learned from his words that he carried money, or they had guessed it from his errand. The early Latin Life tells of men of Padua who plotted to kill a priest. They set an ambush for him in a certain place. Saint Anthony appeared there in the likeness of a friar they did not know. A note from Saint Anthony frees a woman

Rigauld tells of a woman in Portugal who was often troubled by a devil. Her husband once said in anger that she was possessed. She went out to drown herself. On the way she passed the friars' church on the feast of Saint Anthony. She went in to commend herself to God before she died. She fell asleep and saw Saint Anthony in a vision. The collection of about 1367 tells it at Santarém in the time of King Dionysius. It says the woman was a sinner with great devotion to Saint Anthony. The devil tormented her and tempted her to kill herself. It seemed to her that Christ spoke in her heart. If you go to the river Tagus and drown yourself there to pay for your faults, I will forgive all your sins and give you eternal glory.” Her husband was away, and the devil kept urging her. One day she was deceived and went toward the Tagus.

She passed the friars' church on the feast of Saint Anthony and went in to commend herself to him. There she fell on her face before the altar in the chapel of Saint Anthony and prayed in tears. She asked him to show her whether God wanted her to drown herself or to give up that plan altogether. As she prayed, she fell gently asleep. It said, “Look at the Cross of the Lord; flee, you hostile powers; the Lion of the tribe of Judah, the Root of David, has conquered; alleluia, alleluia.” At once the temptation left her. While she kept the note, the devil did not trouble her.

With the help of the friars he got a copy from the King and gave it to her. The copy freed her as the first note had. She confessed with tears and turned wholly to God. She lived a godly life for twenty years and ended her days in peace. King Dionysius put the note among his relics. Many wonders were done when people called on Saint Anthony with it.

Saint Francis and Saint Anthony reach a dying lady

The collection tells of a powerful lady named Lupa at Linhares in Portugal. She was devoted to Saint Francis and Saint Anthony. A demon had served her in the form of a woman as her chambermaid, and she had lived in cruelty and many crimes. In her last illness she lost hope because of the greatness of her sins. She did not care for her soul and would not make her confession, though people urged her. She lay there sad and without hope. Two Friars Minor came in. They comforted her and urged her to confess and repent. She refused. She said she had committed so many sins that God would not show mercy for any penance of hers.

The friar who looked older said, “Confess and be sorry. I take your penance on myself, and I give you a share in my goods. By the power of the Lord's Passion I promise you eternal life.” At these words the lady changed for the better and was given courage to repent. She turned from a wolf into a lamb. She was sorry for her sins and confessed with many tears. Then she asked for the robe of the Friars Minor and received it from that friar's hand. She fell asleep in the Lord and died happy. At once the friars vanished. Everyone thought it reasonable that they were Saint Francis and Saint Anthony, to whom she was so devoted and whom she always called to her aid. She was buried in the convent at Guarda.

Later a squire wanted to go to Linhares, where the lady had died. One night he heard a voice like a woman's. It cried, “O wretched me! For thirteen years I served badly, and I labored in vain.” The squire was terrified. He made the sign of the cross and said, “I command you by Jesus Christ, say who you are.” The voice answered, “I am the devil. For thirteen years I served Lady Lupa in the form of a woman and helped her in many crimes. I wanted to take her to the depths of hell after her death. But at the end two Friars Minor in hoods came, whom she had loved before. They turned her toward repentance. They snatched her soul out of my power and took her with them to the joys of heaven.”

The devil gave him a sign that this was true. When the squire came to Linhares, he would hear that a smith had killed his wife and would be hanged for it. The devil said he had caused that killing and would lead the souls of the smith and his wife to hell. The squire went to Linhares and found the smith already hanged for killing his wife. He told what he had heard, to everyone's amazement.

Saint Francis and Saint Anthony save a woman

The collection tells of a woman named Seri in a castle in Portugal. She had a special devotion to Saint Francis and Saint Anthony. Her husband was a wicked man. He had left her and lived with other women. He hated her and often beat her and hurt her in many ways. Her sorrow grew until she lost all hope. She decided to end her troubles by hanging herself. One night her husband was away, and the household was asleep. She set up a rope in the room and was about to put her neck in the noose, as the devil urged her. Someone knocked loudly at the door and cried out. She hid the rope and went to see who it was.

She opened the door and saw two Friars Minor. They humbly asked her to take them in for the love of God. She asked where they came from and what their names were. They answered, “We come from far away. One of us is called Francis, and the other Anthony.” She said, “Come in, for love of Saint Francis and Saint Anthony.” She set a table for them, and they gave her new strength with sacred words. Out of respect for them she decided not to do that night what the enemy of mankind had urged. The friars went into the room that was given them for sleeping, and the woman went to bed.

At the same hour the two friars appeared to her husband. They said, “We are Saint Francis and Saint Anthony, sent by God to tell you this. Turn from your evil way. Send away your other women and stay with your wife, who is devoted to us. If you do not, you will die in three days and fall into hell. This night your wife would have hanged herself because of your cruelty, if we had not come to her door. Go to her, and as a sign ask her for the rope she meant to use.” The man woke suddenly, afraid and sorry for his sins. In the morning he got up and went to his house.

His wife had risen too. She did not find the friars. She found the bed made, as if no one had slept in it. This amazed her. All the doors were shut, and she could not think how the friars had gone out. Her husband came in and greeted her kindly. He said, “Dearest, where is the rope you wanted to hang yourself with last night?” She stood astonished. He said, “I know well what a favor they did you. Saint Francis and Saint Anthony have freed you and me from death of body and soul, and you took them in last night.” Then he told his vision and humbly asked his wife's pardon. They lived together for a long time in love and peace. They did many good works and praised Saint Francis and Saint Anthony together.

A woman rises at her burial with a vision

The collection tells of a married woman in the village of Elbrun, near the castle of Torres Novas in Portugal. On the feast of Saint Anthony she was carrying a sack of wheat on her head to be ground. She was walking with a girl. A violent wind struck her from the front. It threw the sack to the ground and made her fall on her back. At once a young man with a shining face came and took her soul. He led her along a very wide road to a deep pit that was dark and terrible. Terrible flames seemed to rise from its mouth to the sky. Black and foul smoke came out of it. From inside she heard the roars of the tormentors and the tormented, with weeping and great howling.

She looked in, trembling, and saw demons torturing many kinds of people, each for the deeds by which they had sinned. Cheating merchants wore burning purses around their necks. Moneylenders were fed burning coins. Robbers, adulterers, murderers, false witnesses, and judges were tormented with fitting punishments. She asked the young man what the place was, and he said it was the pit of hell. She saw there many people who were still living. They had been given places there and walked with demons. They were from the city of Lisbon and the town of Santarém, and she named them. She had never been to those places. The collection says it should not seem unbelievable that things still to come are shown in such a vision as if they were present.

After this the young man led her to another place, pleasant and full of joy. It was bright with grasses of many kinds and adorned with all kinds of flowers and fruits. She saw a very large and high tent. Bright men came out of it two by two in procession. They wore garments of many kinds and crowns on their heads. At the end came one who was like a bridegroom, dressed in wonderful splendor. The whole procession seemed to be held in his honor. She asked who the place was for and who the people were. The young man said that it was the resting place of souls and that everyone there was saved. He said the bridegroom who came with such splendor was Saint Anthony. His feast was kept in heaven by the saints, far more splendidly than on earth. At the feasts of other saints, others kept the feast in turn. He told her, “You were led here and shown these things so that on the feasts of saints you will stay away from servile work. Give the saints the reverence they are owed, and above all keep away from evil works.”

While her soul was being led, people carried her body to the castle to bury it. She seemed completely dead. The grave was being made ready, and everyone who saw her was amazed and afraid. The woman rose up. She told her whole vision in order, first to the people there, then to many others, and to the writer who set this down.

Saint Anthony's statue shakes its head, Bordeaux 1367

The collection tells that in 1367 the Prince of Aquitaine, Edward, gathered a large army. He meant to help King Peter of Castile, whom his illegitimate brother Henry had driven out of his kingdom. The Prince ordered a surgeon named Peter, who lived in the city of Bordeaux, to follow his army and treat the wounded. Peter found this heavy and bitter. He saw that the Prince's will was firm, and he did not dare to resist. Peter had a special devotion to Saint Anthony. He went with confidence to the friars' house at Bordeaux. At his request a friar said a Mass of Saint Anthony in a chapel that held a carved wooden statue of him.

Peter heard the Mass devoutly and looked at the statue. He prayed with great fervor. If the trip was not good for his soul, he asked Saint Anthony to stop it in his mercy. If it was good, he asked him to make Peter willing to go. He saw the statue's head move from side to side as he prayed. It was as if the statue were saying no. Peter was astonished. He thought it might be his own strong imagination. He gathered all his strength and looked again carefully, and he said the prayer again. Again he saw the wooden statue shake its head, as if refusing.

After the Mass he left in wonder. He did not know what the sign meant. Perhaps Saint Anthony was telling him not to go with the army. Or perhaps the sign meant that his going would be good for his soul. He went home confused and unsure. A little later a messenger came from the Prince and told him to come at once. Peter went to the Prince's house, and the Seneschal met him. The Seneschal asked, “Are you ready to go with the Prince to Spain, as he ordered you?” Peter was afraid and answered, “Lord, I am ready to do the Prince's will in everything.” The Seneschal smiled and answered cheerfully, “You speak well, as a good and faithful man. For your comfort the Prince allows you to stay here, unless you receive another order.” Peter went gladly to the friars' church and thanked Saint Anthony. He told the story to some of the friars.

Status: told in the collection of his miracles written about 1367. Peter swore an oath before the friars that it was true.

Men sent to destroy his picture are struck down

The collection tells that in the time of Pope Boniface VIII the apse of the Lateran basilica of the Saviour in Rome was being decorated. Two skilled Friars Minor were chosen to make the mosaic. They finished the pictures the Pope had ordered. They saw that there was still room for other pictures. On their own, or perhaps by God's prompting, they painted Saint Francis and Saint Anthony on either side. Out of envy, some clerics told the Pope. The Pope said, “We will put up with the picture of Saint Francis, since it is already there. But what are we to do here with Saint Anthony of Padua? Go and destroy his picture, and have a picture of Saint Gregory made in its place.”

The clerics went up one after another. They later confessed that a terrible figure appeared to them there and threw them down from the height to the floor. They seemed to go mad, and they were stopped from doing what they had planned. The painter friars said that some of them died at once and the others all died within a short time. The Pope heard this. He told those who reported it, “Let that Saint stand as he wishes. As we plainly see, we could sooner perish than gain anything in dealing with him.”

He was so attached to the Friars Minor that he gave them a place to build a convent and gave much more for the buildings. One night he was gravely ill. Lost things found through Saint Anthony

The 1883 chronicle records things found through Saint Anthony's help in the 1500s and 1600s. They include a manuscript of the theologian Ambrosius Catharinus, a church worker's incense burner, and money that belonged to King Charles II. Catholics ask Saint Anthony especially for help with lost things. The story of Saint Anthony's own stolen book is also tied to this devotion. It is told earlier in this list.

The chronicle tells the first two stories at more length. Ambrosius Catharinus had lost a manuscript. He promised to mention the favor in the book if the Saint helped him get it back. Soon an unknown person came up to him and asked whether he had lost a manuscript. A church worker had lost a valuable incense burner. He told this to the man who had stolen it. The thief proposed that they go together to hear a Mass in honor of Saint Anthony at the friars' church nearby. There they would pray to the Saint to show who had taken it. During the Mass the thief took his handkerchief from his pocket. He had forgotten that a piece of the burner's chain was in the pocket too. The chain fell out, and the thief's prayer was answered to his own shame.

The Acta Sanctorum gives the Catharinus story from his own book on the glory of the saints, printed at Lyon in 1541. He was a Dominican bishop. He set out from Toulouse with a companion and carried a manuscript to take to the printer. It held his notes for debates with heretics. The whole bundle fell from him on the road. He did not notice until he had gone several leagues. Then he went back to Toulouse by the same road and found nothing. Catharinus did all he could and asked his friend the governor of the city to help, but it was in vain. He set out again, sad.

Human help had failed, so he remembered Saint Anthony. He vowed that if he recovered the papers through the Saint's prayers, he would add this favor to the other praises of the saints in his book. He had hardly spoken when a traveler came up to him and asked whether he had lost a book with some papers. Catharinus said yes and described how it could be recognized. The traveler led him to the place where everything lay, with nothing missing. He knew what a favor he had received and told the whole story in his book.

Status: told in the 1883 chronicle. The friars sing the responsory “Si quaeris miracula” in his honor. The Basilica says Julian of Speyer wrote it in 1233. For the first story the Acta Sanctorum draws on Catharinus's own book.

A Note on the Sources

The early Latin Life is the one the Bollandists printed; a 1904 editor took it for the Life by Julian of Speyer. Tellers place some stories in different towns. The fish sermon is set at Rimini and near Padua. The kneeling animal is set at Rimini and at Toulouse. The Child Jesus is set at Camposampiero, at Padua, and in the Limousin. The appearance in the friars' choir is set at Limoges and at Montpellier. The humbling of Ezzelino is set at Verona and at Bassano. The Catholic Encyclopedia of 1913 holds that Saint Anthony went to Verona in 1230 to ask Ezzelino to free prisoners. It calls the tyrant's humbling a legend of doubtful origin and says Saint Anthony may have failed. It says the documents do not decide where the Child Jesus appeared. Baring-Gould read the fish in the sermon as a later addition to a story that rebukes men who will not listen. The Catholic Encyclopedia credits the responsory “Si quaeris” to Julian of Speyer, and the 1883 chronicle gives it to Saint Bonaventure. These are the views of modern writers. The Order and the Church have handed the stories down as they are told above. The Bollandist notes in the Acta Sanctorum ask which queen is meant in the accounts of the two princesses, and they would date the surgeon's story to 1366 rather than 1367. This list tells the accounts of the Ancona manuscript and of the collection of about 1367, both printed in the Acta Sanctorum. A few of their accounts are left out because they only repeat another account or give a general statement.
