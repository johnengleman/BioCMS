## SC-06 — The ordination meal, about 1222 (Forlì)
- Status: full
- When and where: About 1222 (date from Woodcock, O-S2 p.~28; the early Lives give no year). Forlì, at an ordination of friars. The Latin says "collationis hora", the hour of the collation (the talk before a meal or evening reading). The notes say "meal"; the sources do not name a meal. No room is described.
- Who was there: Anthony; several friars sent with him to be ordained; some Dominican friars; the local Minister of the friars; later the Minister General hears of it [W-S1 p.200; W-S2 pp.45-47].
- What happened, in order:
  1. Friars gather at Forlì to be ordained, among them Anthony; some Dominicans are also present [W-S1 p.200].
  2. The Minister asks the Dominicans to give the friars a word of exhortation. All refuse, saying they are unprepared [W-S1 p.200; W-S2 p.45].
  3. The Minister turns to Anthony, whose learning no one suspects, and orders him to say whatever the Holy Spirit gives [W-S1; W-S2 p.45].
  4. Anthony protests he is unfit, and holds out until a formal command is laid on him [W-S2 pp.45-46].
  5. He begins "simply, in the fear of the Lord". As he goes on, his talk grows deep and clear. His memory "served him in place of books" [W-S2 p.46].
  6. The hearers are astonished. Julian says they honoured the hidden light in him and the virtue of his proved humility [W-S1 p.200].
  7. The Minister General hears of it and appoints him preacher [W-S1 p.200; W-S2 p.46].
- Details: Julian says he was thought better at washing kitchen pots than at explaining the mysteries of Scripture. Julian also says nothing else showed his learning, except that he seldom spoke bookishly [W-S1 p.200].
- Words spoken: Anthony's own refusal is not quoted in either source; both give it as reported speech only (unfit, unprepared). The crowd: "Never man spoke like this man" [W-S2 p.46] (Rigauld quotes John 7:46 here). Plain: "No one has ever spoken like this man."
- Testimony: Rigauld, W-S2 pp.45-46, English 1904, public-domain.
  "Antony, like a true lover of humility, infinitely preferred the place of disciple to that of teacher; he protested, therefore, alleging his unfitness and want of preparation, doing all in his power to avoid what appeared to him an ambitious act, until he was forced to submit to the formal command laid upon him. [...] He began, in the fear of God, with simple words, but enlightened by heavenly grace, and assisted by his memory, which served him in place of books, his language as he went on became so sublime, he explained so clearly the deep mysteries of Holy Scripture, he captivated the minds of his hearers with such overpowering eloquence, that it was evident to all that 'a gracious tongue in a good man aboundeth.' His marvellous address astonished his hearers, who were quite carried away by his eloquence, and exclaimed one to another, 'Never man spoke like this man,' and acknowledged that they had never heard a like discourse."
  plain: Anthony would rather have been a pupil than a teacher. He said he was unfit and unprepared. He did all he could to avoid what looked like showing off, until the formal order left him no choice. He began humbly, in simple words. Then his words rose, and his memory served him in place of books. He explained the hard parts of Scripture so clearly that his hearers were astonished. They said to one another that no one had ever spoken like this man.
- Julian's Latin (W-S1 p.200, OCR copied): "Sed cujus Incernam , dndum sub modio posi- (am , Doniinus super caudelabrum slatuerc nunc voluit" (reads: "Sed cujus lucernam, dudum sub modio positam, Dominus super candelabrum statuere nunc voluit"). plain: But the Lord now chose to set on a lampstand the lamp that had long been hidden under a bushel.
- What changed: The Minister General hears of it and appoints him preacher; Anthony is made to appear in public.
- Not in the sources: his exact words of refusal; a meal; the room; the hour; the Minister's name; the text of his sermon (Phil 2:8 is tradition only, O-S2 pp.21-22, not in these two Lives); the Dominicans' names; his age as an exact number (the Bollandist note says "26" in a garbled line only).
- Sources: W-S1 p.200 (Latin; also note b); W-S2 pp.45-47; O-S2 (date 1222).

## SC-07 — Rimini, among the heretics (1220s)
- Status: thin (the early sources give two sentences; no scene detail)
- When and where: Rimini, Italy; no year or day given. Woodcock puts the Italy years between the 1222 Forlì ordination and France, "1224" being almost certain for France (O-S2 pp.28-29, secondary guess).
- Who was there: Anthony; "many heretics"; Bononillo (Julian: "Bonovillum"), a leader of heretics.
- What happened, in order:
  1. Julian says Anthony refuted "the wrong teachings of the heretics" with fitting arguments [W-S1 p.200, "Nam et apud Ariminum quamplures haereticos ad integritatem fidei Christianae convertit"].
  2. Many heretics at Rimini returned to the Christian faith, among them a leader named Bononillo, who had been blinded for thirty years by the "pestilential mists" of error [W-S1 p.200; W-S2 p.65].
  3. Anthony "restored him to the light of truth" and he kept the Church's commands until death [W-S1; W-S2 p.65].
- Words spoken: none from Anthony in these sources.
- Testimony: Julian, W-S1 p.200, Latin, public-domain (OCR copied): "Nain et apud Ari- minnra qiiamplures hsereticos ad integrifafera fidei Christiaiue convertit : Infer quos et Ineresiarcham qucindain , Bonovillura nomine , ab annis triginta erroris pestiferi fenebris obcaicatum, ad lumen ve- rifatis induxit , et usquc ad mortera inandatis Ec- clesia; stare fecit."
  reads: Nam et apud Ariminum quamplures haereticos ad integritatem fidei Christianae convertit: inter quos et haeresiarcham quendam, Bonovillum nomine, ab annis triginta erroris pestiferi tenebris obcaecatum, ad lumen veritatis induxit, et usque ad mortem mandatis Ecclesiae stare fecit.
  plain: At Rimini he also brought very many heretics back to the full Christian faith. Among them was a certain leader of heretics named Bonovillo. For thirty years Bonovillo had been blind in the dark of a deadly error. Anthony led him to the light of the truth and kept him in obedience to the Church's commands until he died.
- Patarines in one line (secondary): Woodcock says the Catharist heresy was called "Cathari, or Patareni" in Italy, held two opposing principles of Good and Evil, and had its chief strongholds in Rimini and Milan (O-S2 p.25). Do not state more.
- What changed: Anthony wins the fight he wanted by words. A heretic leader of thirty years returns to the Church.
- Not in the sources: how the conversation went; Bononillo's reasons; the fish sermon and the mule (late legends; keep apart, see outline); dates; Anthony's mood. Note: Woodcock writes "Bonillo" and "Cathari"; Rigauld writes "Bononillo"; Julian "Bonovillum".
- Sources: W-S1 p.200; W-S2 p.65; O-S2 pp.25, 28-29 (secondary).

## SC-08 — The chapter at Arles, about 1226 (Provence)
- Status: full
- When and where: Provincial chapter at "Arles, in Provence" (Rigauld). Julian says only "in Provincia" (the Provence province). The Bollandist note e guesses 1226 or a little earlier [W-S1 note e]. Place of the chapter-room: not described beyond "the door" (Rigauld).
- Who was there: Anthony preaching; the friars gathered at the chapter; Francis, who is "alive at that time but in a distant part of Italy" [W-S2 p.65]; Friar Monaldo, "of tried virtue and an eye-witness" [W-S2 p.66].
- What happened, in order:
  1. The friars are gathered at a provincial chapter. Anthony preaches "in sweet and earnest words" on the title over the cross, "Jesus of Nazareth, King of the Jews" [W-S2 p.65; W-S1 p.200].
  2. Francis, still living but far away, appears at the door of the chapter-room, raised in the air, arms stretched out in the form of a cross, blessing the friars [W-S2 pp.65-66].
  3. Julian says that "as if approving the man of God's sermon, to show the hearers what to imitate" Francis appeared to the eyes of one of those sitting there, and blessed the sons with the sign of the cross [W-S1 p.200].
  4. Rigauld says Friar Monaldo affirmed it, and that Francis himself "openly attested the truth of it" [W-S2 p.66].
- Details: Julian says only one of those sitting there saw it ("felicibus cujusdam assidentium oculis"). Rigauld gives the witness as Monaldo. Rigauld says Francis is the upholder of the Cross and that Anthony had long desired its sufferings [W-S2 p.66].
- Words spoken: the sermon's subject only: the title of the cross. No words of Francis.
- Testimony: Rigauld, W-S2 pp.65-66, English 1904, public-domain.
  "St. Antony was preaching, in sweet and earnest words, on the title over the cross: 'Jesus of Nazareth, King of the Jews,' when his Blessed Father, Francis — who was alive at that time but in a distant part of Italy — appeared at the door of the Chapter-room, raised in the air, with his arms stretched out in the form of a cross blessing the Friars: thus bearing testimony to the truth of Antony's words. [...] This miracle was affirmed by Friar Monaldo, a man of tried virtue and an eye-witness, and was, moreover, confirmed by our holy Father, St. Francis, himself, who openly attested the truth of it."
  plain: Anthony was preaching gently and warmly on the sign above the cross, "Jesus of Nazareth, King of the Jews". Francis, his holy father, was alive then but far away in Italy. He appeared at the door of the chapter-room, lifted in the air, with his arms stretched out like a cross, blessing the friars. This showed that Anthony's words were true. Friar Monaldo, a man of proven virtue, saw it and said so. Francis himself later told the same story.
- Julian's Latin (W-S1 p.200, OCR copied): "Sanclus liic de titulo Crucis, dulcisque Jesu passionis suppliciis , dulci modulo pra;dicavit ; cum beatissimus Pater Franciscus , eo tempore Curpo- raliter adhuc vivens , sed in alia regione tunc lon- gius remotus, se in aere filiis, novo ac stupendo miraculi genere , prajsentavit." Reads: "Sanctus hic de titulo Crucis, dulcisque Jesu passionis suppliciis, dulci modulo praedicavit; cum beatissimus Pater Franciscus, eo tempore corporaliter adhuc vivens, sed in alia regione tunc longius remotus, se in aere filiis, novo ac stupendo miraculi genere, praesentavit." plain: This saint was preaching sweetly on the title of the cross and the sufferings of sweet Jesus, when the most blessed Father Francis, then still alive in the body but far away in another land, showed himself in the air to his sons, in a new and astonishing kind of miracle.
- What changed: Anthony's preaching on the Cross is confirmed in the eyes of the friars. Rigauld ties it to Anthony's long wish for martyrdom.
- Not in the sources: Celano's wording (C-S7 not read; the outline "need" is NOT filled; Julian's Life is said by the notes to add one Celano incident, which I did not verify); a meeting of Anthony and Francis in life (none recorded); the room, the crowd size, or the time of day; the town's name beyond "Arles" (Rigauld) and "Provence" (Julian). Rigauld's "alive" and "distant" are his wording; Bollandists date the chapter 1226, the year Francis died, and say this is a guess.
- Sources: W-S1 p.200 and note e; W-S2 pp.65-66.

## SC-09 — Rain held back, Creux-des-Arènes (France)
- Status: full
- When and where: No date. Rigauld names the place as "a vast plain, called the Creux des Artnes [Arènes]" where "the temples of the heathen gods once stood" [W-S2 p.71]. The passage does not name the town. Rigauld's introduction (p.~20) lists the places in his book as "Bourges, Brive, St. Junien, Limoges, St. Pierre-du-Queyroix, the Creux-des-Arènes" in one run, so the outline's "Limoges" is a guess from the Limousin context, not a quotation. Anthony's function there (guardian in Limousin) comes from Rigauld p.47.
- Who was there: Anthony; "an immense crowd"; friars who were living when Rigauld joined the Order and who "had been present at this sermon". Rigauld names none of them [W-S2 p.72].
- What happened, in order:
  1. A crowd too large for the church is gathered on a wide plain [W-S2 p.71].
  2. Anthony begins to preach [W-S2 p.71].
  3. Thunder, lightning, and heavy rain begin. The people, afraid, start to break up [W-S2 p.71].
  4. Anthony calms them and tells them not to move [W-S2 p.71].
  5. They stay. The rain pours all around, but not a drop falls on them [W-S2 p.71].
  6. He keeps preaching as long as he wishes. At the end the people see the ground around them soaked and their own place dry, and praise God [W-S2 p.72].
- Details: Rigauld compares it to Gideon's fleece and the dew [W-S2 p.72]. He adds that the friars who were there still told what subject Anthony preached on (he does not say what) [W-S2 p.72].
- Words spoken: Anthony, "quietly" [W-S2 p.71]: "Fear not; do not move nor cease listening to the Divine Word, for I trust in Him who never suffers us to trust in vain that the rain will not touch you." Plain: "Do not be afraid. Stay where you are and keep listening to God's word. I trust in the One who never lets our trust be empty. The rain will not touch you." (English from the 1904 translation; the Latin is not reachable.)
- Inner life: only the trust in his own words, as above. Rigauld says it showed "the might of Antony's prayer" [W-S2 p.72]; this is Rigauld's reading, not Anthony's words.
- Testimony: Rigauld, W-S2 pp.71-72, English 1904, public-domain.
  "As soon as this immense crowd was collected, the Man of God began to scatter the seed of the Word of God. But just as the people were listening most diligently, suddenly the thunder began to peal, the lightning to flash across the clouds, and the rain to fall. Afraid of the tempest and heavy rain, the people were beginning to break up — all were overcome by fear — but the Man of God tranquillized them, saying quietly: 'Fear not; do not move nor cease listening to the Divine Word, for I trust in Him who never suffers us to trust in vain that the rain will not touch you.' The people obeyed the words of the Man of God, and the Almighty [...] withheld the rain from falling on their assembly, though all around them it poured in torrents."
  plain: Anthony had just started to preach when thunder rolled, lightning flashed, and rain began. The frightened people started to leave. He calmed them quietly and told them to stay and keep listening. He said he trusted God, who never lets trust be wasted, and that the rain would not touch them. They stayed. Rain poured all around, and none fell on them.
- What changed: The crowd's veneration of Anthony grows; Rigauld's witnesses (friars who were present) told the story to him [W-S2 p.72].
- Not in the sources: the size of the crowd as a number; the town (not named in the passage); the date; the witnesses' names; how long he preached; what he preached on; the people's clothes or cries. Rigauld alone tells this (W-S1, Julian, does not); it is late (about 1293-1300), but he says he spoke with friars who were there.
- Sources: W-S2 pp.71-72, p.47, intro p.~20.

## SC-10 — Lent 1231 (Padua)
- Status: full
- When and where: Lent 1231 (the Lent before his death on 13 June 1231), Padua. First in the churches; then in "spacious open places of the fields" ("spatiosa sese camporum loca"); some talks were at night, with lights [W-S1 p.202].
- Who was there: Anthony; the people of Padua and of nearby towns, castles, and villages; clergy and "religious men"; the bishop; the stallholders; priests who heard confessions [W-S1 p.202].
- What happened, in order:
  1. A year before his death, at the General Chapter of Assisi where Francis's relics were moved (1230), Anthony is freed from governing the friars and given freedom to preach anywhere. He goes first to Padua and spends the winter writing sermons for the feast days at the cardinal bishop of Ostia's request [W-S1 p.202; W-S2 p.73].
  2. In Lent he plans to spend all his time on daily preaching and hearing confessions, and does [W-S1 p.202].
  3. The people crowd in so that "daily stations" (preaching stops) are set up in the churches. Then the churches are too small and he preaches in open fields [W-S1; W-S2 pp.73].
  4. Crowds come from the whole city and from towns and villages around, with the clergy, and the bishop is present. People come at night with lit lights to take places early [W-S1 p.202].
  5. The delicate and well-off sit there without their finery [W-S1].
  6. Among "thirty thousand or more" hardly a murmur is heard when he speaks [W-S1; W-S2 p.75].
  7. The shops are shut and stallholders sell nothing until the sermon ends [W-S1; W-S2 pp.73-74].
  8. After the sermon, everyone tries to touch him; he fears being crushed unless a strong escort of young men surrounds him [W-S1; W-S2 p.75].
  9. Results: enemies reconciled, prisoners freed, loans and usury returned, pledges given back, debts forgiven, public sinners converted. The priests are not enough to hear the confessions [W-S1 p.203 (Latin); W-S2 p.74].
  10. He does this though ill all the time and weighed down by corpulence [W-S1 p.203].
  11. Forty days: "he ran through the forty days' span and gathered for the Lord a rich harvest of the faithful" [W-S1 p.203].
- Details: Woodcock says Anthony often went without food until evening without noticing, spending the whole day between pulpit and confessional [O-S2 p.81, secondary; Woodcock gives no source for it, so treat as "Woodcock says"]. People cut small pieces from his habit as relics [W-S1 p.203; W-S2 p.75]. The debtors' law: a Padua statute of 15 March 1231 said that a citizen who gave up all his goods would no longer be jailed for life or banished for debt, and says it was passed at the request of Blessed Antony [O-S2 pp.70-71, secondary, public-domain; Catholic Encyclopedia C-S1 per the notes, not re-read].
- Words spoken (from the Lent sermons, a sample of his message, not words in this scene): Latin, Paris 1521, fol. LXXXV (Feria V, week 2 of Lent), OCR copied: "O quot induutur hodie purpura» panno.fintin&o fudore & fanguine pauperis;quia Induutur rapina,furto,vfura,illidto lucro". Reads: "O quot induuntur hodie purpura, panno intincto sudore et sanguine pauperis; quia induuntur rapina, furto, usura, illicito lucro". plain: How many people today are dressed in purple, in cloth dipped in the sweat and blood of the poor, because they are dressed in robbery, theft, usury, and dishonest profit. Note: Woodcock cites this saying as "Sermon in Fer. 6, Dom. I Quadr." (p.82); the Latin I found is at the Thursday of week 2 sermon on the rich man and Lazarus, so Woodcock's citation does not match. Use the Latin folio. Whether he said it in Lent 1231 is not known; these Lent sermons are written texts.
- Testimony: the old robber, Rigauld W-S2 pp.74-75, English 1904, public-domain, about 1292 (told to a friar):
  "I was a robber by profession [...] I belonged to a gang of a dozen brigands; we lived in the forest and plundered all the passers-by. But having heard the fame of Blessed Antony's preaching, we resolved to go in disguise, the whole twelve of us, on a certain day to hear his sermon [...] no sooner had his burning words sounded in our ears than we began to feel bitter remorse and compunction for our sins and evil deeds, and when the good Father had heard our confessions, one after another, and had given a suitable penance to each, he forbade us positively to return to our former sinful life [...] As for myself, the Saint had imposed on me the penance of going twelve times in pilgrimage to the tombs of the Apostles, and I am now on my way from Rome for the twelfth time."
  plain: I was a robber with a band of twelve. We lived in the forest and robbed travellers. We heard of Anthony's preaching and went in disguise to hear him. As soon as his words reached us, we felt deep sorrow for our crimes. He heard each of our confessions and gave each a penance. He told us never to go back to that life. For my penance I had to go to Rome twelve times. This is my twelfth trip back.
  Caution: Rigauld does not place this in Lent 1231 or in Padua; he places it just after the Padua paragraph and says an old man told it to a friar "about the year 1292". Use it as an example of his preaching, not as a Padua witness.
- What changed: A city is moved. The opening question is answered: the man no one asked for fills fields with thirty thousand. Padua's law protects debtors.
- Not in the sources: the exact number of days in the fields; the field names or place; weather; how many shops; any single named penitent in Padua; whether he spoke the "purple" line in this Lent; what he ate or when. Also not in the sources: that he "forgot to eat" in Julian or Rigauld; that comes from Woodcock alone.
- Sources: W-S1 p.202-203 (Julian; Latin as above); W-S2 pp.73-75; O-S2 pp.70-71, 81-82; O-S3 fol. LXXXV (archive:bub_gb_7QMdY8Vu5N0C); C-S1 for the 15 March 1231 law (not re-read this session).

Sed et stationari d, clausis rerum venalium apothecis, nihil omnino vendere prffisuinebant, donec ad sua sermone soluto singuli quique redibant." Reads: "ut in triginta vel amplius hominum millibus vix murmur aut strepitus audiretur. Sed et stationarii, clausis rerum venalium apothecis, nihil omnino vendere praesumebant, donec ad sua sermone soluto singuli quique redibant." plain: so that among thirty thousand men or more you could hardly hear a murmur or a sound. And the stallholders shut their shops and did not dare sell anything until the sermon was over and each went back to his own work.
