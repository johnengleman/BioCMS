## SC-11 — A night in Lent 1231, Padua
- Status: full
- When and where: "one night near the start of Lent" (nocte quadam circa Quadragesimae principium) [W-S1 n.26, p.202]. The Bollandist note works out Ash Wednesday as 5 February 1231 [W-S1 note c, p.202]. Place: his small cell (cellula) in Padua, where he had spent the winter writing sermons and preaching now and then [W-S1 n.26].
- Who was there: Anthony alone. The Latin Life names no one else.
- What happened, in order:
  1. He had worked through the winter. Lent began, and he chose to spend all of it preaching daily and hearing confessions [W-S1 n.26].
  2. One night, after he had fallen asleep from his labour, "the enemy of the human race" gripped his throat so hard that he would have choked him, if God's will had not held the enemy back [W-S1 n.26].
  3. At once he called on the name of the glorious Virgin. He opened his eyes free, and saw the whole cell filled with a bright light from above. The enemy could not bear the light and left [W-S1 n.26].
  4. He kept to his Lent plan and carried on [W-S1 n.27].
- Details: light from above; the cell. Rigauld names the same event in two lines, as his preaching and confessing: the Virgin "prevented the devil from strangling him while he was preaching and hearing confessions" [W-S2 pp.83-84; see also p.79, "delivered by her in after life from the power of the demon"].
- Words spoken: none quoted. Only "he called on the name of the glorious Virgin" (nomen gloriosae Virginis invocans).
- Inner life: how we know it. The Latin Life says "as he himself later told it among friends" (sicut ipse postmodum familiariter retulit). The story comes from Anthony.
- Testimony (Latin Life, n.26, exact OCR copy; public-domain):

## SC-12 — Camposampiero, spring to June 1231
- Status: full for what the sources give; his words on the hill are indirect only.
- When and where: after Easter (23 March 1231 per the Bollandists) a hill over the plain of Padua; then, "as the harvest was near", the place called Campo San Pietro (Camposampiero), north of Padua [W-S1 nn.29-30, notes c, f; W-S2 pp.77-78].
- Who was there: Anthony; "the companion of his journey" on the hill, unnamed [W-S1 n.29; W-S2 p.77]; Tiso, a nobleman, lord of the land where the friars' house stood [W-S1 n.29]; the friars of the house.
- What happened, in order:
  1. One day, looking at the plain of Padua and the site of the city from a hill, he "rejoiced in spirit" and began to praise the city with great praise, while his companion listened. He said it would soon be adorned with great honour [W-S1 n.29; W-S2 p.77]. Both Lives read this as his knowing what would happen at his death.
  2. As harvest came, he saw the people busy in the fields and chose to stop preaching for a time. Tired of the frequent talk of laymen, he left the city for Campo San Pietro "to refresh his spirit" [W-S1 n.29].
  3. Tiso received him with great devotion and kindness. A few days later, in a pleasant place planted with trees, not far from the friars' house, Tiso built a cell "over a walnut tree, with branches spreading out from one trunk", and Anthony gladly took it for his use [W-S1 n.29 (scan partly broken: read "cellam ... super nucem, ramis a stipite dilatatam")]. Rigauld says only "a cell ... on a height" [W-S2 p.78].
  4. Up there he gave himself to "holy meditation and devout prayers" [W-S1 n.30].
  5. One day, at the hour of the meal, he went down to the friars and was suddenly gripped by a violent illness. He lay down and soon lost all his strength [W-S1 n.30; W-S2 p.78].
  6. Not to burden the poor friars, he asked to be taken to Padua. The friars held back as long as they could, then agreed unwillingly. Rigauld adds that they put him in a carriage, "with tears and lamentations" [W-S1 n.30; W-S2 p.78].
- Details: Tiso later became Anthony's disciple in the Third Order and died 31 January 1234, and is buried at Campo San Pietro (Scardeone, quoted in Bollandist note g, p.201; a late source). The same note says a later tradition of six branches from the trunk "like a crown", and preaching to crowds from the tree; do not use.
- Words spoken: none direct on the hill. The Latin gives only indirect speech. Request to go to Padua: indirect.
- Inner life: the Latin Life says his "conversation was in heaven" and calls the cell a place of "sacred meditations and devout prayers" [W-S1 n.30]. Nothing in his own words.
- Testimony (Latin Life n.29, exact OCR copy): "Nam die quodain planitiein Padu^, situmque civitatis ipsius, a quodara colle prospiciens ; exul- tans in spiritu, raagnis e^ira , cjmite itineris au- diente. laudibus extoUere co^pit, grandique lionure in proximo dccorandara asseruit."
  plain: "One day he was looking over the plain of Padua and the site of the city from a hill. He rejoiced in spirit and began to praise the city greatly, while his travelling companion listened. He declared that it would soon be adorned with great honour."
- What changed: his last retreat starts; the illness at the meal begins the journey to death (SC-13).
- Not in the sources: his exact words on the hill (the Latin and Rigauld only report them); the day or month of the hill; the name of the companion; the date of the collapse. The Latin says "one day" (die quadam). I did not read a source that puts the collapse on 13 June itself. The outline's "at dinner on 13 June" is not confirmed by either Life. No food, no place of the table, and no weather.
- Sources: W-S1 nn.29-30, notes c, g, h, f (pp.201-202); W-S2 pp.77-78.

## SC-13 — Arcella, 13 June 1231
- Status: full
- When and where: Friday 13 June 1231 (sexta feria, die Junii tertio decimo, 1231) [W-S1 n.31]. The house of the friars who served the Poor Ladies (the Poor Clares) at Arcella, just outside Padua [W-S1 n.30; W-S2 pp.78-79].
- Who was there: Anthony; the friars around him; a friar who met the carriage on the road. Baring-Gould's name "Brother Roger" is not in either Life [W-S3 pp.187-188; check separately].
- What happened, in order:
  1. Carried in a vehicle (in vehiculo), near the city he met a friar on the road, who persuaded him not to go to the city friary, because of the crowds and noise, but to turn to the cell of the friars who served the Poor Ladies. He agreed [W-S1 n.30; W-S2 pp.78-79].
  2. "Not long after", as the illness grew, he showed clear signs of death. He made confession and received absolution [W-S1 n.31].
  3. He began to say the hymn "O gloriosa Domina". Then he raised his eyes and looked straight up for some time [W-S1 n.31].
  4. Asked what he saw, he answered "Video Dominum meum", "I see my Lord" [W-S1 n.31; W-S2 p.79].
  5. The friars brought the oil of anointing. He said he already had it within him, but that if they did it, it pleased him and was useful to him. He took it devoutly [W-S1 n.31].
  6. With the friars, he sang the penitential psalms to the end [W-S1 n.31].
  7. He lasted about half an hour more, and died "between the hands of those standing by, like one gently asleep" [W-S1 n.31].
- Details: Latin "dicere coepit" (he began to say) the hymn; the psalms are "decantavit" (sang). Rigauld: confession and Holy Communion as viaticum, then the hymn [W-S2 p.79]; he also sees the death on a Friday as a sign that he did not win martyrdom, so Christ's day took its place [W-S2 p.80].
- Words spoken: "Video Dominum meum" = "I see my Lord" (Anthony, to the friars; W-S1 n.31; W-S2 p.79). Latin: "Habeo unctionem hanc intra me: sed, etsi necesse non sit ut mihi hoc faciatis; verumtamen bene placet, et utile mihi est." plain: "I have this anointing within me. You do not need to do this for me. But it pleases me, and it is useful to me."

## SC-14 — The fight for the body, 13 to 17 June 1231, Padua
- Status: full
- When and where: from the death on 13 June until burial "on the fifth day" after his passing [W-S1 n.37] (that is 17 June). Arcella, then the roads and river of Padua, then the church of St Mary the Mother of God (S. Maria), beside the friars' house.
- Who was there: the friars and the Poor Ladies; the people of "Capo di Ponte" (Capite-pontis), a district; the Bishop; the Podesta (city governor); the Minister (the friars' head, arriving at evening on day 3); the citizens.
- What happened, in order:
  1. The friars meant to keep the death quiet, fearing the crowd. But children went through the city crying the news: "Mortuus est Pater Sanctus: mortuus est, inquiunt, Sanctus Antonius". The news ran into every corner at once [W-S1 n.32].
  2. The men of Capo di Ponte ran to the cell with a crowd of armed men, to keep the body. The friars asked for it, to bury it at St Mary's, where Anthony had chosen his burial. The men of Capo di Ponte stood in the way and would not let them touch it [W-S1 nn.32-33].
  3. The friars asked the Bishop. He ordered the Podesta to help. The men of Capo di Ponte resisted the Podesta too [W-S1 n.33].
  4. At midnight the crowd tried three times to break into the cell. They were "stupefied and struck with blindness" and could not enter even with the doors open [W-S1 n.34].
  5. Next day, people from nearby places pressed in. They held out belts, necklaces, and rings to be touched by the body. In the summer heat, the friars put the body in a wooden chest and in a shallow grave. A rumour then said the body was stolen. Everyone rushed to the cell "with swords and clubs" until they saw what had been done [W-S1 n.34].
  6. The Minister came at evening of the third day. The Podesta set a fine of 100 pounds against anyone who used violence. On the fourth day the Bishop gave judgement for the friars, because Anthony had chosen to be buried with them [W-S1 nn.35-36].
  7. The Podesta had a bridge of boats built across the river, so that the body need not cross Capo di Ponte. The Capo di Ponte men smashed the bridge and the other side armed. The friars and Poor Ladies were afraid and prayed. The Podesta then called the council and shut the ringleaders in one quarter under threat of heavy loss [W-S1 nn.36-37; W-S2 p.82].
  8. The Bishop and clergy, the Podesta with nobles and knights, and a great crowd went in procession to the cell. They lifted the body from the grave, and noblemen took turns to carry it. With hymns and praises, "with a great glow of lights", they came to St Mary's, where after Mass by the Bishop they buried him "on the fifth day" [W-S1 n.37].
  9. Cures began at the tomb that same day. The people of Capo di Ponte, men and women, were the first to come, barefoot, to throw themselves down at the tomb [W-S1 nn.38-39].
- Details: Rigauld has the same story, with the line that the crowd's zeal was "not to a coffin, but to a pearl of great price"; and "five days dead" and no smell of death in the summer heat, but a sweet perfume [W-S2 pp.81-83]. The Latin Life has the Poor Ladies sending messengers secretly to city leaders to keep him, since they had lost him alive [W-S1 n.33].
- Words spoken: the children's cry, Latin "Mortuus est Pater Sanctus: mortuus est, inquiunt, Sanctus Antonius", plain: "The holy father is dead! Saint Anthony is dead!" [W-S1 n.32; Rigauld p.81 has the same].
- Testimony (Latin Life n.32, exact OCR copy): "Adhuc euim omnes, pra^ter illos qui affiierant, id quod acciderat ignorabant ; cum protinus catervatim per civitatera pueri gradientes, sic vociferantes claiua- bant ; Mnrtuus est Pater Sanctus : mortuus est, inquiunt, Sanctus .Antonius. Qui rumor in omnes continuo civitatis angnlos decurrit. et totius aures populi uiox implevit."
  plain: "Everyone except those who had been there still did not know what had happened. Then groups of boys went through the city, shouting: 'The holy father is dead! Saint Anthony is dead!' This news ran at once into every corner of the city and soon filled the ears of all the people."
- What changed: the body is buried with the friars at St Mary's; the cures begin; Padua turns to the pope (see the outline's bridge).
- Not in the sources: how old the children were or who sent them; the number of armed men; the river's name; any death or wound in the fighting; day-by-day times. The Bishop's name is not given.
- Sources: W-S1 nn.32-39, pp.202-203; W-S2 pp.81-83.

## SC-15 — The tongue, 1263, Padua
- Status: full
- When and where: 1263; the shrine in Santa Maria Maggiore, Padua, at the translation of the body to the new church [C-S2 pp.197-198]. The basilica site gives 8 April 1263 (notes, facts only); another source gives 18 February; write "1263" only.
- Who was there: Bonaventure, Minister General of the friars (Rigauld calls him "Brother Bonaventure ... at that time Minister-General", W-S2 p.76); others "present" (W-S2 p.76). Coleridge also says the bones were moved "to the high altar of the new church" [C-S2 p.197].
- What happened, in order:
  1. The body had lain in the marble shrine in Santa Maria Maggiore until 1263. Bonaventure came to visit the shrine and made the translation "to promote an increase of devotion" [C-S2 p.197].
  2. On opening the shrine they found the bones disjointed, the flesh a mass of dust. But the skin of the head, the hair and the teeth were perfect, and the tongue was incorrupt and of its natural colour [C-S2 p.198].
  3. Bonaventure took it out with reverence, kissed it, and spoke [C-S2 p.198]. Rigauld: he showed it with great joy to all present [W-S2 p.76].
  4. He ordered it kept in a separate, costly reliquary [C-S2 pp.198-199].
- Words spoken: Coleridge gives direct speech (Bonaventure; no Latin given): "O blessed tongue, which always didst bless the Lord, and cause others to bless Him, now does it appear plainly how highly thou wert esteemed by God!" [C-S2 p.198]. plain: "Blessed tongue, you always blessed the Lord and made others bless him. Now it is plain how highly God valued you." Rigauld gives only the sense (no marks): Bonaventure said the tongue was proof of how faithfully the man of God had preached the truth [W-S2 p.76]. Use ONE version in the text, as the outline asks. Suggested: Coleridge's line, since it is direct speech. The Latin original of Bonaventure's words was not reachable.
- Testimony (Rigauld, exact English, W-S2 p.76; public-domain): "his tongue, which had spoken in every place the word of truth, was seen to be as fair and fresh, and ruddy, as if his body had only just been buried. On perceiving this, Brother Bonaventure showed it with great joy to all who were present, exclaiming that the tongue so miraculously preserved was a proof of how faithfully the Man of God had preached the immortal truth."
- What changed: the preacher's tongue becomes his best-known relic, kept in its own reliquary.
- Not in the sources: the hour or weather; the crowd's words or numbers; Bonaventure's tone beyond "great joy" (Rigauld) and "transport of devotion" (Coleridge); whether Bonaventure used Latin or Italian. Coleridge's claim that Bonaventure was then "Master of Theology in the University of Paris" should not be used; Rigauld says Minister-General. The 1981 exam (notes S5) found the body's remains in a chest and the vocal organs on display; not read in this session beyond the notes.
- Sources: C-S2 pp.197-199; W-S2 pp.75-76; notes-church-miracles for date conflict.

## FN-1 — Exulta Lusitania felix
- Fact: Pope Pius XII declared Anthony a Doctor of the Universal Church in the Apostolic Letter "Exulta, Lusitania felix", dated Rome, at St Peter's, 16 January 1946 (feast of the Franciscan protomartyrs), in his seventh year as pope. The decisive words are "Sanctum Antonium Patavinum, Confessorem, Ecclesiae Universalis Doctorem constituimus, declaramus" (we establish and declare Saint Anthony of Padua, Confessor, Doctor of the Universal Church). The title "Evangelical Doctor" comes from his frequent use of Gospel texts. The letter opens "Exulta, Lusitania felix; o felix Padua, gaude" (Rejoice, happy Portugal; O happy Padua, rejoice).
- Source: vatican.va, Latin text, https://www.vatican.va/content/pius-xii/la/apost_letters/documents/hf_p-xii_apl_19460116_antonio-di-padova.html (Holy See copyright; facts only; quotes above are short).

## FN-2 — The basilica: start and finish
- Fact: no source I reached gives one start date and one finish date. The Basilica's own page says it "was initiated in 1232" and its main part was "completed towards the end of that century" (Italian: iniziato nel 1232, compiuto sul finire del 1200). Its Messenger article says the friars began quickly: the building site was already active in 1238. The body was moved into the new church on 8 April 1263 (notes S5, from the basilica site; Coleridge says 1263). I found no source for the "1301" in the outline. Say: begun in the 1230s, body moved 1263, main part finished by the end of the 1200s.
- Sources (facts only): https://www.santantonio.org/en/basilica/virtual-tour ; https://www.santantonio.org/it/basilica/percorso-artistico ; https://www.santantonio.org/it/content/la-storia-delledificazione-della-basilica-del-santo-nel-messaggero-di-santantonio

## FN-3 — "Si quaeris" (the miraculous responsory)
- Fact: the Basilica of Padua says the responsory "Si quaeris" was composed by the friar Julian of Speyer, as part of the Officium rhythmicum S. Antonii, dated 1233, two years after the death; it is sung in the basilica on Tuesdays. Conflict: Coleridge (1883) says St Bonaventure wrote it and treats it as proof of Anthony's miracles in Bonaventure's time [C-S2 pp.208-210]. Follow the Basilica and write "Julian of Speyer, 1233"; mention no Bonaventure.
- Sources: https://santantonio.org/en/node/583 (facts only); C-S2 pp.208-210 (public-domain).
