## 3. Timeline (own-words view, in the order Augustine tells it)
- c.370 (age 16, "sixteenth year"): the pear theft with friends at night. Detail: he stole pears he did not want, and threw them away; "my only feast therein being my own sin." The tree stood near the family vineyard [S1 II.4.9, II.6.12; S2; S3]. Date "sixteenth year" is his own count; year not stated.
- Age 19: reads Cicero's Hortensius; it "changed my affections" and turned his prayers to God [S1 III.4.7; S3 III.4.7]. NEW: popular pages skip that Augustine says he read it for eloquence, not for faith.
- Death of a close friend at Thagaste. His grief: he marvels that others live when his friend is dead; "half of my soul" [S1 IV.6.11].
- Milan: he watches Ambrose read silently, eyes moving, "voice and tongue" still [S1 VI.3.3]. NEW detail for readers: the crowds that kept Augustine from speaking to Ambrose.
- Milan, 386: the garden scene. Weeping under a fig tree; a child's voice chants "tolle lege"; he reads Romans 13:13-14 and the doubt vanishes [S1 VIII.12.29; S2; S3].
- Milan, after baptism: he weeps at the hymns of the Church [S1 IX.6.14].
- Ostia, 387 (age 33): the window talk with Monica in the garden of the house; then her illness and death on the ninth day [S1 IX.10.23-IX.11.27; S2; S3 IX.10-12]. She asks only to be remembered at the altar.
- 391: as a presbyter he writes Letter 21 to Valerius: he asks for time "till Easter" for "praying, reading, and weeping" because he was never trained for the office [S5 section 4]. Date: 391 per S5.
- 413-426: City of God. Book XIV.28 gives the two cities; Book XIX.13 gives the definition of peace [S4].
- 5 May 418 (per search summary only): Sermo 256, "Canta et ambula," on the Alleluia [S8]. Year to check against a raw source.
- Pusey's header dates the Confessions "AD 401" [S2]. This is the translator's note, not Augustine's own claim.

## 4. Character (from his own text)
- Self-mockery: "Give me chastity and continency, only not yet" (his own joke on his past self) [S1 VIII.7.17].
- Fear of being healed too soon: "I feared lest Thou shouldest hear me soon" [S2; S1 VIII.7.17: timebam enim ne me cito exaudires].
- Honest about motives: the theft was done for the company as much as the fruit; "alone I had never done it" [S2; S1 II.8.16 to check number].
- Control and breakdown: at Monica's death his eyes "sucked back the fountain dry" by force of mind; he calls it a struggle [S3 IX.12.29]. The boy Adeodatus wept aloud; they hushed him [S3 IX.12.29].
- Humility about the office of bishop/priest: unfit "in boyhood or in the earlier years of manhood" to learn how to do it [S5 section 1].
- He admits he once judged the "sailors" before he learned their work [S5 section 2: "rashness of my censures"].
- Delight in singing: wept at hymns [S1 IX.6.14].
- He praises his mother's courage and quotes her words; no spin about her faults [S3 IX.11].
- Memory and time obsessions: on time, "If no one asks me, I know" [S1 XI.14.17].

## 5. Quotations (13 of max 25). Originals first; our English; locator; rights.

Q1. Latin: "fecisti nos ad te et inquietum est cor nostrum donec requiescat in te" [S1 I.1.1].
Our English: You made us toward yourself, and our heart is restless until it rests in you.
Pusey (S2): "for Thou madest us for Thyself, and our heart is restless, until it repose in Thee." Rights: public-domain.
Note: "ad te" is "toward you", not only "for yourself". Good for the writer.

Q2. Latin: "da mihi castitatem et continentiam, sed noli modo." [S1 VIII.7.17].
Our English: Give me chastity and self-control, but not yet.
Pusey: "Give me chastity and continency, only not yet." Rights: public-domain.
Context: a prayer of his youth, told from later regret; "not yet" is the point.

Q3. Latin: "sero te amavi, pulchritudo tam antiqua et tam nova, sero te amavi! et ecce intus eras et ego foris, et ibi te quaerebam" [S1 X.27.38].
Our English: Late have I loved you, beauty so ancient and so new, late have I loved you. You were within, and I was outside, and there I looked for you.
Pusey: "Too late loved I Thee, O Thou Beauty of ancient days, yet ever new!" Rights: public-domain.
Note: The common "Late have I loved you" is a modern wording; Pusey and Dods say "Too late".

Q4. Latin: "mecum eras, et tecum non eram." [S1 X.27.38].
Our English: You were with me, and I was not with you. Rights: public-domain.

Q5. Latin: "ego vero ... 'tolle lege, tolle lege.'" [S1 VIII.12.29]. Quote text: "audio vocem de vicina domo cum cantu dicentis et crebro repetentis, quasi pueri an puellae, nescio: 'tolle lege, tolle lege.'"
Our English: I heard a voice from the next house, singing and repeating again and again, as of a boy or a girl, I do not know which: "Take up and read, take up and read."
Pusey: "I heard from a neighbouring house a voice, as of boy or girl, I know not, chanting, and oft repeating, 'Take up and read; Take up and read.'" Rights: public-domain.
Detail: he first asks whether children sing such words in any game, and cannot remember any [S3 VIII.12.29].

Q6. Latin (Romans 13:13-14 as he read it) is in the same section; Pusey: "put ye on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make not provision for the flesh, in concupiscence. No further would I read; nor needed I" [S2; S3 VIII.12.29]. Rights: public-domain.

Q7. Latin: "ponite hoc corpus ubicumque. nihil vos eius cura conturbet. tantum illud vos rogo, ut ad domini altare memineritis mei, ubiubi fueritis." [S1 IX.11.27: Monica].
Our English: Put this body anywhere. Do not let care for it trouble you. I ask only this: remember me at the altar of the Lord, wherever you are.
Pusey: "Lay this body any where; let not the care for that any way disquiet you: this only I request, that you would remember me at the Lord's altar, wherever you be." Rights: public-domain.

Q8. Monica's other line, as Augustine reports it (paraphrase of what she said, in his text): "Nothing is far to God" [S2; S3 IX.11.28]. Latin not fetched for this line (grep "nihil longe" found no match; check Latin). Keep as English only until Latin is found.

Q9. Latin: "quid est ergo tempus? si nemo ex me quaerat, scio; si quaerenti explicare velim, nescio." [S1 XI.14.17].
Our English: What, then, is time? If no one asks me, I know. If I want to explain it to someone who asks, I do not know.
Pusey: "If no one asks me, I know: if I wish to explain it to one that asketh, I know not." Rights: public-domain.

Q10. Latin: "dimidium animae" (quoting a poet) and "nolebam dimidius vivere" [S1 IV.6.11].
Our English: half of my soul; I did not want to live as half a person. Rights: public-domain.
Note: S3 attributes "Thou half of my soul" to "one" who "said well of his friend"; the Latin says "quidam", a poet. Do not say whom (Horace) without a source.

Q11. Latin: "Fecerunt itaque civitates duas amores duo, terrenam scilicet amor sui usque ad contemptum Dei, caelestem vero amor Dei usque ad contemptum sui." [S4b XIV.28].
Our English: So two loves made two cities: the earthly city by love of self, even to contempt of God; the heavenly city by love of God, even to contempt of self.
Dods (S4): "two cities have been formed by two loves: the earthly by the love of self, even to the contempt of God; the heavenly by the love of God, even to the contempt of self." Rights: public-domain.

Q12. Latin: "Quomodo solent cantare viatores; canta, sed ambula; laborem consolare cantando, pigritiam noli amare; canta et ambula. Quid est 'ambula'? Profice, in bono profice." [S8, Sermo 256.3].
Our English: As travelers usually sing, sing, but walk. Comfort your labor by singing. Do not love laziness. Sing and walk. What does "walk" mean? Make progress, make progress in good.
Rights: public-domain Latin; our English.
IMPORTANT: this is genuine. See section 8 on "Sing and keep marching".

Q13. Latin: "Dilige et quod vis fac" (Tractate 7.8 on 1 John, from the Latin form given in a search summary; not raw-verified). Raw English, Browne 1888 (S7/wist): "Once for all, then, a short precept is given thee: Love, and do what thou wilt: whether thou hold thy peace, through love hold thy peace; whether thou cry out, through love cry out; whether thou correct, through love correct; whether thou spare, through love do thou spare: let the root of love be within, of this root can nothing spring but what is good."
Our English: Here is one short rule, given once: love, and do what you want. If you stay silent, be silent out of love. If you cry out, cry out out of love. If you correct, correct out of love. If you spare, spare out of love. Let love be the root. Nothing but good can grow from this root.
Locator: Homily (Tractate) 7, section 8, on 1 John 4:4-12 [S7]. Rights: public-domain. Nuance: see section 8.

Q14. Latin: "Gloria mea et exaltans caput meum" is a psalm line he quotes in XIV.28; not his own. Skip.

Q15. English only, On Christian Doctrine I.36.40: "Whoever, then, thinks that he understands the Holy Scriptures, or any part of them, but puts such an interpretation upon them as does not tend to build up this twofold love of God and our neighbor, does not yet understand them as he ought." [S6, Shaw]. Latin not fetched. Rights: public-domain English.
Our English: Whoever thinks he understands the Scriptures but gives them a meaning that does not build up love of God and neighbor does not yet understand them as he should.

Q16. City of God XIX.13, Dods (S4): "The peace of all things is the tranquillity of order. Order is the distribution which allots things equal and unequal, each to its own place." Latin not fetched. Rights: public-domain English.
Our English: The peace of all things is the calm of order. Order is the arrangement that gives each thing, equal or unequal, its own place.

Q17. Letter 21 section 4, Dods/Schaff (S5): "by praying, reading, and weeping" and "that a short time, say till Easter, be granted me". Latin not fetched. Rights: public-domain.
Our English: by praying, reading, and weeping. I ask that a short time, until Easter, be given me.

Q18. Letter 21 section 1, S5: "on the one hand, if the duties of the office of a bishop, or presbyter, or deacon, be discharged in a perfunctory and time-serving manner, no work can be in this life more easy, agreeable, and likely to secure the favour of men ... but none at the same time more miserable, deplorable, and worthy of condemnation in the sight of God". Rights: public-domain English. Use short.

Q19. Latin: "quantum flevi in hymnis et canticis tuis, suave sonantis ecclesiae tuae vocibus commotus acriter!" [S1 IX.6.14].
Our English: How much I wept at your hymns and songs, deeply moved by the voices of your sweet-sounding Church!
Rights: public-domain.

Q20. Latin: "sed cum legebat, oculi ducebantur per paginas et cor intellectum rimabatur, vox autem et lingua quiescebant." [S1 VI.3.3].
Our English: When he read, his eyes moved over the pages, and his heart searched for the sense, but his voice and tongue were at rest. Rights: public-domain.

Q21. Latin: "viluit mihi repente omnis vana spes, et immortalitatem sapientiae concupiscebam" [S1 III.4.7; text cut after "concupisce"; check end].
Our English: Every empty hope suddenly lost its worth to me, and I longed for the deathlessness of wisdom. Rights: public-domain. Verify the ending word in the Latin before use.

Q22. Latin: "intravi in intima mea duce te" [S1 VII.10.16].
Our English: I entered into my inmost self, with you as my guide. Rights: public-domain.

Q24. Pears: Latin "arbor erat pirus in vicinia nostrae vineae pomis onusta nec forma nec sapore inlecebrosis" [S1 II.4.9].
Our English: There was a pear tree near our vineyard, heavy with fruit, but neither its look nor its taste was tempting. Rights: public-domain.
Raw English (Pusey): "Fair were those pears, but not them did my wretched soul desire; for I had store of better, and those I gathered, only that I might steal. For, when gathered, I flung them away" [S2; also S3 II.6.12].

Q25. Pusey on the pear theft's company: "alone I had never done it" [S2; II.8.16 number to check].

## 6. Scenes (10)
1. The pear tree at night: a gang of boys shake fruit down and carry it off; he throws his share away [S2; S1 II.4.9]. Do not add other details (such as pigs) without reading the rest of II.4.9.
2. (No second scene from other reads.)
3. Carthage and Cicero's Hortensius: age 19, he takes up a book for eloquence and is changed [S1 III.4.7].
4. Death of the friend: a city turns into a place of horror; "my life was a horror to me" [S1 IV.6.11].
5. Ambrose reading in silence: crowds, Augustine and friends sit long in his room and watch him read without a sound, then leave [S1 VI.3.3].
6. The garden in Milan: tears under a fig tree, a child's chant, an open book, finger kept as a bookmark, a calm face; he tells Alypius [S2; S1 VIII.12.29].
7. Ostia window: Augustine and Monica alone at a window over the garden, talking of eternal life "very sweetly" [S2; S3 IX.10.23].
8. Monica's death: ninth day of sickness; her last request; Adeodatus weeps aloud and is hushed [S3 IX.11-12].
9. Letter 21: a priest asks his old bishop for time till Easter [S5].
10. Sermo 256: a bishop tells a crowd to sing the Alleluia while they walk [S8].

## 8. Other

### Misattributed or nuanced lines
- "Sing and keep marching": a form of "Canta et ambula" is genuine, Sermo 256.3 [S8]. The Latin is "canta, sed ambula ... canta et ambula". The right translation is "sing and walk" (ambula = walk). "Marching" is a loose paraphrase; the source image is travelers who sing to lighten the road, not soldiers. Extra phrases in Sermo 256.3 "noli errare, noli redire, noli remanere" (do not wander, do not turn back, do not stay behind): from S8 text "Noli errare, noli redire, noli remanere". Verdict: genuine, poorly translated. This corrects the brief, which listed it as wrongly credited.
- "Love, and do what you will": genuine, but not a general permission. Context is Homily 7 on 1 John, section 8: the example is about correction by a father who beats a boy for love versus a boy-stealer who "caresses" out of iniquity. Raw English (S7): "If you mark the persons, it is charity that beats, iniquity that caresses." and "deeds of men are only discerned by the root of charity." The Latin "Dilige et quod vis fac" is often quoted as "ama et fac quod vis" (wist.info note; secondary).
- "Pray as though everything depended on God; work as though everything depended on you": marked "Misattributed" on wist.info (S10); likely from later; not found in the Augustine texts I read. Do not use.
- "The world is a book, and those who do not travel read only a page": a search summary says it is a misattribution, perhaps from Fougeret de Monbron, Le Cosmopolite (1750). Secondary only; not verified in a raw source. Do not use.
- "Late have I loved you": a modern wording; the Pusey and Dods English say "Too late". Our English may use "late".
- "Lord, make me chaste, but not yet" is the popular form; the Latin has "da mihi castitatem et continentiam, sed noli modo" (Give me ... but not yet). Genuine.
- "Our hearts are restless until they rest in you": genuine, Conf. I.1.1.
- "Solvitur ambulando" ("it is solved by walking"): often credited to Augustine; I did not search for it. To check, no source key.
