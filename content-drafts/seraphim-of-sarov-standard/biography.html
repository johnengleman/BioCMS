Every Sunday a monk of Sarov carried a week’s food out to a hermit’s hut in the forest. In winter there was no road. He waded through snow up to his knees, sometimes in a blizzard. At the entry of the hut he said a prayer. Inside, the hermit answered “Amen” to himself and opened the inner door. He stood there with his arms crossed on his chest and his eyes on the ground. He did not bless the brother, or even look at him. On a small tray he laid only a scrap of bread or a little cabbage, to show what to bring next Sunday. Then the brother went back to the monastery without having heard one word. For about three years Saint Seraphim of Sarov lived like this. What passed between him and God in that silence, the brother never heard.1

He was a merchant’s son from Kursk, and from his youth he wanted one thing: to belong to God entirely. He looked for God in the forest, on a stone and in silence, as the desert hermits of the ancient Church had done. Yet he lived in an age when, as he once said, “they forbid us the desert.”2 He became one of the best-loved saints of the Russian Church. Today his remains rest in a convent at Diveyevo, a village about eight miles from Sarov, and the Church honors them as holy relics. The story of how he came to rest there begins with a promise he made at the bedside of a dying old man.3

Saint Seraphim Is Healed and Leaves Home

Saint Seraphim was born Prokhor Moshnin in Kursk in 1754, the youngest of three children. His father was a merchant named Isidor who built stone churches and houses. He had begun a large stone church in honor of Saint Sergius, but he died when the boy was about three. His widow, Agafia, took over the work and watched over the builders for years.4

When the boy was about seven, she took him up with her onto the unfinished building. While she looked over the work, he fell all the way to the ground. Agafia ran down, sure she would find him dead. He was standing on his feet without a scratch.5

At about ten he fell so ill that they gave up hope of his recovery. The illness cut short his first lessons in reading. In the worst of it he dreamed of the Virgin Mary. The Orthodox call her the Mother of God, or Theotokos, Greek for “the one who gave birth to God.” In the dream she promised to come and heal him. When he woke, he told his mother.6

Then one day the clergy of a Kursk monastery carried the city’s wonderworking icon of the Mother of God along the Moshnins’ street. It was the icon called the Sign. A heavy rain had turned the street into a muddy bog, so the clergy carried the icon through the Moshnins’ yard instead. Agafia saw it and was glad of the chance. She came out to meet the icon with her sick son in her arms and let him kiss it. Then the icon was carried over him. The boy was healed.7

The Mother of God had kept her promise, and something was lit in the boy that day. A burning love for Christ and His Most Pure Mother began in him, and it burned more and more as he grew. He went back to his books and came to love the Bible and the books of the Church.8

His elder brother Alexei kept a shop that sold harness and tar, rope and bark shoes, and the young man was put to work behind its counter. His heart was not in it. On Sundays and feast days he liked to read books about the faith, and sometimes he gathered his friends to listen.9

In the end he resolved to leave his home and property, his mother and brother and his native town, to give his life to God as a monk. When he was about seventeen, he asked his mother carefully whether he might go to a monastery. Agafia did not stand in his way. She had seen where his heart was turning, and in her own heart she agreed with him and was glad. Five merchant friends talked of coming with him. He also obtained his official release from the town society of Kursk, as Church and civil rules required.10

On the day he left, mother and son sat down together for a little while, by an old Russian custom. Then he stood up and prayed. He bowed down to her feet and asked for her blessing. Agafia let him kiss the icons of the Saviour and of the Mother of God. Then she blessed him with a copper cross.11

He took the cross with him as the sign of her blessing. He walked first to Kiev to pray at the relics of the saints, and a recluse there sent him on to a forest monastery called Sarov. That year in Kursk his mother finished the church his father had begun. Her son reached Sarov on 20 November 1778, the eve of the feast of the Entry of the Mother of God into the Temple. He would wear his mother’s copper cross openly on his chest for the rest of his life.12

The Sick Novice Gives Himself to God

Sarov stood in a great forest that ran for miles around it. Father Pachomius, its head, received the young man from Kursk and gave him to an elder named Joseph to train. The newcomer did a novice’s work: he woke the brothers for the services, baked the altar bread, and carved crosses out of cypress wood.13

In 1780 his body began to swell. The doctors could not tell what it was. They supposed it was dropsy, a sickness that fills the body with water. It lasted about three years, and for at least half that time he lay in bed in his cell. Pachomius and an elder named Isaiah took turns caring for him and hardly left his side. In time they came to fear for his life. Pachomius pressed him to send for a doctor, or at least to let himself be bled. The sick novice answered him:14

“Holy father, I have given myself to the True Physician of souls and bodies, our Lord Jesus Christ, and to His Most Pure Mother. If your love thinks fit, then for the Lord’s sake give me, poor as I am, the heavenly medicine: the Communion of the Holy Mysteries.”15

Saint Seraphim to Father Pachomius, 1780

It was his first great choice, and the whole of his life is already in it. He did not bargain with God for his body. He had given himself away, body and soul, and he left the rest to the One he had given himself to. Pachomius never spoke of medicine again. Joseph served an all-night vigil and a liturgy for the sick novice’s health, and after the liturgy Holy Communion was brought to him in his cell. He made his confession and received the Holy Mysteries, and then he was left alone. The next night God visited him. He recovered so quickly that the whole monastery wondered at it.16

He kept it a secret. Only later did he tell a few people what had happened after Communion. The Mother of God had come to him in a light beyond all words, with the apostles Saint John and Saint Peter. She turned to Saint John and pointed to the sick novice. “This one is of Our kin,” she said.17 One of those he told was Ksenia, a woman who looked after a church. She remembered how he told it:

“She laid her right hand on my head, my joy, and in her left hand she held a staff. With this staff, my joy, she touched poor Seraphim. And in that place, on my right thigh, a hollow formed, mother. All the water ran out into it, and the Queen of Heaven saved poor Seraphim. The wound was very big, and the pit is still there, mother. Look, give me your hand!” Mother Ksenia added: “And Father would take my hand himself and put it into the hollow, and it was so big that my whole fist went in!”18

Ksenia’s account of Saint Seraphim’s words

Of Our kin. The young man who had left his mother and brother in Kursk had been claimed by another family. On the spot where he had lain sick, the monks built a hospital with a church beside it. He went out to collect money for the church. With his own hands he made its altar out of cypress wood. The altar was blessed on 17 August 1786. For the rest of his life he received Communion in that church above all others, to remember what God had done for him there.19

Pachomius had made him a monk four days earlier, on 13 August 1786. The elder Isaiah stood with him as his sponsor and became his spiritual father. His new name was Seraphim, which means “fiery.” Soon afterward he was ordained a deacon, and for nearly six years he served at the altar almost without a break. He spent the nights before Sundays and feasts standing in prayer until the liturgy. At times during the services he saw angels serving and singing with the brothers. “My heart melted like wax,” he said later, “from joy that cannot be told.”20

At the liturgy on Holy Thursday he once saw the Lord Jesus Christ in glory. Christ came through the air from the west doors with the heavenly powers around Him “like a swarm of bees,” and He blessed those who served and those who prayed. Saint Seraphim could not move or speak. Two deacons led him into the sanctuary. He stood there without moving for about two hours, while his face went white as snow and then flushed. Afterward he told Pachomius and Joseph everything. The two old men kept it in their hearts and warned him never to think himself worthy of such a thing.21

Pachomius would hardly serve without him now, and he often took the young deacon along when he left the monastery. In June 1789 he set out with Isaiah and Saint Seraphim to bury a benefactor, and on the way they stopped at the village of Diveyevo. There an old widow, the nun Saint Alexandra, had gathered a small community of women. Before she became a nun, her name had been Agafia, like Saint Seraphim’s mother. She was ill and knew that she was dying, and she asked the elders to anoint her. Then she gave Pachomius all her savings for her sisters and begged him not to abandon them.22

Pachomius was honest with her. He would pray for her and help her sisters as long as he lived, he said, but he was old and weak. “But here is the monk-deacon Seraphim,” he told her. “You know his spirit, and he is young. He will live to see it. Entrust this great work to him.” So Saint Alexandra begged Saint Seraphim not to leave her community. One day the Queen of Heaven herself would tell him what to do.23 She died on 13 June while they were still away. They came back just in time to bury her. It rained so hard all that day that no one stayed dry. After the burial Saint Seraphim did not even stay for the meal. He walked back to Sarov alone, twelve versts or about eight miles.24

In 1793 he was ordained a priest. Years of standing in church had taken their toll: his legs swelled, and sores opened on them. By rights he should have asked for a bed in the new hospital. In the autumn of 1794 Saint Seraphim went to Pachomius and asked for the forest.25

Saint Seraphim Prays on a Stone

Pachomius was seventy and ill, and he knew he was dying. He gave Saint Seraphim his blessing to go, the last blessing he would ever give him. Saint Seraphim remembered how the old man had once cared for him in his own illness, and now he nursed Pachomius in turn. One day he saw that something more than pain was troubling the old man.26

“Holy father, what are you grieving about?” he asked.

“I am grieving for the sisters of the Diveyevo community,” said Pachomius. “Who will watch over them after me?”27

Saint Seraphim promised that after Pachomius was gone, he would watch over the sisters himself and care for them just as the old man had. The promise comforted Pachomius and made him glad. He kissed Saint Seraphim one last time, and soon afterward he fell asleep in the Lord. Saint Seraphim wept bitterly for him. Pachomius had loved him like a son and had led him through every step of the monastic life.28

Isaiah was now the abbot, the head of the monastery. With his blessing, Saint Seraphim went into the forest on 20 November 1794. It was sixteen years to the day since he had come to Sarov. His hut stood about five versts from the monastery, a little over three miles away. There he lived on his own vegetables and the Gospel, and he came in to the monastery only for Sundays and feasts.29

He knew what he had chosen. He used to say that a monk in a monastery fights the enemy as if fighting doves, but a hermit in the desert fights lions and leopards. And he liked to say: “They forbid us the desert, but our first father, John the Forerunner, was a hermit.”30

Even in the forest, he was offered honors. In 1796 the Church authorities wanted to make him head of a monastery in the town of Alatyr. He begged Isaiah to turn it down for him, and another Sarov monk went instead.31

In February 1800 his mother Agafia was buried by her parish priest in Kursk, far away. She was in her seventies. To the end of his life her son asked others to remember his parents, Isidor and Agafia, in their prayers.32

In those years he set himself a new labor. Saint Symeon the Stylite had prayed for decades on top of a pillar, and Saint Seraphim decided to imitate him on a stone. In the deep forest, halfway between his hut and the monastery, lay a huge block of granite. At night he climbed onto it so that no one would see him, and he stood or knelt there from evening until morning with his hands raised to heaven. Over and over he prayed the prayer of the tax collector in the Gospel: “God, be merciful to me a sinner.” He kept a second stone in his cell, and on it he prayed from morning until evening. He stepped down only to eat the food a brother brought him, or when his strength gave out.33

For a thousand days and a thousand nights he kept this up. His body changed under it. The hair on his head matted like felt, and the disease in his legs came back and never left him again. And he hid it all so well that no one guessed. Later a bishop of Tambov wrote privately to Abbot Nifont of Sarov to ask about him. Nifont’s draft reply was kept in the monastery papers. In it the abbot wrote that the brothers knew of his life and labors. But of his secret deeds, “and of the standing of 1,000 days and nights on a stone, no one knew.”34

Only near the end of his life did he tell a few of the brothers. One of them was amazed and said that it went beyond human strength. Saint Seraphim reminded him of the saint he had imitated: “That ascetic stood forty-seven years on a pillar. Are my labors anything like his?” The brother pressed him. Surely he had felt the help of grace? Saint Seraphim answered:35

“Otherwise human strength would not have been enough. I was strengthened and comforted within by that heavenly gift which comes down from above, from the Father of Lights.” Then, after a pause: “When there is tenderness of heart, God is with us.”36

Saint Seraphim to a brother of Sarov

In the end he came down from the stone himself, because he saw that it would wear out his spirit as well as his body. He said later that it is foolish to wear out the body by our own will until the spirit is worn out too, even for the sake of virtue. His back was already bent, because a tree he was cutting down had fallen on him. Now other people began to come out to the hut, women among them. To three peasants of the district, it meant that the hermit must have money.37

Robbers Beat Him and He Forgives Them

On 12 September 1804 he was chopping wood in the forest when three men he did not know, dressed as peasants, came up to him and demanded money. “Worldly people come to you and bring money,” they said. “I take nothing from anyone,” he answered. They did not believe him. One of them rushed at him from behind to throw him down, but it was the robber who fell. That frightened them for a moment. Then they came on again.38

Saint Seraphim was a strong man, and he had an axe in his hands. For an instant the thought flashed through his mind that he could defend himself and might well succeed. Then he remembered the words of the Saviour: “All who take the sword will perish by the sword.” He did not want to resist. He set the axe down on the ground and crossed his arms on his chest. “Do what you need to do,” he said gently. He had resolved to suffer innocently for the Lord’s sake, whatever came.39

One of them picked up the axe and struck him on the head with the butt, so hard that blood gushed from his mouth and ears. He fell senseless. They dragged him toward the entry of his hut and beat him all the way there with the axe, a stick, their fists and their feet. They talked about throwing him into the river. When he lay as if dead, they tied his hands and feet, left him in the entry, and rushed into the hut to find his riches. They broke open the stove and pulled up the floor. All they found was an icon and a few potatoes. Then fear came over them, and they ran.40

When he came to, he worked his hands and feet free. He thanked the Lord that he had been found worthy to bear wounds for Him. Then he prayed that God would forgive the men who had tried to kill him. He spent the night in pain in the wrecked hut. The next day he made his way to the monastery with great difficulty, and he came in during the liturgy. His hair and beard were matted with blood and dirt, several teeth were knocked out, and his ears and mouth were caked with blood. His clothes had dried onto his wounds. The brothers were horrified and asked what had happened. He said nothing to them. He asked only for Abbot Isaiah and the confessor, and he told the two of them everything.41

For eight days he could not eat, drink, or sleep for the pain, and no one at Sarov expected him to live. On the seventh day Isaiah sent to Arzamas for doctors. They found his skull broken, his ribs smashed, and his chest trampled, and they wanted to bleed him. Once again Saint Seraphim asked the abbot to leave his life to God and to the Mother of God, “the true and faithful physicians of souls and bodies.” He began to recover, and he told one brother later that he had been healed through the prayers of the Mother of God.42

He stayed about five months in the monastery. Then he asked to go back to the forest. Isaiah begged him to stay in the monastery for good, since such an attack could happen again. Saint Seraphim answered that such attacks were nothing to him. He feared only the robbers who could harm the soul.43

Meanwhile the three men were found. They were serfs from the village of Kremenki. Saint Seraphim had already forgiven them, and he begged the abbot not to punish them. He wrote to their landowner with the same request. “Otherwise,” he said, “I will leave Sarov and go away to other holy places, where no one will know me.” Not long afterward a fire burned down the robbers’ houses. They came to him in tears and begged for his pardon and his prayers.44

The beating bent his back further still. From then on he walked stooped over, leaning on a small axe, a hoe or a stick.45

His Spiritual Father Dies, and He Falls Silent

In 1806 Isaiah grew too weak to lead the monastery, and he asked to step down. The brothers chose Saint Seraphim to take his place. Once again he refused, and they elected the treasurer Nifont. Isaiah lived on for a year, ill the whole time. He could no longer walk the miles out to the forest, but he did not want to lose his talks with Saint Seraphim. So the brothers took their old abbot out to the hut in a little cart, and Saint Seraphim sat with him and comforted his last days. Isaiah died on 4 December 1807, in his sixty-seventh year.46

The loss struck Saint Seraphim to the heart. His first teacher Joseph had long been in his grave, and so had Pachomius. Now the last of the three men who had raised him was gone. For the rest of his life he prayed for them, and whenever he passed the monastery graveyard, he went in and knelt at their graves.47

After Isaiah’s death he stopped speaking. He took as his models two hermits of the old Church who were known for their silence, Saint Arsenius the Great and Saint John the Silent. If visitors came to his hut, he did not come out. If he met someone in the forest, he fell on his face and stayed there until the person had gone by. Toward the end of those three years he stopped coming to the monastery even on Sundays. Each Sunday the brother came out to him with his food and the little tray.48

Some of the brothers were sorry to lose him and asked why he lived shut away when he could teach them. He answered them in the words of Saint Isaac the Syrian: “Love the leisure of silence more than feeding the hungry of the world.”49

The bowed head and the tray were only the outside of his silence. Inside it he was trying to give up every worldly thought, so as to give himself to the Lord entirely. “When we remain in silence,” he said, “the enemy, the devil, can do nothing against the hidden man of the heart.” Silence, he taught, brings tenderness and meekness and at last peace of soul. It draws a person close to God and makes him like an angel on earth.50

But his legs had never recovered from the years on the stone. They could no longer carry him to the monastery, and he had stopped coming for Communion. Nifont called a council of the senior monks. They decided to put a choice to him: either come to the monastery on Sundays and feasts, as before, or move into a cell inside the walls for good. The brother who brought the food carried the message out. Saint Seraphim listened in silence and sent him away without a word. The next Sunday the brother came back with the food and said it all again. This time Saint Seraphim blessed him and walked back to the monastery beside him.51

It was 8 May 1810, and he had lived in the forest for fifteen years. That evening he stood at the vigil in the cathedral, and the brothers were amazed. On the feast of Saint Nicholas the next morning he received Communion at the early liturgy in the hospital church. Then he asked Nifont for his blessing. He went into his old cell and shut the door.52

Fifteen Years Behind a Shut Door

To give up his own will, he took nothing into the cell, not even what anyone would call necessary. There was an icon with a lamp before it, and a tree stump to sit on. Sacks of sand and stones served as his bed, and he never lit the stove. Under his shirt he wore a large iron cross hung on ropes over his shoulders.53

Brother Pavel from the next cell brought him his food: oatmeal and chopped white cabbage, with water to drink. Pavel set the dish down at the door and said a prayer. Then Saint Seraphim came out, covered with a large linen cloth so that no one should see him. He knelt, took the dish onto his knees as if he were receiving food from the hands of God, and carried it inside. Afterward he set the dish out again and hid his face once more. On some days he did not come out at all. Those were his days of fasting, and Pavel carried everything away.54

From Monday to Thursday he read a whole Gospel each day, from Matthew through John, and every day he made a thousand bows to the ground. Sometimes he stood before the icon reading nothing, bowing not at all, “only contemplating the Lord with the mind in the heart.” On Sundays and feasts Holy Communion was brought to him in his cell.55

For five years he let no one in. A bishop of Tambov came to Sarov to see him, and still he kept his vow of silence. Nifont offered to lift the door off its hooks, but the bishop would not allow it. “We might commit a sin,” he said, and he went away. A week later the governor of Tambov came with his wife to ask for a blessing. This time Saint Seraphim opened the door himself and blessed them. It was the autumn of 1815.56

From then on his door stood open. The brothers began to come to him, and then lay people, and he blessed them. But he himself did not cross the threshold of his cell for ten more years.57

In the entry of the cell stood one thing more. He had asked for a coffin to be made, so that he would never forget the hour of his death. It was hollowed, body and lid, out of a single oak trunk. It stood unpainted in the entry, and he often prayed beside it. “When I die,” he said to the brothers, “I beg you, brothers, put me in my coffin.” He sat in his cell “as in a tomb, like a living dead man.”58

The Mother of God Sends Him Out

By 1825 his body was worn out. He suffered from violent headaches, and he asked the doctors what to do. Open a vein, they said, or go out into the fresh air. He had turned doctors away all his life, so he chose the air. But he had made it a rule never to do anything without some sign from above. So he laid the matter before God in prayer and asked Him to show His will.59

That same year the sisters of Diveyevo began coming to his cell. Then their head, Ksenia Mikhailovna, came herself. Their community had grown, and the rule they had taken from the monks of Sarov was too hard for them. He begged Ksenia Mikhailovna to give it up for a lighter one. “Listen to me, my joy!” he said. But she would not hear of it. “No, father, let it stay as it was,” she said. “Father Pachomius set us up.” He let her go and was at peace. He thought that what Saint Alexandra had laid on him no longer lay on his conscience, or else that God’s hour had not yet come.60

The answer to his prayer came on 25 November 1825, the feast of Saint Clement of Rome and Saint Peter of Alexandria. As he slept, the Mother of God appeared to him in a vision with those two saints. She allowed him to leave his cell and go to the forest. That day he walked out of his enclosure and into the woods for the first time in fifteen years.61

He was making his way through the thickets along the river Sarovka toward his old hut. Near the riverbank, below the spot where there had once been an old well, there was now only a bog. There he saw the Mother of God. The apostles Saint Peter and Saint John stood behind her on a rise. She struck the ground with her staff, and a spring of clear water burst up out of the earth like a fountain. Then she spoke to him:62

“Why do you want to abandon the commandment of My servant Agafia, the nun Alexandra? Leave Ksenia with her sisters. But the commandment of this servant of Mine you must not abandon. Strive to fulfil it entirely, for she gave it to you by My will.”63

The Mother of God to Saint Seraphim, 25 November 1825, as he told it to Nikolai Motovilov

She would show him another place in Diveyevo, she said. He was to take eight sisters, whom she named, and start a new community there for girls alone, beginning with a windmill of Sarov timber. On the very day he left his cell, the Mother of God called him back to the promise he had made at Pachomius’s bedside thirty-one years before.64

He built a new hut by the spring, about two versts from the monastery. From then on he went out to the forest nearly every day to cut wood and hoe his potatoes. He went to and fro bent over, in a worn white linen smock and a shabby monk’s cap, with an axe or a hoe in his hand. On his back he carried a heavy sack of sand and stones, with the Gospel inside it. When someone asked him why, he answered in the words of Saint Ephraim the Syrian: “I torment him who torments me.” The brothers often heard him singing an old hymn of the Church: “Those who live in the desert, apart from the vain world, have a ceaseless longing for God.” He sang it from his heart, in a rapture of spirit.65

Now the people came, more of them every day. After the early liturgy in the hospital church, he went back to his cell with a crowd behind him. In his last years, two thousand people or more passed through that cell in a single day. He received them all alike, the poor man in rags and the rich man in bright clothes. He kissed each one with love and bowed down to the ground before them, and when he blessed many lay people he kissed their hands. Each one he greeted with joy: “Little father! Little mother! My joy!”66

On the feast of the Conception of the Mother of God, 9 December 1825, he prayed with some of the Diveyevo sisters in the farther of his two huts. From that day he set to work on the new community. The man who had hidden his face under a linen cloth now had a family of poor girls to feed.67

A Healed Landowner Gives Everything Away

He had a helper for the work. During the years in his cell, a landowner named Mikhail Mantirov had been brought to his door. Mantirov had served in the army in Livonia and had married Anna, a Lutheran woman from there. Then he fell ill with a disease the best doctors could not name, and pieces of bone came out of his legs. His serfs brought him with great difficulty into the entry of the cell.68

Saint Seraphim came out. “What brings you here?” he asked. “To look at poor Seraphim?” Mantirov fell at his feet. Three times Saint Seraphim asked him, “Do you believe in God?” Three times Mantirov said yes. “My joy!” said Saint Seraphim. “If you believe so, then believe also that to the one who believes, all things are possible from God. So believe that the Lord will heal you too. And I, poor Seraphim, will pray.”69

He sat Mantirov down beside the oak coffin in the entry and brought out holy oil. “By the grace given me by the Lord,” he said, “you are the first I heal!” He anointed the sick man’s legs and pulled coarse linen stockings onto them. Then he filled the skirts of Mantirov’s coat with dried bread and told him to carry it to the guesthouse. Mantirov walked there and found that he was well. When he came back and fell at the old man’s feet, Saint Seraphim lifted him up and spoke sharply. “Is it Seraphim’s business to kill and make alive?” he asked. “Give thanks to the Lord Almighty and to His Most Pure Mother!”70

Some time later Mantirov came back. “My joy!” Saint Seraphim greeted him. “We did promise to thank the Lord for giving us back our life!” Mantirov did not know how. “What do you tell me to do?” he asked. Then Saint Seraphim looked at him in a special way. He said cheerfully: “Here, my joy, give the Lord everything you have, and take poverty on yourself of your own free will!”71

Mantirov was shaken. A thousand thoughts ran through his head. He thought of the rich young man in the Gospel, and of his own young wife. Saint Seraphim read his thoughts. “Leave everything,” he said, “and do not worry about what you are thinking of. The Lord will not leave you, in this life or the next. You will not be rich, but you will always have your daily bread.” The young man in the Gospel had gone away sad. Mantirov answered at once: “I agree, father! What will you bless me to do?”72

He sold his estate and freed his serfs. Saint Seraphim told him to keep the money for now, and to buy only about forty acres at Diveyevo, on a spot he showed him. Mantirov was never to sell that land, and he was to leave it to the old man’s community when he died. He settled there with Anna and lived in want. His friends laughed at him and his wife reproached him, but he did nothing without the old man’s blessing. Saint Seraphim called him by the pet name Mishenka and left the whole business of Diveyevo in his hands.73

The sisters began with the mill. Through 1826 Saint Seraphim cut posts and timber in the forest with the sisters’ help, and on 7 July 1827 the mill began to grind. He sent six sisters to live beside it.74

Early one spring morning a sister went out to the cellar and saw a candle burning in the ground behind their hut. She ran back and woke the others. When they all rushed out, the candle was gone. In the morning light they found the earth at that spot freshly dug. With Saint Seraphim’s blessing the sisters then spent three years digging a ditch around the place of the vision.75

He watched over them closely, and with humor. He kept the widows apart from the girls: “In a community it is easier to manage seven maidens than one widow.” When a sister named Paraskeva lost heart and decided to go back into the world, he sent for her and talked about his own life as a monk. “I, mother, have gone through the whole of monastic life,” he told her again and again, “and never, even in thought, have I left the monastery.” When he had finished, she felt as if a diseased limb had been taken away from her. She was at peace.76

In 1827 he told Mantirov that their poor little community had no church of its own. He said it pleased the Queen of Heaven that they should have one, built onto the porch of the Kazan church in Diveyevo. Saint Alexandra had stood praying on that porch so often that her tears had washed it all. “So, my joy,” he said, “you build this church to the Nativity of her only-begotten Son, for my orphans!” Mantirov’s money paid for it.77

The Monks of Sarov Turn Against Him

Not everyone at Sarov rejoiced. For some of the monks he was “hard to look at.” Once some of the brothers had been sorry that he shut himself away. Now one brother told him that people of both sexes were bothering him, and that he let everyone in without any distinction. Saint Seraphim answered with the example of Saint Hilarion the Great, who would not let his door be shut against strangers. Suppose he closed his door, he said, and people thirsty for a word of comfort begged him in God’s name and went away grieving. What answer could he give at the Last Judgment?78

Someone told him outright that some people took offense at him. “I am not upset,” he said, “that some people profit from me and others take offense.” With those who disliked him he kept the peace, and he treated them gently.79

The sisters knew the hardest part of it, because they were the cause. He sent them honey and linen, oil and candles. The abbot and the treasurer were hurt that he gave the sisters so much. One day he handed a heavy sack to a sister named Evdokia and told her to go out by the main gate of the monastery. “Don’t be afraid of anyone,” he said. Two soldiers on guard seized her and took her to Abbot Nifont, who made her untie the sack. Her hands shook as she did it. Inside were old bark shoes, broken crusts of bread, wood chips and stones. “Ah, Seraphim, Seraphim!” cried the abbot. “Look at him! He torments himself, and he torments the Diveyevo girls too!” And he let her go. The next time the sack held sand and stones, and again she was marched to the abbot. When she told Saint Seraphim, he said: “Well, mother, this is the last time. Go and don’t be afraid. They won’t touch you any more.” And so it was.80

Nifont was no villain. He was a God-fearing man who loved his brothers and guarded the rule of the house. But Evdokia remembered what those years had cost the old man:81

“Everyone knows how the men of Sarov disliked our father Seraphim on our account. They even drove him and persecuted him for our sake all the time, and they gave him much to bear and much grief! And he, our dear one, bore it all good-naturedly. He even laughed, and often, knowing it himself, he joked with us.”82

Sister Evdokia of Diveyevo

He did laugh a good deal. A sister who had been sent away from her community knelt before him in tears. He laughed and clapped his hands. Then he prayed and found her a new home at the mill. Another sister once found flies crawling on his face and blood running down his cheeks. She wanted to brush them off. “Don’t touch them, my joy,” he said. “Let everything that has breath praise the Lord!”83

Why did he hold so firmly to the sisters, whatever it cost him? He told two monks who had found fault with him. Once he had asked God for a sign that his care for the sisters pleased Him: a hundred-year-old tree, marked with a cross, was to bow down. The next morning the tree lay on the ground torn out by its huge roots, though the air was perfectly still. “You and many others reproached me for this,” he told the monks, “asking why I busy myself with them.” He told them he cared for the sisters out of obedience to his elders Pachomius and Isaiah. They had promised to look after the sisters until they died, and they had charged the monks of Sarov never to abandon them.84

In September 1831 four men carried a young landowner named Nikolai Motovilov to the spring by the Sarovka, while a fifth held up his head. He had been nearly paralyzed for more than three years. They set him down beside a great pine. He begged Saint Seraphim to heal him. “But I am not a doctor,” said the old man. Did Motovilov believe that the Lord could still heal with one word, as He had in the Gospel? He did. “Well, if you believe,” said Saint Seraphim, “then you are already well!” He sent the servants away and took Motovilov by the shoulders. Then he lifted him off the ground and stood him on his feet. “Stand firmer,” he told him. “Don’t be afraid. You are completely well now!”85

Saint Seraphim and Motovilov Talk in the Snow

It was a Thursday in November 1831, and the day was grey. Snow lay about seven inches deep on the ground, and thick grains of snow were still sifting down. The clearing lay close to the nearer of Saint Seraphim’s two huts, across from the river Sarovka, at the foot of a hill that came close to the bank. He sat Motovilov on the stump of a tree he had just cut down, and he squatted on his heels in front of him.86

“The Lord has shown me,” the old man began, “that ever since you were a boy you have wanted to know the aim of our Christian life.” It was true. The question had given Motovilov no rest since he was twelve. He had put it to many churchmen, and their answers had never satisfied him.87

“The true aim of our Christian life,” Saint Seraphim told him, “is to acquire the Holy Spirit of God.” Prayer, fasting, vigils and every other good work had their worth, but they were only the means to that aim. To acquire is to gain, he said, and people in the world spend their lives gaining money. The Holy Spirit is capital too, a capital of grace that lasts forever. Then he spoke of his own life. “I come from Kursk merchants,” he said. “Before I was in the monastery, we used to trade in the goods that brought us the most profit.” A Christian should trade the same way and spend himself on whatever brings the most grace. And one trade was open to everyone, always: prayer. It was open “to rich and poor, to noble and simple, to strong and weak, to healthy and sick, to righteous and sinner.”88

Motovilov listened to it all. Then he said that he still did not understand. How could he be certain that he was in the Spirit of God? How could anyone know? Saint Seraphim said he had already explained it in detail. “What more do you need?” he asked. “I need to understand it well,” said Motovilov.89

“Then Father Seraphim took me very firmly by the shoulders and said to me: ‘We are both in the Spirit of God now, you and I. Why don’t you look at me?’ I answered: ‘I can’t look, father, because lightning is pouring from your eyes. Your face has become brighter than the sun, and my eyes ache with pain.’ … Imagine, in the middle of the sun, in the full dazzle of its noonday rays, the face of a man who is talking with you. You see his lips move and the look in his eyes change, you hear his voice, you feel someone holding you by the shoulders. But you do not see those hands, you do not see yourself or him, only a blinding light that spreads several fathoms all around and lights up with its brightness the snow lying on the clearing and the snow grains falling on me and on the great elder.”90

Nikolai Motovilov

“Do not be afraid,” Saint Seraphim told him. “You have become just as bright as I am. You are in the fullness of the Spirit of God yourself now, or you could not see me like this.” Then he bent his head close and whispered in his ear. He had not even made the sign of the cross, he said. He had only prayed silently in his heart: “Lord, make him worthy to see clearly, with bodily eyes, the coming down of Your Spirit.” And the Lord had granted “the humble request of poor Seraphim” at once. “Why don’t you look me in the eyes?” he asked. “Look simply, and don’t be afraid. The Lord is with us!”91

Then he began to question the young man like a teacher who already knows the answers. “What do you feel now?”

“Uncommonly well!”

“Well how? What exactly?”

“I feel such quiet and peace in my soul that I cannot put it into words!” said Motovilov. And what else? An uncommon sweetness. An uncommon joy in his whole heart. And what else?92

“An uncommon warmth!”

“Warmth, father? Why, we are sitting in the forest. It is winter out here. There is snow under our feet and more than an inch and three-quarters of snow on us, and more is falling. What warmth can there be?”93

Motovilov told him it was the warmth of a bathhouse when water is thrown on the hot stones and the steam pours up. Did it smell like a bathhouse too? No, nothing on earth smelled like this. When his mother was alive, he used to go to balls, and she would sprinkle him with perfume from the best shops in Kazan. Even that had no such fragrance.94

Saint Seraphim smiled. “I know it just as well as you do, father,” he said. “I am asking you on purpose.” Then he pointed out that the snow was not melting, either on them or under them. So the warmth came from within the two of them and not from the air.95

Through the whole talk the light did not fade. The old man stayed just as he was, squatting in the snow. Motovilov wondered whether God would let him remember this mercy always as clearly as he felt it now. Saint Seraphim believed He would:96

“It is not for you alone that you have been given to understand this, but through you for the whole world, so that you yourself may be made firm in God’s work and be of use to others. … The Lord seeks a heart filled with love for God and for neighbor. That is the throne on which He loves to sit, and on which He appears in the fullness of His heavenly glory.”97

Saint Seraphim to Nikolai Motovilov, November 1831

Saint Seraphim Says Goodbye

His cell was full of candles now. Large ones and small ones burned in heaps on round trays before the icons, and over the years the dripping wax had built up into little hills. The heat in the cell was hard to bear. Motovilov wondered why, and the old man answered before he could ask. Many people brought him oil and candles and asked for his prayers, he said. There were too many names to repeat at every point of his rule of prayer, so he lit a candle before God for each of them. Then he prayed: “Lord, remember all those people, Your servants, for whose souls I, poor one, have lit these candles and lamps to You.”98

About a year before his death his strength began to fail. He went out to his forest hut less often, and on some days he could not receive visitors. People stayed on at the monastery for days and waited for him.99 He seemed to be setting his house in order. In January 1831 he had told the visiting abbot Saint Anthony (Medvedev) that God would one day put a great monastery in his care. “Do not forsake my orphans when your time comes,” he told him. And he asked: “Remember my parents Isidor and Agafia in your prayers.” A visitor bound for Kursk asked whether he had a message for his brother and his relatives there. Saint Seraphim smiled and pointed to the icons of the Saviour and the Mother of God. “Here are my family,” he said. “To my living relatives I am already a living dead man.” But to some merchants from Kursk he gave another message for his brother. “Tell him I pray for him to the Lord and to His Most Pure Mother day and night.”100

In 1832 an officer named Ladyzhensky came to Sarov with his sisters before leaving on a mission to China. They found Saint Seraphim in the forest, sitting by his spring. He talked with the officer for half an hour, went into his hut, and came back with half of a small loaf blessed at the liturgy. “Here,” he said, “from my soul.” Then he added, as if with sorrow: “We will not see each other again.” The officer promised to come back the next day. “We will not see each other again,” said the old man. The officer said he would stop at Sarov on his way home. “No,” Saint Seraphim said for the third time. “We will not see each other again.”101

A few months before the end, a brother came to see him. Saint Seraphim gave him some advice, and then he said: “Blow on the candle.” The brother blew, and the flame went out. “That is how they will not see me either,” said the old man. He told Brother Pavel next door that his end was coming soon. Pavel was a simple man, and he could not tell whether the old man meant his own end or the end of the world.102

On Christmas morning 1832, a government official named Bogdanov found him sitting on the floor of the hospital church before the early liturgy, and asked to talk with him. “Later, later,” said Saint Seraphim, and hurried into the sanctuary. After the service he blessed Bogdanov and his daughter and gave them both dried bread. Afterward Bogdanov came to the cell with his questions written out beforehand. Should he stay in government service or retire to his village? How should he teach his children? What should he read? The old man told him to read four passages of the Gospel every day, and the Book of Job. And for the times when people treated him badly: “If they scold, do not scold back. If they persecute, bear it. If they slander, praise.”103

“During our whole talk Father Seraphim was extremely cheerful. He stood leaning on the oak coffin he had prepared for himself, and he held a lighted wax candle in his hands.”104

Bogdanov, of his visit on Christmas Day 1832

When Bogdanov left, the old man thanked him for visiting “his poverty.” He tried to kiss Bogdanov’s hand and bowed to the ground before him. He gave him dried bread to share with the men who worked under him. “Go with God!” he said. “This bread is fresh. It has just come out of the oven.”105

That same Christmas he received Communion at the liturgy Nifont served. Afterward he asked the abbot to look after the younger brothers, and for the last time he asked to be laid in his own coffin. To another monk he gave a small enamel icon of Saint Sergius that Saint Anthony had sent him. “Put this on me when I die,” he said, “and lay me in the grave with it.” Before the new year he went out to the Dormition cathedral and measured his own grave at the side of its altar. It was the spot he had marked with a stone after he came out of his enclosure.106

Saint Seraphim Reposes at Prayer

On Sunday 1 January 1833 he came for the last time to the hospital church. Its altar was the one he had made with his own hands. He went round himself and lit a candle before every icon and kissed each one. The brothers had not noticed him do this before. Then he received Communion. After the liturgy he said goodbye to every brother there. He blessed each one and kissed him. “Save yourselves,” he told them. “Do not lose heart. Stay awake. Today crowns are being prepared for us.” He kissed the cross and the icon of the Mother of God, walked once around the altar, bowed, and went out through the north door. Everyone could see how weak his body had become. But in spirit he was alert, calm and joyful.107

Then a sister from Diveyevo came to see him. The sisters had run out of everything, and he sent two hundred rubles back with her to buy them bread. A priest-monk named Theoktist was visiting from another monastery. “You will serve here,” Saint Seraphim told him. Theoktist said he had to leave for home that day. “Well, then,” said the old man, “you will serve at Diveyevo.”108

Brother Pavel saw him go out three times that day to the place he had marked for his grave and stand there a long while, looking at the ground. That evening Pavel heard him singing the hymns of Easter, though it was the first day of January. He sang “Having beheld the Resurrection of Christ…”, “Shine, shine, O new Jerusalem…”, and “O great and most holy Pascha…”. Pavel had sometimes warned him that the candles he left burning in his cell might start a fire. The old man always gave the same answer: “While I live there will be no fire. When I die, my death will be revealed by a fire.”109

At about six the next morning, Pavel stepped out of his cell to go to the early liturgy and smelled smoke in the entry. He said the customary prayer at Saint Seraphim’s door and knocked, but there was no answer, and the door was hooked shut from the inside. He went out onto the porch and called to the monks passing in the dark: “Fathers and brothers! There is a strong smell of smoke. Is something burning near us? The elder must have gone out to the forest.” A novice threw himself at the door and tore it off the hook. Inside, a heap of linen and books lay smouldering on a bench by the door. There was no flame. The brothers ran for snow and smothered it. The cell was dark, and there was no sound from the old man. They thought he was resting after his night of prayer.110

The early liturgy went on in the hospital church. While they were singing the hymn to the Mother of God, a boy slipped in and whispered the news to a few of the brothers, and many came running. Pavel and a novice named Ioann felt their way through the little dark cell, and they found him:111

“They brought a lighted candle and saw that the elder, in his usual white smock, was on his knees at his usual place of prayer before the icon of the Mother of God of Tenderness. His head was bare, a copper crucifix hung on his neck, and his arms were crossed on his chest. They thought he had fallen asleep, and they began gently to wake him. But there was no answer. The elder had finished his life of struggle. His eyes were closed, and his face was alive with the thought of God and with prayer. His body was warm, as if his spirit had left its dwelling only that moment.”112

From the account of Saint Seraphim’s death in Elagin’s Life, 1863

Saint Seraphim had died on 2 January 1833. When the brothers later went through his cell, they found that the book in front of which he had died was a little scorched. On the road home Theoktist’s sleigh broke down for no reason he could see, and he had to stop at Diveyevo. The news had reached the sisters, and he found them in tears. They begged him to serve a memorial service for their father. So he served at Diveyevo after all. The sister sent to buy bread with the old man’s money was on her way back with it when she heard the news. Without stopping at home, she drove her horse straight to Sarov. The sisters wept as if nothing could comfort them, because their father had not found anyone to take his place with them.113

The brothers carried his body to a neighbor’s cell, washed his forehead and his knees, and dressed him as a monk. Then they laid him in the oak coffin that had stood in his entry for so many years, with the icon of Saint Sergius, as he had asked. For eight days the coffin stood open in the Dormition cathedral, and thousands came from the country around to kiss him goodbye. On the day of the funeral the church was so crowded that the candles around the coffin went out from the heat. Abbot Nifont buried him on the right side of the cathedral’s altar, in the place Saint Seraphim had measured. No speeches were made.114

The Church Glorifies Saint Seraphim

The people he loved kept faith with him. Mantirov kept the Diveyevo land “like the apple of his eye,” as the old man had told him, even when the general whose estates he managed demanded it. For refusing he was thrown out with nothing, and he and Anna set off on foot for Diveyevo, begging their way. With not even a crust of bread left, he stood in Moscow and poured out his grief before an icon of the Mother of God. On his way back he found a few coins scattered in the road. On 7 July 1858 at Diveyevo he received Communion in the church he had paid for. Afterward he went out into his garden. He felt a sudden tiredness, sat down on a bench, and gave his soul to God. He was sixty. They buried him on the left side of his church, where the old man had told him to pray that he might lie: “Here the ground is holy. The little feet of the Queen of Heaven passed here!”115

Motovilov went to Kursk in the autumn of 1834, to gather memories of the old man’s childhood and to buy two thousand apple trees for an orchard at Diveyevo. Saint Anthony became head of a great monastery, the Trinity-Saint Sergius Lavra, as he had been told. He took in monks from Sarov and did not forget the orphans. Under his roof the first Lives of Saint Seraphim were written, the books that tell his story. The first was printed in 1841. Its author was a monk named Sergius, who had lived some fifteen years at Sarov under the old man’s guidance. In 1861 a chapel was built over the grave, and the same year the community at Diveyevo became a convent.116

Pilgrims kept coming to the grave, and many received healings through his prayers. From 1892 to 1894 a Church commission examined ninety-four cases, most of them backed by sworn statements. In 1902 Emperor Saint Nicholas II made it known that he wanted the Church to finish its work of glorifying Saint Seraphim. In the Church’s word, to glorify a saint is to declare him a saint. The chief government official over the Church had long disapproved. In January 1903 bishops examined the remains and found bones, not a body kept whole. The Holy Synod, the council that governed the Russian Church, sent the emperor its report. On 26 January he wrote on it that he had read it “with a sense of true joy.” Three days later the Synod glorified Saint Seraphim. It ordered his memory to be kept on 2 January, the day he died, and on the day his relics would be uncovered.117

That day came on 19 July 1903, during celebrations at Sarov that drew a great crowd. One count put the pilgrims at more than a hundred thousand, and another at two or three hundred thousand. His relics were laid in a new coffin and carried in procession, and the emperor was one of the men who carried them. That summer a Cossack widow named Anna Ilyeva was immersed in the spring at Sarov. She had been blind for nineteen years, and the blindness fell from her eyes like scales. The same year his talk with Motovilov in the snowy clearing, found among Motovilov’s papers, was printed for the first time. It has been read ever since. The people of Russia had taken him to their hearts. Seventeen years later the new Soviet state opened his shrine.118

His Holy Relics Come Home to Diveyevo

On 17 December 1920 the local Soviet had his shrine opened. Its report described a figure of cotton wool in brocade and silk, with slippers set with amethysts, and old dark brown bones inside the cotton wool. In 1922 the relics were taken away to a museum in Moscow. In 1926 the Bolsheviks shut the Sarov monastery, and in 1927 they closed the Diveyevo convent. In 1946 it vanished from the maps and became a secret city for the building of nuclear weapons.119

Among the nuns turned out of Diveyevo in 1927 was Margarita, then twenty-eight. Before he died, Saint Seraphim had given the sisters of Diveyevo a candle. He told them that with it they would one day receive him back. The candle passed from one generation of sisters to the next, and in the end it came into her hands.120

In the autumn of 1990 a set of unknown remains, with no inventory number, turned up in the storerooms of a museum of religion in Leningrad. With them was a pair of mittens. One was embroidered “Venerable Father Seraphim,” and the other “Pray to God for us.” In December a Church commission examined the remains and recognized them as his. On 11 January 1991 the relics were handed back to the Church, and in February thousands followed them to a cathedral in Moscow.121

On 28 July 1991 a procession set out from Moscow with his relics. On 1 August it reached Diveyevo, where a great crowd of people met him. By the old calendar, it was the day his relics had first been uncovered. Mother Margarita was ninety-two, the last nun still living from the old convent. She lit the candle he had given the sisters, and his orphans received their father home.122

A Note on the Sources

Dates before 1918 follow the Julian calendar then used in Russia. For texts on azbyka.ru and other web pages, the paragraph numbers in the notes follow the text as it appears on that page. His gravestone and the early Lives give his birth as 1759, but the Church now uses 1754, from Kursk parish records, and we follow it. The early Lives differ in places. Hieromonk Sergius has Saint Seraphim in his garden when the robbers came, and Elagin has him cutting wood. Sergius says the boy was healed at once under the icon, and Elagin says he recovered from that day; only Elagin tells of the dream before it. Elagin writes that in the 1780s, while collecting money for the hospital church, Saint Seraphim came to Kursk and found that his mother had died. A Kursk parish register records her burial in February 1800, as an article of 2002 quotes it. We follow the register. Elagin also tells of a second vision of the Mother of God after the beating of 1804, much like the vision of 1780; we tell the vision once. Sergius counts seventeen years of enclosure from 1809; Elagin dates the move into the monastery to 1810, and we count fifteen years. The early Lives give no year for the healing of Mikhail Mantirov. The Conversation with Motovilov was found among Motovilov’s papers by Sergei Nilus and first printed in 1903. Motovilov alone recorded it, and its authenticity has been debated. We leave out the well-known story of Saint Seraphim feeding a bear. It rests on the telling of one sister and one monk. A note in the manuscript department of a Moscow library says that the sister, before she died, said she had been taught to tell it. The famous saying “Acquire a peaceful spirit, and thousands around you will be saved” appears in later collections; we did not find it in the early Lives.

How This Life Was Researched

This life draws on 23 sources in Russian and English. At its center are three Russian Lives now in the public domain. Hieromonk Sergius lived about fifteen years at Sarov in Saint Seraphim’s time and wrote the first Life, printed in 1841. Nikolai Elagin compiled the fullest, in 1863, from the Sarov archive and the accounts of many named witnesses. Saint Seraphim Chichagov’s Life of 1903 and his chronicle of the Diveyevo convent draw on the memories of the Diveyevo sisters and on Motovilov’s notes. Other sources include Motovilov’s Conversation, the Holy Synod’s act of glorification of 1903, and modern studies of the early Lives and of the relics. The quotations from Russian are our own translations.
