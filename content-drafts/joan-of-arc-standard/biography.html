On the morning of Thursday 22 February 1431, a prisoner was brought into a room at the end of the great hall of the castle of Rouen. She was a farm girl of about nineteen. Her judge was the bishop of Beauvais, and forty-eight learned churchmen sat with him to hear her. The day before, she had complained that they kept her fastened in chains and irons.1

The bishop ordered her to swear once more that she would tell the truth about everything they asked. “I swore yesterday,” she said. “That should be enough.” He pressed her: not even a prince could refuse to swear in a matter of faith. “I swore to you yesterday,” she answered, “and that should be quite enough for you. You burden me too much!” In the end she swore to tell the truth about matters of the Faith, and no more.2

A well-known teacher of theology was to ask the questions. Before he began, she warned him. “You may well ask me some things I will answer truthfully, and some I will not. If you were well informed about me, you would wish to have me out of your hands. I have done nothing except by revelation.” He asked whether she had learned a trade as a girl. Yes, she said. She had learned to sew and to spin, and at sewing and spinning she feared no woman in Rouen.3

Two years before, this spinner had raised the siege of the city of Orléans. She had stood with her banner near the altar at Reims while her king was crowned. Yet she had learned her prayers from her mother, and she could not read. Before that morning's questions were over, she told them where it had all begun. It began in her father's garden at noon on a summer day, when she was about thirteen.4

Saint Joan Grows Up in Domrémy

Her father was Jacques d'Arc, and her mother was Isabelle. They were farm people in the village of Domrémy, good Catholics with a good name among their neighbors. At home their daughter was called Jeannette. She spun and sewed and helped in the house, and sometimes she went out with her father to the plow.5

One of her friends was Hauviette, a girl three or four years younger. Hauviette often slept at the d'Arc house, out of love for her friend. On a Sunday in Lent each spring, the two girls walked out with the other children of the village to an old tree they called the Ladies' Tree. They carried small loaves, ate them under the branches, danced, and drank from a spring nearby.6

She was cheerful and liked to see a happy face. When poor people came to the house, she gave them her bed and slept on the floor herself. She went to church gladly and often, to Mass and to the evening prayer. The sexton who rang the church bell learned how much the evening bell meant to her. When he did not ring it, she scolded him and told him it was not well done. Then she promised him wool if he would take care to ring it.7

The village noticed her prayer, and not always kindly. People told her she went to church too much, and their words often made her shy. When she was out in the fields and heard the church bell ring, she knelt down where she was. Sometimes at play she drew a little apart from the others to speak with God. Some of the children she played with laughed at her.8

Saint Joan Hears a Voice in the Garden

On that February morning in Rouen she told her judges how it had started, and a clerk wrote her words down in Latin:

“She also said that when she was thirteen, she had a voice from God to help her guide herself. The first time she was very much afraid. The voice came about noon, in summer, in her father's garden. She heard it on her right side, toward the church, and she seldom heard it without a light.”9

The record of her trial at Rouen, 22 February 1431

After she had heard it three times, she knew it was the voice of an angel. It taught her to be good and to go to church. Later she told her judges who it was. It was Saint Michael. She saw him before her eyes, with the angels of heaven all around him. “I saw them with my bodily eyes as well as I see you,” she said. “When they went from me, I wept. I would have liked them to take me with them.”10

From then on she wanted more than anything to do what God asked of her through the Voice, and to stay in His grace. The first time she heard the Voice, she promised God to keep her virginity for as long as it pleased Him. Later Saint Catherine and Saint Margaret came to her as well, and she knew them by the greeting they gave her. “There is not a day when I do not hear this Voice,” she told her judges, “and I have great need of it. But I have never asked it for any reward except the salvation of my soul.”11

Then the Voice began to ask more. Two or three times a week it told her that she must leave and go into France, and that she could not stay where she was. It told her to raise the siege of Orléans. That city lay on the river Loire, and an English army was camped around it. The English had taken many of the good towns of France. The king she would serve was Charles. He had not yet been crowned, and people still called him the Dauphin.12

The Voice told her to go to Robert de Baudricourt, the captain of the town of Vaucouleurs. He would give her men to take her to the Dauphin. “I answered that I was only a poor girl,” she said, “who knew nothing about riding or fighting.”13

Saint Joan Leaves Home for Vaucouleurs

She said nothing about it to her parents. She was afraid above all that her father would stop her. Her mother had told her about a dream he had had, in which their daughter went off with soldiers. He told her brothers that if he thought the dream would come true, he wanted them to drown her. If they would not, he said, he would drown her himself.14

When she was about sixteen, Durand Laxart came to Domrémy to fetch her. He was married to a relative of hers, and she called him her uncle. She passed her father's house with him and said, “To God! I am going to Vaucouleurs.” Hauviette had not known that her friend was leaving. When she heard, she wept a great deal, because she loved Saint Joan for her goodness and was her friend.15

Long afterward a judge asked her whether she had done right to leave without her father's and mother's permission. She said that she had obeyed them in everything else. But God had commanded this, so it had to be done. If she had had a hundred fathers and a hundred mothers, she said, she would still have gone. Some time later she wrote to her parents, and they forgave her.16

In her uncle's house at Burey she told him what she meant to do. She would go to the Dauphin in France and have him crowned. Then she asked him a question. Had it not been said long ago that France would be ruined by a woman, and later restored by a virgin? She asked him to go to Robert de Baudricourt and tell the captain to send her to the Dauphin.17

Her uncle took her to Vaucouleurs around Ascension in 1428. The captain was not moved. More than once he told Laxart to take her back to her father's house and give her some slaps. The captain asked her who her lord was. “The King of Heaven,” she said. Then she left the town with her uncle.18

Near the start of Lent in 1429 she came back, and this time she stayed. She lodged three weeks with a woman of the town and spun there, as she had at home. Baudricourt refused her again. When she saw that he would not send her to the Dauphin, she took her uncle's clothes and said she meant to leave. But the third time she went to the captain, he received her.19

The people of Vaucouleurs bought her a man's clothes and boots. Her horse cost twelve francs. Two of Baudricourt's men agreed to take her to the king. As she left, the captain told her: “Go, and let come what may.”20

Saint Joan Meets the King at Chinon

The ride took eleven days, through country held by the English and their Burgundian allies. She rode in a man's clothes with her small escort. On the way she stopped at the church of Saint Catherine at Fierbois and heard three Masses in one day. Early in March 1429 she reached the castle of Chinon, where the king was.21

Charles did not want to see her. He kept her waiting two days before he let her in. When she came into the room where he was, she recognized him among many others. She said that she knew him by the counsel of her Voice.22

The duke of Alençon was a prince of the king's own blood. He was out at Saint-Florent hunting quail when a servant brought him the news. A girl had come to the king, saying that God had sent her to drive out the English and raise the siege of Orléans. The next day the duke rode to Chinon and found her talking with the king. As he came near, she asked who he was. The king told her he was the duke of Alençon. “You are very welcome,” she said. “The more of the blood of the king of France there are together, the better.”23

The next morning she came to the king's Mass and bowed when she saw him. He took her into a private room with the duke and one other lord and sent everyone else away. There she asked the king for several things. Above all, she asked him to give his kingdom to the King of Heaven. She said that after that gift, the King of Heaven would do for Charles what He had done for the kings before him. He would set Charles back in his former state. After dinner the king walked in the meadows, and she ran with a lance. The duke saw how she handled it, and he gave her a horse.24

Before the king would trust her with an army, churchmen had to question her. For about three weeks learned men at Chinon and at Poitiers asked about her Voices and her faith. She lodged in a tower of the castle of Chinon, and a page of fourteen or fifteen stayed with her through the day. He often saw her on her knees in prayer, and sometimes she was weeping.25

“I know neither A nor B,” she told two of the examiners when they came to her lodging. A friar who spoke the dialect of the Limousin asked her what language the Voice spoke. “A better one than yours,” she said. He asked if she believed in God. “Truly, more than you do!” He told her that God did not want her to be believed without some sign. “In God's name,” she answered, “I have not come to Poitiers to show signs. Send me to Orléans, and there I will show you the signs I was sent for.” One of the churchmen said in public that they saw nothing in her but what can be in a good Christian and a true Catholic.26

On 22 March she dictated a letter to the English, which began with the names “Jesus Maria”. In the name of the King of Heaven, she called on them to give up the good towns they had taken in France and go home. At Tours the king had armor made for her. She also had a white banner made. Lilies were scattered over the cloth, and the world was painted on it, with an angel on each side. Later she told her judges that she loved her banner forty times more than her sword. She had never killed a man, she said. When she went against the enemy, she carried the banner herself, so that she would not kill anyone.27

Saint Joan Brings Help to Orléans

At the end of April an army gathered at the town of Blois with a convoy of food for Orléans. When it set out, priests walked ahead of the soldiers behind a banner of their own. They sang the Veni Creator, the hymn to the Holy Spirit. She told the men to put their trust in the Lord and to go to confession. Her page saw her receive Holy Communion among them. “By my martin,” she promised, “they will be well led. Have no doubt of it.”28

The commander inside Orléans was Dunois, known as the Bastard of Orléans. He had already sent two men to the king to learn about this girl, whom people were already calling the Maid. Now the army came up along the south bank of the Loire, and he crossed the river to meet it. Things looked bad. To bring the food into the city, boats had to sail upstream past an English fort at the church of Saint-Loup. The wind was dead against them, the boats were few, and the army looked too weak to force a way past the English.29

She went straight to the point. “Are you the Bastard of Orléans?” she asked. “I am,” he said, “and I am glad you have come.” She asked whether he was the one who had advised them to come by this side of the river, instead of going directly to where Talbot and the English were. He said that he and men wiser than he had given that advice, because they thought it safer. “In God's name,” she answered, “the counsel of God our Lord is safer and wiser than yours. You thought you could deceive me, and you deceive yourselves more. I bring you better help than ever came to any knight or city, because it is help from the King of Heaven. It does not come out of love for me. It comes from God Himself, who has taken pity on the city of Orléans at the prayer of Saint Louis and Saint Charlemagne. He would not let the enemy hold the lord of Orléans and his city.”30

Suddenly, almost in an instant, the wind turned and blew for them. The sails went up, and the boats carried the food past Saint-Loup in spite of the English. Dunois had seen the wind change, and he had seen the food come into the city against a stronger English army. From these two things he judged that what she did came from God rather than from man. From that hour he had more hope in her than before.31

He asked her to cross the river and come into the city with him. She did not want to. Her soldiers had confessed their sins, and she would not leave them. So the captains took the army back to Blois to cross the river there, and she agreed to go in. She entered Orléans beside Dunois, with the French captain La Hire, carrying her white banner.32

Saint Joan Is Wounded and Orléans Is Freed

On 4 May fighting began outside the walls while she was up in her room. She came down at once and turned on her page: “Ah, you wretched boy! You did not tell me that French blood was being spilled!” At the city gate she met men carrying in a badly wounded Frenchman, and she said she never saw French blood without her hair standing on end. That day the French took the English fort at Saint-Loup.33

In the days that followed, the French took more English forts on the far bank. Then came the Tourelles, which guarded the south end of the city's bridge. At seven in the morning on Saturday 7 May she had the trumpets sounded and carried her banner to the edge of the ditch. Early in the fight an arrow struck her between the neck and the shoulder and went in half a foot deep. She did not leave the field, and she took no medicine for the wound. The assault went on all day. By about eight in the evening there was almost no hope of winning, and Dunois wanted to pull the tired army back into the city.34

“Then the Maid came to him and asked him to wait a little longer. She mounted her horse and rode off alone into a vineyard, well away from the crowd. There she prayed for a short while. When she came back from that place, she took her banner in her hands at once and stood on the edge of the ditch. At that moment the English shuddered and were afraid.”35

Dunois, the Bastard of Orléans, sworn testimony of 1456

By sunset the man who carried her banner was worn out, and he had handed it to a soldier called the Basque. Her steward Jean d'Aulon thought the soldiers would follow if the banner went forward. He asked the Basque to follow him. Then he went down into the ditch to the foot of the earthwork, covering himself with his shield. When she saw her banner in another man's hands, she thought it was lost. She seized it by the end and shook it. “Ah, my banner! My banner!” she cried. “Ah, Basque!” shouted d'Aulon. “Is this what you promised me?” The Basque pulled the banner free, and at that the whole army rallied and took the earthwork in front of the fort.36

That evening the French took the Tourelles, and the English captain Glasdale and other English leaders fell into the river and drowned. That same day d'Aulon had heard her say, “In God's name, we will enter the city tonight by the bridge.” And that night the French came into the city by the bridge. There her wound was dressed. She ate four or five pieces of bread dipped in wine mixed with a great deal of water, and nothing else that day.37

Early on Sunday 8 May the English came out of their camps and drew up in a line of battle. She rose from her bed and put on only a coat of mail. Then she forbade anyone to attack them. They must be allowed to go. The English marched away, and no one went after them. Orléans came to keep the eighth of May as a feast each year, with a Mass, a sermon, and a procession.38

The duke of Alençon once heard her tell the king what lay ahead. She had four tasks. She must drive out the English, have the king crowned at Reims, free the duke of Orléans from the English, and raise the siege of Orléans. They must use that year well, she said. She would last a year, and little more.39

Saint Joan Brings the King to Reims

In June the army went out under the duke of Alençon against the English towns along the Loire. Before they left, the duke's wife had been afraid for him. He had lately been a prisoner of the English, and his ransom had cost a great deal. “Lady, do not be afraid,” Saint Joan told her. “I will give him back to you safe, and in as good a state as he is now, or better.”40

The first town was Jargeau. After some days the captains met, and the heralds cried out for the assault. “Forward, gentle duke, to the assault!” she said. He thought it was too early. “Do not doubt,” she answered. “The hour is ready when it pleases God. Act, and God will act.” Then she asked him a harder question. “Ah, gentle duke, are you afraid? Do you not know that I promised your wife to bring you back safe and sound?”41

During the assault she told him to move from the place where he stood, or a gun in the town would kill him. He moved. A little later a lord named du Lude was killed on that very spot, and the duke was filled with fear and wonder. She herself climbed a ladder with her banner in her hand. The banner was struck, and a stone broke into pieces on her light helmet and threw her to the ground. She got up and called to the soldiers: “Friends, friends, up, up! Our Lord has condemned the English. At this hour they are ours. Take heart!” The town was taken at once.42

Once her page saw a Frenchman strike an English prisoner on the head so hard that the man was close to death. She got down from her horse and had the Englishman make his confession. Then she held his head and comforted him.43

On 18 June the English were beaten near the village of Patay, and their captain Talbot was taken prisoner. The army turned toward Reims. With the army she kept one habit every day. At twilight she went into a church and had the bells rung for about half an hour. She gathered the begging friars who followed the king's army and had them sing a hymn to the Virgin Mary while she prayed.44

Charles was crowned in the cathedral of Reims on Sunday 17 July 1429. Her banner stood near the altar, and for a while she held it there herself. Later a judge asked her why her banner had been brought into the church, rather than the banners of the other captains. “It had been in the toil,” she said, “so it was right that it should be in the honor.”45

That same day she had a letter sent to the king's enemy, the duke of Burgundy. She asked him to make a good and lasting peace with the king. “Forgive one another with a good heart, fully, as loyal Christians ought to do,” she wrote. “And if you want to make war, go against the Saracens.” Later in the letter, she begged him “with joined hands” not to make war against the king's side.46

In August the king rode on from Reims. At La Ferté and at Crépy-en-Valois the people came out to meet him, shouting “Noël!” for joy. She rode between the archbishop of Reims and Dunois, and she said to them: “Here is a good people. I have never seen a people so glad at the coming of so noble a king. How I wish I might be so happy, when my days are done, as to be buried in this land!” The archbishop asked her, “Joan, where do you expect to die?” She answered:47

“Where it pleases God, for I am no more sure of the time or the place than you are. And I wish it pleased God my Creator that I might now go away, lay down my arms, and go to serve my father and mother, keeping their sheep with my sister and my brothers, who would be so glad to see me!”48

Saint Joan of Arc, on the road from Reims, August 1429

But the road did not lead home. It led to Paris.49

Saint Joan Fails to Take Paris

Paris was held by the English and their Burgundian allies. The French attacked on Thursday 8 September 1429, the feast of the birth of Our Lady. She said later that she had no revelation to go there. She went because some gentlemen of the army wanted to attack, and they asked her to. At about eight in the morning the army left its camp at La Chapelle in good order. She went with two lords to the Saint-Honoré gate, and she was among the first to go down into the ditch with her banner.50

The fight was long and hard, under heavy fire from the guns. After sunset a crossbow bolt went through her thigh. She called all the more for the men to come up to the walls, and said the place would be taken. The lord of Gaucourt and others took her out of the ditch against her will. “By my martin,” she said, “the place would have been taken.”51

Early the next morning, wounded as she was, she got up and asked the duke of Alençon to sound the trumpets. She would not leave until she had the city. The king had stayed behind at Saint-Denis, and now his messengers came and called her back to him. She and most of the company were very unhappy, but they obeyed. They still hoped to cross the river Seine by a bridge the duke had built near Saint-Denis, and to come at Paris from the other side. The king learned of their plan, and in the night he had the bridge broken.52

Her Voice had told her to stay at Saint-Denis, and she wanted to stay. The lords took her away against her will. On 13 September the king left Saint-Denis for the Loire. When she saw that nothing could change his mind, she laid all her armor before the image of Our Lady and the relics in the abbey of Saint-Denis. Then she followed the king in great sorrow.53

At the town of Gien the army broke up. The duke of Alençon went home to his wife and his lands, and Saint Joan stayed with the king. She grieved at the parting, and most of all at losing the duke, whom she loved dearly. Soon afterward he asked the king to let her ride with him into Normandy, because many men would follow him if she came. The men who ran the king's household and his war would not allow it. The duke never had her with him again.54

She spent three weeks in the town of Bourges, lodging with a woman who shared her bed almost every night. Saint Joan went often to confession and asked to be taken to the early morning prayers. She gave freely to the poor, and she said she had been sent for the comfort of the poor and the needy. When women brought their rosaries for her to touch, she laughed. “Touch them yourselves,” she said. “They will be just as good from your touch as from mine.”55

At the town of Saint-Pierre-le-Moûtier the French fell back from the wall. D'Aulon had been wounded in the heel and was on crutches, and he found her nearly alone there. She took off her helmet and told him she was not alone. She had fifty thousand of her people with her. He could see only four or five men. Then she called out, “Brushwood and hurdles, everyone, to make a bridge!” and soon after the town was taken. In December the king made her and all her family nobles.56

In Easter week of 1430 she was in the trenches before the town of Melun when her Voices told her what was coming. “You will be taken before Saint John's Day,” they said. “It must be so. Do not be troubled. Accept it, and God will help you.” She begged that she might die soon after she was taken, without suffering long in prison. They told her to accept it all, but they did not tell her the hour. From then on she talked more often with the captains about the war, but she did not tell them what her Voices had said.57

In May the enemy came against the town of Compiègne. She left the town of Crépy about midnight with three or four hundred fighting men. Her men told her they were too few. “By my martin, we are enough,” she said. “I will go and see my good friends of Compiègne.”58

Saint Joan Is Captured and Sold

They reached Compiègne at sunrise and slipped into the town. On Monday 23 May 1430 she rode out over the bridge with her company against the Burgundians camped in the meadow. Twice she drove them back as far as their camp, and the third time halfway there. Then the English came up and cut the road between her men and the earthwork that guarded the bridge, and her company began to fall back.59

Her men begged her to get back into the town, or they would all be lost. She was angry. “Be quiet, all of you!” she said. “It depends only on you whether they are beaten. Think of nothing but striking them.” They would not listen, and they forced her back toward the bridge. The captain of the town saw the crowd of enemies about to reach his bridge. Fearing to lose the town, he had the bridge raised and the gate shut. She was left outside with a few of her men.60

She fought hard, but five or six men took hold of her at once, some of her and some of her horse. Each of them shouted, “Yield to me and give me your faith!” She answered, “I have sworn and given my faith to someone other than you, and I will keep my oath to him.” Her brother Pierre was taken with her. She was led away to the lodging of Jean de Luxembourg, a lord who fought for Burgundy.61

Her Voices had always told her that she must be taken prisoner, but they had not told her the day. “If I had known the hour when I would be taken,” she said later, “I would never have gone out of my own free will. But in the end I would always have obeyed their commands, whatever happened to me.”62

Jean de Luxembourg kept her at his lodging for three or four days and then sent her to a castle called Beaulieu. Her steward served her there in prison, as he had before she was taken. One day he said to her, “This poor town of Compiègne, which you have loved so much, will now fall into the hands of the enemies of France.” “It will not,” she answered. “None of the places that the King of Heaven has brought back to the gentle King Charles through me will be taken back by his enemies, as long as he takes care to guard them.”63

In the months after she was taken, the king and his counselors had even less will to fight for the kingdom than before. They began to look instead for a peace with the English and the duke of Burgundy.64

Later she was moved to the tower of the castle of Beaurevoir. In that tower two pieces of news reached her. All the people of Compiègne were to be put to fire and sword, down to the children of seven. And she herself had been sold to the English. She told her judges what passed between her and her saints:65

“Saint Catherine told me almost every day not to leap, and that God would help me, and the people of Compiègne too. I said to Saint Catherine, ‘Since God will help the people of Compiègne, I want to be there.’ Saint Catherine said to me, ‘You must accept it gladly, without fail. You will not be set free until you have seen the king of England.’ I answered her, ‘Truly, I do not want to see him. I would rather die than fall into the hands of the English.’”66

Saint Joan of Arc, to her judges, 14 March 1431

She asked her saints, “How can God let these good people of Compiègne die, when they have been and still are so loyal to their lord?” Many times her Voices forbade her to leap. In the end, for fear of the English, she leaped from the tower, commending herself to God and to Our Lady. She said she did not do it out of despair, but in the hope of saving her body and going to help the many good people who were in need.67

She was badly hurt. Some said she was dead. When the Burgundians saw that she was alive, they scolded her for leaping. For two or three days she could neither eat nor drink. All that time Saint Catherine comforted her. Saint Catherine told her to confess and to ask God's pardon, and she promised that the people of Compiègne would have help before Saint Martin's Day in the winter. Then Saint Joan began to eat again, and she soon got well.68

She told her judges that the leap from Beaurevoir was the thing that had most angered her saints. She had begged their mercy for it. Was it a mortal sin, a sin grave enough to cut her off from God? “I know nothing about that,” she said, “but I leave it to Our Lord.” Compiègne was saved before Saint Martin's Day, as she had been told. But the English paid ten thousand gold francs for her, and they took her to the castle of Rouen.69

Saint Joan Faces Her Judges in Rouen

A church court was judging her, but in Rouen she was not put in a church prison guarded by churchmen. The English kept her in irons in the castle, guarded by English soldiers. Her judge was Bishop Pierre Cauchon of Beauvais. Brother Martin Ladvenu later said that the bishop could have sent her to a church prison, and would not. At the first hearing they told her not to try to escape. “It is true that I wanted to escape,” she said, “and I still want to. Is that not lawful for every prisoner?”70

On Saturday 24 February they began by asking her when she had last eaten. Since yesterday afternoon, she said. When had she last heard her Voices? Yesterday and today, she said. Yesterday they had come three times: in the morning, at the evening service, and again when the bell rang for the evening Hail Mary. What had she been doing yesterday morning?71

“I was asleep,” she said. “The Voice woke me.” Had it touched her arm? “It woke me without touching me.” Was it in her room? “Not that I know of, but it was in the castle.” Had she thanked it, and gone down on her knees? “I thanked it. I was sitting on the bed. I joined my hands and begged it to help me. … The Voice said to me, ‘Answer boldly. God will help you.’” Then she turned to the bishop. “You say you are my judge. Take care what you are doing, for in truth I am sent by God, and you are putting yourself in great danger.”72

She would not tell them everything. She said her Voices had given her things to tell the king, not them. “This very night they told me many things for the good of my king. I wish he could know them now, even if I had to drink no wine until Easter! He would eat his dinner more happily.” Did the Voice have a face and eyes? “You will not know that yet. There is a saying among children, that sometimes people are hanged for telling the truth.”73

A clerk wrote down the next question and her answer:

“Asked if she knows that she is in the grace of God, she answered: ‘If I am not, may God put me there; and if I am, may God keep me in it. I would be sadder than for the whole world if I knew I was not in the grace of God.’ She said further that if she were in sin, she believed the voice would not come to her; and she wished everyone could understand it as well as she does.”74

The record of her trial at Rouen, 24 February 1431

One day they pressed her to submit to the Church. She answered that she would gladly submit to the Pope, and she asked to be taken to him. She would not submit to the judgment of her enemies. A friar named Isambart de la Pierre advised her to submit to the council of the Church then meeting at Basel. She asked what a council was. He told her it was a gathering of the whole Church, and that there were as many men of her own side in it as of the English side. “Oh!” she cried. “If there are some of our side there, I will gladly give myself up and submit to the council of Basel.” At once the bishop shouted at the friar, “Be quiet, in the devil's name!” Then he told the notary to take care not to write down what she had said.75

She was questioned again and again in February and March, and the trial went on into May. In those hearings she told them that she had embraced Saint Catherine and Saint Margaret. She would rather have been torn apart by four horses, she said, than have come into France without God's leave. Once a notary claimed she had not answered a question eight days before. The record was read and she was proved right. She was glad, and she told him she would pull his ears if he made a mistake again. On the morning of Thursday 24 May they led her out to a scaffold in the cemetery of the abbey of Saint-Ouen.76

Saint Joan Signs and Then Takes It Back

A great crowd filled the cemetery of Saint-Ouen. The judges were there, with the Cardinal of England and several bishops and abbots. A famous preacher named Guillaume Érard took his text from the Gospel of Saint John: “A branch cannot bear fruit by itself unless it remains in the vine.” He told the people that she had cut herself off from Holy Mother Church.77

Then he turned on her king. He cried out that the man who called himself king of France was a heretic and a schismatic, a man who had broken away from the Church. He said that Charles had joined himself to the words and deeds of a worthless woman, and he said it two or three times. At last he raised his finger at her. “It is to you, Joan, that I am speaking, and I tell you that your king is a heretic and a schismatic!” She answered: “By my faith, sir, with all respect, I dare to tell you and to swear on pain of my life that he is the most noble Christian of all Christians, and the one who best loves the faith and the Church. He is not what you say.” The preacher turned to the court usher Jean Massieu, who was there with her. “Make her be quiet,” he said.78

When Érard called on her to submit, she would not put the blame on anyone else. “Let all I have said and done be sent to Rome, to our Holy Father the Pope,” she said, “to whom, after God, I refer myself. I did these things by God's command. I blame no one for them, not my king and not anyone else. If there is any fault in them, it is mine and no one else's.” They told her this answer was not enough, because the Pope was too far away. The bishop began to read out the sentence against her.79

Érard read out a paper listing the things he wanted her to take back. She said she did not understand what it meant to abjure, and she asked for advice. Massieu told her that if she went against any of those points, she would be burned. He advised her to leave the question to the Church Universal, the whole Church. “I leave it to the Church Universal whether I should abjure or not,” she said aloud. “You will abjure at once,” Érard answered, “or you will be burned.” Before the bishop had finished reading his sentence, she said she would hold to all that the Church ordained. A short paper of a few lines was read to her in French, and she signed it with a cross, using a pen that Massieu handed her. She did it from fear. Afterward a much longer paper went into the record in its place, full of confessions in her name.80

Some of those present asked that she now be taken to the Church's prison, since the Church had judged her. “Take her back to the castle she came from,” the bishop said. That afternoon she took off the man's clothes before the churchmen and put on a woman's dress, as she had been ordered. The man's clothes were put in a sack in her cell. Five English soldiers guarded her, three inside the room at night and two at the door. At night her legs were held in two pairs of irons. A chain ran through the foot of her bed to a great beam of wood and was locked, so that she could not move from the spot.81

On the morning of Trinity Sunday she said to her guards, “Unchain me, and I will get up.” One of the English took away the woman's dress she had on. They emptied the sack and threw the man's clothes at her. “Get up,” they said. “Sirs,” she said, “you know this is forbidden me. I will not put it on, without fail.” They would give her nothing else. The quarrel went on until midday. At last she had to go out to relieve herself, and so she put on the man's clothes. When she came back, they would give her no other clothes, however much she begged. She also told Brother Martin Ladvenu that she had been beaten and torn in that prison, and that an English lord had assaulted her.82

On Monday 28 May the judges came to her cell. She told them she had gone back to men's clothes because they had not kept their promise to her. They had promised that she could go to Mass and receive her Savior, and that she would be taken out of irons. “I would rather die than be in irons!” she said. “But if I may go to Mass, and am taken out of irons and put in a kinder prison, I will be good and do as the Church wills.” Then they asked whether she had heard her Voices since Thursday.83

“Asked what they told her, she answered that God had sent word through Saints Catherine and Margaret. The word was about the great pity of the betrayal she had agreed to in making the abjuration and recantation to save her life. They said she was damning herself to save her life.”84

The French record of her trial, 28 May 1431

In the same answer she said, “If I said that God had not sent me, I would damn myself, for it is true that God has sent me.” In the margin beside it the clerk wrote: “A deadly answer.” Did she believe her Voices were Saint Catherine and Saint Margaret? “Yes, I believe it, and that they come from God.” She had not meant to deny them, she said. “What I said, I said from fear of the fire. … I would rather do my penance all at once, that is, die, than bear the suffering of prison any longer.”85

She said that the English had done her great wrong and violence in that prison while she wore a woman's dress. Isambart de la Pierre was there with several others, and he saw her weeping. Her face was wet with tears and disfigured, and he was full of pity. “If you, my lords of the Church, had taken me and kept me in your prisons,” she said, “perhaps it would not have gone like this for me.” As the bishop left the prison he called out to the English who were waiting outside: “Farewell, farewell! Be of good cheer. It is done.” On 29 May the judges declared that she had gone back on the paper she signed. They called her a relapsed heretic. Early the next morning, a friar came to her cell.86

Saint Joan Dies in Rouen's Old Market

It was the morning of Wednesday 30 May 1431. Brother Martin Ladvenu came into her cell with another friar to tell her that she must die that day by fire. She cried out and tore at her hair. “Alas! Will they treat me so horribly and so cruelly? Must my body, whole and never defiled, be burned to ashes today? Ah! I would rather have my head cut off seven times over than be burned like this!” Then she appealed to God, the great Judge, against the wrongs done to her.87

Bishop Cauchon came to the cell. She said to him at once, “Bishop, I die through you.” “Ah, Joan,” he said, “be patient. You die because you did not keep what you promised us, and because you went back to your first offense.” “Alas,” she answered, “if you had put me in the prisons of the Church, and given me into the hands of fitting guardians of the Church, this would not have happened. For this I summon you before God.”88

That morning a priest named Pierre Maurice came to see her. “Master Pierre,” she asked him, “where shall I be this evening?” He asked whether she did not have good hope in God. She said she did. God willing, she would be in Paradise. She made her confession to Brother Martin and received Holy Communion.89

Then they took her to the Old Market of Rouen. Brother Martin and Massieu walked at her side, and more than eight hundred soldiers went with them, carrying axes and swords. A preacher named Nicolas Midi gave a sermon, and she listened to it quietly and patiently. At the end he said to her, “Joan, go in peace. The Church can no longer defend you, and it leaves you in the hands of the civil authorities.”90

She knelt and prayed. She called on the Holy Trinity, on the Virgin Mary, and on the saints of Paradise, naming some of them. Then she asked people of every rank, of her own side and of the other, to forgive her and to pray for her. In turn, she forgave them the harm they had done her. All this went on for about half an hour. One man in the crowd heard her say, “Ah, Rouen! I am very much afraid that you will suffer for my death!”91

She asked for a cross. An Englishman standing there made a little cross from the end of a stick, and he gave it to her. She kissed it and put it inside her clothes, against her breast. Then she asked for the cross from the church, and Massieu had a church clerk bring it. She held it close until she was tied to the stake. The English soldiers were growing impatient. “What, priest!” one of them shouted at Massieu. “Will you have us dine here?” They told the executioner, “Do your duty.”92

“At the end Joan had such great contrition and such beautiful repentance that it was a wonder. Her words were so devout, so pitiful, and so Catholic that everyone who watched, a great crowd, wept hot tears. The Cardinal of England and several other English were forced to weep and to feel pity. The poor woman humbly asked and begged him, since he was near her at the end, to go to the nearest church and bring her the cross. She wanted him to hold it high before her eyes until the moment of death, so that the cross on which God hung would be before her sight as long as she lived. In the flames she never stopped, to the end, calling aloud the holy name of Jesus and begging the saints of Paradise for help.”93

Brother Isambart de la Pierre, who stood near her at the end

With her last breath she cried “Jesus!” once more, so loudly that everyone standing there could hear it. Almost all of them wept.94

When it was over, the executioner came to the friars in despair. He was afraid that God would never forgive him for what he had done “to this holy woman.” He had been told to gather up her ashes and all that was left of her, and to throw them into the Seine. And he did.95

Saint Joan's Mother Asks for Justice

Eighteen years later King Charles entered Rouen, and he ordered an inquiry into her trial. Her mother and her two brothers asked the Pope to judge the case again. On 11 June 1455 Pope Calixtus III named three judges to hear it.96

On the morning of 7 November 1455 the judges sat in the cathedral of Notre-Dame in Paris. Her mother Isabelle came in with her son Pierre, who had been taken prisoner with his sister at Compiègne. She was a widow now. With great groans and sighs she threw herself down at the judges' feet and held out the Pope's letter. Humbly and most urgently, she begged them to act on it without further delay, and to do justice to the widow, her sons, and her kin.97

Then the people who had known Saint Joan spoke. In the winter the judges' men came to Domrémy, and the old sexton told them about the bell and the wool. Hauviette was a married woman now, and she told them how she had wept. Dunois, the duke of Alençon, and Jean d'Aulon told what they had seen at her side. D'Aulon had served her for a whole year. In all that time he had seen nothing in her that ought not to be in a good Christian.98

On 7 July 1456 the judges met in the archbishop's palace at Rouen. They declared that her trial, the paper she had signed, and the sentences against her were null and void. Brother Martin Ladvenu was there to hear the sentence read. They ordered a procession and a sermon at Saint-Ouen. The next day there was to be a sermon in the Old Market, in the very place where she had died. A fitting cross was to be set up there, so that she would be remembered forever.99

The Church Declares Saint Joan a Saint

Nearly five centuries passed. On 16 May 1920 Pope Benedict XV came into Saint Peter's Basilica in Rome. More than sixty thousand people were there, and a hundred and forty of them were descendants of her family. Before that packed and joyful crowd the Pope declared her a saint. In his formal letter, which the Church calls a bull, he wrote of the farm girl who “left her family to carry out the commands of God.”100

Her Voices had once told her: “Bear it all willingly. Do not be anxious or troubled about your martyrdom. You will enter the kingdom of Paradise.”101

A Note on the Sources

Most of this life rests on two sets of court records. The first is the record of her trial at Rouen in 1431, kept in French and Latin, where her own answers survive. The second is the record of the retrial of 1455 and 1456. There about twenty people who had known her gave sworn testimony, many of them twenty years or more after the events. The first statements of the retrial inquiry, among them those of Brothers Jean Toutmouillé, Isambart de la Pierre, and Martin Ladvenu, are dated 5 March 1449 in the record. The year then began at Easter, so this life gives March 1450. Memories differ. Perceval de Cagny was a chronicler who served the duke of Alençon. He gives her age at Chinon as about eighteen. She told her judges in February 1431 that she was about nineteen, so this life counts her as about seventeen in 1429. Cagny is also the source for the king's loss of heart after her capture, for her parting from the duke, and for her oath “by my martin”. Jules Quicherat thought the word might mean a stick or Saint Martin.

Her uncle said she stayed six weeks in his house, and she said about eight days, so this life gives no length. Her page and her steward tell the afternoon of 4 May 1429 in different orders; each sentence here follows one of them. Dunois dated her wound at Orléans to “the 27th of May”. The day was Saturday 7 May, as the other sources show. The Latin for her time in the vineyard is not clear, so this life says only “a short while”. Dunois gives the vineyard, and d'Aulon the struggle for the banner. Dunois remembers the image on her banner as Our Lord holding a lily. She told her judges it showed the world with an angel on each side, and this life follows her. Cagny puts her at Beaulieu for about four months. She put her stay in the tower of Beaurevoir at about four months too, so this life gives neither length. Cagny does not name the steward who served her at Beaulieu; Quicherat's note says he was Jean d'Aulon. The price paid for her is ten thousand gold francs in the bull of 1920 and fifteen or sixteen thousand coins in Cagny. The day of her capture is 23 May in Cagny and 24 May in the bull. Quicherat's notes are the source for the capture of her brother Pierre and for the ennobling of her family in December 1429.

Isambart de la Pierre does not say on what day he advised her to appeal to the council of Basel. The account of the guards and the man's clothes on Trinity Sunday is what she told the usher Jean Massieu. The assault in prison is what she told Brother Martin Ladvenu. Her words to the bishop on her last morning come from Ladvenu and from Brother Jean Toutmouillé, a Dominican of Rouen who was with him in her cell. Her question to Pierre Maurice comes through the chaplain Jean Ricquier, who heard it from Maurice. Both Massieu and Isambart tell of the church cross. One witness says the short paper she signed was six or seven lines long; the bull says about eight. No source read for this life says whether her father came to Reims, or whether the cross ordered in 1456 was set up.

How This Life Was Researched

Three research files stand behind this life, drawing on about a dozen sources in Latin, French, and English. The chief ones are the five volumes of Jules Quicherat's edition of the trial and retrial records (1841–1849) and T. Douglas Murray's English translation of 1902. The others are the bull of canonization of 1920, Guérin's French life of the saint, the Catholic Encyclopedia of 1910, and an encyclopedia article on her canonization. The evidence includes Saint Joan's own answers at her trial, her dictated letters, and the sworn testimony of some twenty people who knew her. It also includes two chronicles of her own time, one friendly and one hostile. The researchers marked about twenty details as seldom found on English pages. For this telling every scene was checked again against the scanned text of Quicherat and Murray, so that each note could give the page. Most quotations were translated afresh from the Latin and French; the rest are Murray's English, put into plain modern words.
