Joan of Arc wrote no books, and she could not read or write. What she left is the example of her life and the words she spoke: her answers to her judges at Rouen in 1431, a few letters she dictated, and sayings her companions remembered under oath twenty-five years later. The themes below describe that example. They are not a system of doctrine.

Listening, and answering boldly

For Joan, faith began with listening. She said her voices spoke to her every day, and she treated what she heard as an order to be obeyed, whatever it cost.

“I was sitting on the bed; I joined my hands; I implored its help. The Voice said to me: ‘Answer boldly; God will help thee.’”

— Joan of Arc to her judges, Rouen, 24 February 1431, of the morning before

She told this to her judges the day after it happened, from a prison guarded by English soldiers. She lived it from the start: when Baudricourt sent her home from Vaucouleurs, she came back until he received her; and in court she warned the bishop judging her that he put himself in great danger.

Work, and God will work

Joan did not see trust in God as a reason to wait. God acts, she believed, and so must we; the timing belongs to him.

“Do not doubt; the hour is ready when it pleases God. … Work, and God will work.”

— Joan of Arc to the duke of Alençon at Jargeau, June 1429, as he testified in 1456 (our translation)

She said it when d’Alençon thought the assault on Jargeau premature. At Orléans she lived it the other way round: when the army was exhausted and Dunois wanted to withdraw, she rode alone into a vineyard, prayed alone for a short while, and then planted her banner on the edge of the ditch.

The grace of God

Asked whether she was in God’s grace, Joan would not claim to be sure of her own holiness. She asked God to give and keep his grace, and left the rest to him.

“If I am not, may God place me there; if I am, may God so keep me.”

— Joan of Arc, asked whether she was in the grace of God, Rouen, 24 February 1431

The court asked her the question at her trial. She lived the answer in frequent confession: her hostess in Bourges saw her confess often, and at her first hearing she refused to say the Our Father unless the bishop would hear her confession.

Mercy in the middle of war

Joan led soldiers, but she told her judges she had never killed anyone, and her squire said she could not bear to see French blood spilled.

“Better, forty times better, my banner than my sword!”

— Joan of Arc to her judges, Rouen, 27 February 1431

She told her judges she carried the banner “to save killing any one, for I have never killed any one.” Her page saw her get down from her horse to hold the head of a dying English prisoner while he made his confession, and on the morning the English left Orléans she forbade any attack on them. Asked whether her saints hated the English, she answered, “They love what God loves: they hate what God hates.”

Falling and rising again

Joan’s life includes two falls, and she named both honestly. She jumped from the tower of Beaurevoir against the counsel of her saints, and she signed an abjuration at Saint-Ouen out of fear.

“All I said and revoked, I said for fear of the fire.”

— Joan of Arc to her judges, Rouen, 28 May 1431

She said it on 28 May, four days after the abjuration. After the leap from Beaurevoir she confessed and asked God’s pardon, and later she called it the greatest wrong she had done.

Prayer and the Mass

Prayer and the sacraments were the frame of Joan’s days, from the village church of Domrémy to her prison cell.

“I would rather die than be in irons! but if I am allowed to go to Mass, and am taken out of irons and put into a gracious prison … I will be good, and do as the Church wills.”

— Joan of Arc to her judges, Rouen, 28 May 1431

As a girl she knelt in the fields when the church bell rang and scolded the sexton when he forgot to ring for Compline. At Blois she told the soldiers to go to confession and received Communion with them, and at Bourges she asked to go to Matins.

Simplicity before the learned

Joan could not read. Learned men examined her at Poitiers and again at Rouen, and she answered them in plain words.

“There is more in our Lord’s books than in yours.”

— Joan of Arc, on the clerics who examined her, as Marguerite La Touroulde testified in 1456 (our translation)

When two doctors came to her lodging at Poitiers, a witness recalled, she told them, “I know neither A nor B.” When the women of Bourges brought her their rosaries to touch, she laughed and told them that their own touch would do as much good as hers.
