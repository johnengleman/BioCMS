# Joan of Arc — scene cards, group 2 (SC-06 to SC-10)

Source keys follow the outline: W-S3 = Quicherat vol. 3 (1844), W-S4 = Quicherat vol. 4 (1847), O-S1 = Murray 1902. New key: **Q1** = Quicherat vol. 1 (1841), archive.org `procsdecondam01joanuoft`, Latin and French, public-domain. All quotations are copied from the archive.org OCR text, which I fetched in this session. Where OCR damaged a letter (for example "u" for "à", "ii" for "n"), I wrote the obvious letter. Quicherat page numbers come from the running heads. The English after each quotation is our own.

## SC-06 — "Bloody boy!" (Orléans, 4 May 1429)
- Status: full
- When and where: Wednesday 4 May 1429, after dinner (Cagny: "le ive jour du mois de may après disner") [W-S4 p.6]. Orléans, the house of the treasurer (Jacques Boucher, per Quicherat's note), near the Porte Bannier [W-S3 p.67-68]. Joan's room is upstairs; the street, then the Burgundy gate.
- Who was there: Jean d'Aulon (her steward, resting on a bed in her room); Louis de Coutes (her page, 14-15 years old); her hostess and the hostess's daughter; Dunois (earlier, at her lodging after dinner).
- What happened, in order:
  1. After dinner, Dunois comes to her lodging and tells her Fastolf is near. Joan is glad. She orders Dunois to tell her the minute he knows, or she will have his head cut off. Dunois says not to worry [W-S3 p.212, d'Aulon].
  2. Tired, d'Aulon lies down in her room. Joan lies on another bed with her hostess [W-S3 p.212].
  3. Joan wakes: her counsel tells her to go against the English, but she does not know whether to the forts or against Fastolf. d'Aulon arms her [W-S3 p.212].
  4. A great noise and cry rise in the city. Joan goes out into the street while d'Aulon is still arming, finds a page on a horse, makes him get down, takes the horse, and rides to the Burgundy gate [W-S3 p.212].
  5. Coutes tells it differently. Joan first goes to Dunois, comes back very angry that no assault is planned, and goes to a boulevard. There she calls to the English to leave in God's name. The "Bastard of Granville" insults her [W-S3 p.68]. She goes up to her room. She comes down at once and shouts at Coutes (words below). She sends him for her horse. The hostess and her daughter arm her. Coutes finds her already armed. He hands her banner from the upper window. She runs to the gate [W-S3 p.68-69].
  6. At the gate they meet a badly wounded Frenchman being carried in. She asks who it is. "A Frenchman." She says she never saw French blood without her hair standing on end [W-S3 p.213; Coutes also: she met wounded Frenchmen and was angry, p.69].
  7. The French take the bastille of Saint-Loup. Churchmen in vestments come to meet her; she protects them [W-S3 p.69, Coutes]. She dines little that night [W-S3 p.69].
- Details: banner passed through a window; hostess and daughter arm her; she eats very little, "twice a day", often only a mouthful of bread (Coutes) [W-S3 p.69]. Granville's insult (Coutes, Latin with French words): he asked if she wanted them to surrender to a woman, and called the French with her "maquereaulx mescréans" (my reading: foul insults) [W-S3 p.68].
- Words spoken:
  - Joan to Coutes, 4 May. Latin report with her French: «Ha, sanglant garson, vous ne me djriez pas que le sanc de France feust répandu!» [OCR "djriez", probably "disiez"]. Our English: "Ah, you bloody boy! You did not tell me that French blood was being spilled!" [W-S3 p.68]
  - Joan, in d'Aulon's French: "jamais n'avoit veu sang de François que les cheveulx ne luy levassent ensur." Our English: she said she never saw French blood without her hair standing on end [W-S3 p.213].
- Testimony: Jean d'Aulon, deposition for the rehabilitation, French, [W-S3 p.212]. Rights: public-domain.
  «Dit que après ces parolles, il qui parle, lequel estoit las et travaillé, se mist sur une couchete en la chambre de ladicte Pucelle, pour ung pou soy reposer, et aussi se mist icelle avecques sadicte hostesse sur ung aultre lit pour pareillement soy dormir et reposer ; mais ainsi que ledit depposant commençoit à prendre son repos, soubdainement icelle Pucelle se leva dudit lit, et en faisant grant bruit l'esveilla. Et lors luy demanda il qui parle qu'elle vouloit ; laquelle luy respondit : "En non Dé, mon conseil m'a dit que je voise contre les Anglois ; mais je ne sçay se je doy aler à leurs bastilles ou contre Ffastolf, qui les doibt avitailler".»
  plain: He says that, tired and worn out, he lay down on a small bed in the Maid's room to rest a little. She lay down with her hostess on another bed. As he began to rest, the Maid suddenly got up, made a great noise, and woke him. He asked what she wanted. She answered: "In God's name, my counsel has told me to go against the English. But I do not know whether I should go to their forts or against Fastolf, who is to bring them supplies."
- Inner life: she speaks of "mon conseil" (her Voices) as the cause; she does not know where it points.
- What changed: she takes Saint-Loup that day, her first assault outside the walls.
- Not in the sources: the time of night (d'Aulon says she woke after the dinner talk; he gives no hour). The page's name in d'Aulon's version. Coutes and d'Aulon disagree on the order of events: do not blend them silently. Coutes dates the day as "the day after" the troops entered; Cagny gives 4 May. No weather, no crying.
- Sources: W-S3 p.67-69, 212-213; W-S4 p.6. New: none.

## SC-07 — The vineyard (Les Tourelles, 7–8 May 1429)
- Status: full
- When and where: Saturday 7 May 1429 [W-S4 p.8: "Le samedi"]. At the boulevard before the Tourelles fort at the south end of the Orléans bridge. Next morning, 8 May, in the city and the field.
- Who was there: Joan; Dunois (commander, wants to withdraw); Jean d'Aulon (steward); "le Basque" (a soldier of the lord de Villars, who took her banner); the English commander Glasdale; a surgeon.
- What happened, in order:
  1. At 7 a.m. Joan has trumpets sounded and takes her banner to the edge of the ditch. The assault is long and hard [W-S4 p.8].
  2. Early in the assault an arrow strikes her between neck and shoulder, "half a foot" deep. She does not leave the fight and takes no medicine [W-S3 p.8, Dunois].
  3. The assault lasts from morning to about 8 in the evening. Dunois wants to pull the army back to the city [W-S3 p.8].
  4. Joan asks him to wait a little. She rides alone to a vineyard, far from the crowd, and prays about a quarter of an hour [W-S3 p.8].
  5. d'Aulon's account of the same hour: the captains sound the retreat at sunset. d'Aulon has the Basque take the banner. He goes into the ditch under his shield. Joan sees the banner in other hands, grabs it by the end, and shakes it. He shouts at the Basque. The Basque pulls it free. The army rallies and takes the boulevard [W-S3 p.216-217].
  6. Dunois: she comes back, takes her banner, stands on the edge of the ditch. The English tremble; the French climb and meet no resistance [W-S3 p.8]. Glasdale and other English leaders fall into the river and drown [W-S3 p.9; Cagny p.9].
  7. She goes back into the city; the wound is dressed. She eats four or five sops of bread in wine mixed with much water, and nothing else that day [W-S3 p.9].
  8. 8 May, early: the English leave their camp and form a line. Joan rises from bed, arms herself in one garment (a mail coat), and forbids an attack. They go, and nobody pursues [W-S3 p.9].
- Details: arrow wound dressed by a surgeon (Dunois) or by d'Aulon (d'Aulon: "il qui parle la list habiller") [W-S3 p.9, 217]. Cagny gives her words that morning: "Par mon martin, je la prendray demain et retourneray en la ville par sus les pons" ("By my martin, I will take it tomorrow and return to the city over the bridge"), and in the assault "Ne vous doubtez, la place est nostre" ("Have no doubt, the place is ours") [W-S4 p.8-9].
- Words spoken: d'Aulon: «Haa ! mon estandart ! mon estandart!» ("Ah, my banner! My banner!") [W-S3 p.216]. d'Aulon to the Basque: «Ha, Basque ! est ce que tu m'as promis?» ("Ah, Basque! Is this what you promised me?") [W-S3 p.216]. Joan that day, to d'Aulon: «Ou nom Dé, on entrera ennuyt en la ville par le pont.» ("In God's name, we will enter the city tonight over the bridge.") [W-S3 p.217].
- Testimony: Dunois, deposition for the rehabilitation, Latin, [W-S3 p.8]. Rights: public-domain.
  «Et tunc dicta Puella venit ad eum, et requisivit quod adhuc paulisper exspectaret, ipsaque ex illa hora ascendit equum, et sola recessit in unam vineam, satis longe a turba hominum ; in qua vinea fuit in oratione quasi per spatium dimidii quarti horae ; ipsa autem regressa ab illo loco, statim cepit suum vexillum in manibus suis, posuitque se supra bordum fossati, et instanti, ipsa ibi exsistente, Anglici fremuerunt et effecti sunt pavidi.»
  plain: Then the Maid came to him and asked him to wait a little longer. She mounted her horse and rode off alone to a vineyard, quite far from the crowd. There she prayed for about a quarter of an hour. When she came back from that place, she took her banner in her hands at once and stood on the edge of the ditch. At that moment the English shuddered and were afraid.
- Inner life: none in the sources for the vineyard. What she prayed is not recorded.
- What changed: the Tourelles fall; Orléans is open across the bridge; the English leave on 8 May.
- Not in the sources: any words of the prayer; what she did with the arrow (Guérin says she pulled it out; I did not check it here). Dunois's date, "vicesima septima die maii" ("the 27th of May") in the Latin of W-S3 p.8 cannot be right, because Cagny says Saturday 7 May and the English left next day. Flag it for the writer; Quicherat's page gives no note on it that I could find. The wound site differs by source: Dunois says "between neck and shoulders"; the notes say Catholic Encyclopedia says the breast.
- Sources: W-S3 p.8-9, 216-217; W-S4 p.8-9.

## SC-08 — "Are you afraid?" (Jargeau, June 1429)
- Status: full
- When and where: June 1429 (the deposition gives no day). Before and inside the suburbs of Jargeau, on the Loire, held by the English. The notes give the date as June; I did not find a day.
- Who was there: Joan; the duke of Alençon (lieutenant of the king in this army, speaking here); Dunois and Florent d'Illiers (arrived with troops); La Hire; the Earl of Suffolk (English commander); Monseigneur du Lude.
- What happened, in order:
  1. Joan says on the first day that the captains need not fear the English numbers: God leads the work. If she were not sure of it, she says, she would rather keep sheep than expose herself to such dangers [W-S3 p.95].
  2. The French reach the suburbs. The English sally out and drive them back at first. Joan takes her banner and charges, urging the men on. The French sleep that night in the suburbs [W-S3 p.95].
  3. After some days the captains hold a council. The heralds cry "To the assault!" Joan says to d'Alençon: "Forward, gentle duke, to the assault!" [W-S3 p.95-96].
  4. He thinks it is too early. She answers (below). She then asks whether he is afraid, and reminds him that she promised his wife to bring him back safe (his wife had feared for him: he had lately been a prisoner and the ransom was large) [W-S3 p.96].
  5. During the assault she tells him to leave the spot where he stands, or "the machine" (a gun in the town) will kill him. He leaves. A little after, Monseigneur du Lude is killed at that very spot. d'Alençon is frightened and amazed [W-S3 p.96-97].
  6. Joan on a ladder with her banner is struck on her helmet by a stone, falls, and rises (below). The town is taken at once. More than 1,100 English are killed in the pursuit [W-S3 p.97].
- Details: the helmet was a light cap-helmet (Latin "capellaniam", Quicherat's note: read "capellinam", "chapeline", a light helmet with no visor) [W-S3 p.97]. Joan's banner was struck too [W-S3 p.97].
- Words spoken (d'Alençon's deposition, Latin with her French) [W-S3 p.95-97]:
  - «Avant, gentil duc, à l'assault !» Our English: "Forward, gentle duke, to the assault!"
  - «Nolite dubitare, hora est parata quando placet Deo ; operate, et Deus operabitur.» Our English: "Do not doubt. The hour is ready when it pleases God. Act, and God will act."
  - «A ! gentil duc, times-tu ? Nonne scis quod ego promisi uxori tuae te reducere sanum et incolumem ?» Our English: "Ah, gentle duke, are you afraid? Do you not know that I promised your wife to bring you back safe and sound?"
  - To the duchess, earlier: «Domina, nolite timere. Ego eum vobis reddam sanum, et in statu tali aut meliori quam sit.» Our English: "Lady, do not be afraid. I will give him back to you safe, and in as good a state as he is, or better."
  - After the stone: «Amys, amys, sus ! sus ! Nostre Sire a condempné les Angloys. Ista hora sunt nostri ; habeatis bonum cor !» Our English: "Friends, friends, up, up! Our Lord has condemned the English. At this hour they are ours. Take heart!"
- Testimony: d'Alençon, [W-S3 p.97], Latin. Rights: public-domain.
  «Et erat ipsa Johanna in scala, tenens in manu sua vexillum suum, quod vexillum fuit percussum, et ipsa Johanna fuit percussa super caput de uno lapide quod fuit diminutum super capellaniam ipsius Johannae. Ipsa tamen Johanna prostrata fuit ad terram ; et cum surrexisset dixit armatis.»
  plain: Joan was on a ladder, holding her banner in her hand. The banner was struck, and Joan was struck on the head by a stone, which broke up on her helmet. Joan was thrown to the ground. When she rose, she said to the soldiers [the words above].
- What changed: Jargeau falls; the duke returns alive; the road to Beaugency opens.
- Not in the sources: how hurt she was after the fall; how d'Alençon felt in the moment (he says only "great fear" and amazement at the du Lude death); the day of the month.
- Sources: W-S3 p.95-97 (also p.93-94 for context before the march).

## SC-09 — The ditch at Paris (8 September 1429)
- Status: full
- When and where: Thursday 8 September 1429, the feast of the Nativity of Our Lady. From about noon to dark (Cagny) [W-S4 p.26-27]. The gate Saint-Honoré, the ditch by the Marché aux Pourceaux. Afterwards the camp at La Chapelle, then Saint-Denis.
- Who was there: Joan; the duke of Alençon; Marshal de Rais; the lord de Gaucourt; the duke of Bar and the count of Clermont (sent by the king, who stayed at Saint-Denis); her standard-bearer; a Parisian crossbowman (unnamed).
- What happened, in order:
  1. About 8 a.m. the army leaves La Chapelle in order. Joan, de Rais and Gaucourt go to the Saint-Honoré gate. Joan takes her banner and enters the ditch among the first [W-S4 p.26].
  2. The assault is hard and long, with great fire from guns. Cagny says no man died on the French side [W-S4 p.26-27]. (The Bourgeois says many were maimed [W-S4 p.464].)
  3. After sunset she is hit through the thigh by a crossbow bolt. She urges all the more that men come to the walls and says the place will be taken [W-S4 p.27]. (Her own account: wounded "in the trenches before Paris", cured in five days [O-S1 p.14].)
  4. Gaucourt and others take her out of the ditch against her will [W-S4 p.27].
  5. The hostile account of the same hour (Bourgeois de Paris): a man shouts, then shoots her through the leg; her standard-bearer is shot between the eyes and dies [W-S4 p.465].
  6. Next morning, wounded, she gets up early and asks d'Alençon to sound the trumpets. She will not leave until she has the town. Messengers from the king arrive and ask her to come to him at Saint-Denis. She and most of the company are very unhappy but obey [W-S4 p.27-28].
  7. On Saturday the king has the bridge d'Alençon had built across the Seine broken during the night, so the army cannot cross [W-S4 p.28]. This is why the attack stopped.
- Details: Bourgeois says cartloads of faggots were brought to fill the ditches. The Bourgeois dates the assault to the feast day itself and has the attack end around four in the afternoon; Cagny has noon to nightfall. Joan later said she was told to stay at Saint-Denis but the lords took her away against her will (22 February answer) [Q1 p.260; O-S1 p.13-14].
- Words spoken:
  - Cagny, as she is led away: «Par mon martin, la place eust esté prinse.» Our English: "By my martin, the place would have been taken." [W-S4 p.27]
  - Bourgeois, Joan at the ditch: «Rendez vous, de par Jhesus, à nous tost» ("Surrender to us quickly, in Jesus's name"). The man answers «paillarde ! ribaude !» ("whore! slut!") [W-S4 p.465].
  - Joan at her trial denied the words. Q1 p.148: «Rendez la ville de par Jeshus» — «respond que non ; mais dist : "Rendez-la au roy de France."» Our English: she said no, she said "Give it up to the King of France."
  - Q1 p.147-148: asked if it was well done to attack on the Nativity of Our Lady, she said it is good to keep Our Lady's feasts, and "it seems to her in her conscience" that it always was and will be (my paraphrase of the French).
- Testimony: Journal d'un bourgeois de Paris (a hostile Paris writer, not an eyewitness to the ditch), French, [W-S4 p.465]. Rights: public-domain.
  «Et là estoit leur Pucelle, à tout son estendart, sur les conclos des fossez, qui disoit à ceulx de Paris : "Rendez vous, de par Jhesus, à nous tost" ... "Voire," dist ung, "paillarde ! ribaude !" Et trait de son arbalestre droit à elle, et lui perce la jambe tout oultre, et elle de s'enfouir. Un autre perça le pie tout oultre à celui qui portoit son estendart. Quant il se sentit navré, il leva sa visière pour veoir à oster le vireton de son pie, et ung autre lui trait, et le saingne entre les deux yeulx, et le navre à mort.»
  plain: And there was their Maid, with her banner, on the ridge of the ditches, saying to the people of Paris: "Surrender to us quickly, in Jesus's name." ... "Yes!" said one, "whore! slut!" He shot his crossbow straight at her and pierced her leg right through, and she ran away. Another pierced the foot of the man who carried her banner right through. When he felt the wound, he raised his visor to see about pulling the bolt from his foot. Another man shot him between the eyes and wounded him to death.
- Inner life: Joan on 22 February: the Voice told her to stay at Saint-Denis; she wished to; "against her will the lords took her away; and had she not been hurt, she would not have left" [Q1 p.260, Latin summary: «vox dixit ei quod maneret apud Sanctum Dionysium ; sed, contra voluntatem suam, domini eduxerunt eam ; et nisi fuisset laesa, ipsa non recessisset»]. She also said she went to Paris not on a revelation but at the request of the men-at-arms [Q1 p.147].
- What changed: the first defeat; the king returns to the Loire; Joan is idle against her will.
- Not in the sources: the Bourgeois (an enemy, writing without being there) makes the standard-bearer's death a detail Cagny does not give; Cagny says nobody died in the ditch. The two accounts disagree about who was hit when. Do not give the crossbowman a name. Do not say she wept.
- Sources: W-S4 p.26-29, 464-466; O-S1 p.13-14 (Murray, public-domain); Q1 p.147-148, 260.

## SC-10 — Taken in the meadow (Compiègne, 23 May 1430)
- Status: full
- When and where: Monday 23 May 1430. Compiègne: the meadow before the town, the bridge, the boulevard, the Oise. About 9 a.m. the skirmish begins (Cagny); she says she sallied "in the evening" (own account) [W-S4 p.33; Q1 p.115].
- Who was there: Joan; 300-400 fighters she brought from Crépy; the captain of Compiègne (Cagny does not name him; the later chronicle names Guillaume de Flavy); the Burgundians and English; Jean de Luxembourg; the five or six who took her.
- What happened, in order:
  1. Joan leaves Crépy about midnight with 300-400 fighters. Her men say she has too few. She says: "By my martin, we are enough; I will go and see my good friends of Compiègne." She arrives at sunrise and enters the town [W-S4 p.32-33].
  2. About 9 a.m. she hears of the skirmish in the meadow. She arms and rides out into the fighting [W-S4 p.33].
  3. The French drive the enemy back. A Burgundian ambush from the Mont de Clairoy comes out and cuts them off from the bridge [W-S4 p.33].
  4. Her men say: "Try to get back to the town, or we are all lost." She answers angrily that they will lose only by not striking [W-S4 p.33-34].
  5. They force her back toward the bridge. The captain of the town sees the crowd of enemies and has the bridge raised and the gate shut. She is shut out with few men [W-S4 p.34].
  6. Five or six take hold of her, some by her, some by her horse, each saying "Yield to me and give your faith." She answers (below). She is led to Jean de Luxembourg's lodging [W-S4 p.34].
  7. Her own account: her men retreated; "in retreating into the fields on the side toward Picardy, near the boulevard, she was taken" [Q1 p.116].
- Details: she was warned in Easter week 1430 at Melun that she would be taken before St John's day; she asked to die soon and not to lie long in prison, but the Voices gave no hour [Q1 p.115-116; O-S1 pp.57-58]. She says that day she had no command to go out, but "it was always said to her that she must be a prisoner" [Q1 p.116].
- Words spoken: Cagny, her answer when called on to yield: «Je ay juré et baillé ma foy à autre que à vous et je luy en tendray mon serement.» Our English: "I have sworn and given my faith to someone other than you, and I will keep my oath to him." [W-S4 p.34]. (Cagny's text; he does not say who the "other" is.)
- Testimony: Joan's own account, 10 March 1431, French minute (Q1 p.116-117), then Murray's English. Rights: public-domain.
  «elle passa par le pont et par le boulevart, et ala avec la compaignie des gens de son party sur les gens de Monseigneur de Luxambourg, et les rebouta par deux fois jusques au logeis des Bourgueguons, et à la tierce fois jusques à my le chemin ; et alors les Anglois, qui là estoient, coupèrent les chemins à elle et ses gens, entre elle et le boulevart ; et pour ce, se retrairent ses gens ; et elle en se retraiant es champs en costé, devers Picardie, près du boulevert, fut prinse»
  plain: She crossed the bridge and the boulevard and went with the company of her side against the men of my lord of Luxembourg. She drove them back twice as far as the Burgundians' lodgings, and the third time to the middle of the road. Then the English who were there cut the road between her and her men and the boulevard. Her men fell back, and in falling back into the fields on the side toward Picardy, near the boulevard, she was taken.
- Inner life: «si ipsa scivisset horam ... non ivisset»; our English: if she had known the hour, she would not have gone willingly, though she would have obeyed her Voices in the end [Q1 p.115-116].
- What changed: she is a prisoner, held three or four days at Luxembourg's lodging, then about four months at Beaulieu, then sold to the English (Cagny, who gives 15 or 16 thousand "saluz") [W-S4 p.34-35].
- Not in the sources: Cagny does not say who gave the order to close the gate or why; the account naming Flavy as a traitor who sold her comes from a late chronicle that quotes old men in Compiègne in 1498 [W-S4 p.272-273 of the same volume]. I did not check the author. Treat it as rumour, not fact. No source gives her words as she was pulled from the horse beyond the reply above. The date 23 or 24 May differs by source (see outline accuracy notes). The "bells" question was asked at the trial; she says she did not order them rung [Q1 p.115].
- Sources: W-S4 p.32-35, 272-273; Q1 p.115-117; O-S1 pp.57-58 (Murray, public-domain, for the English).
