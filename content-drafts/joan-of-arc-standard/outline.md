## 1. The driving question

Saint Joan wanted to do what God asked through her Voices, and so to stay in His grace: "never have I asked of it any recompense but the salvation of my soul" [O-S1 p.13]. Against her stood her smallness ("a poor girl, who knew nothing of riding or fighting", O-S1 p.11), her father, captains and a king who held her back, prison, a church court, and at last her fear of the fire, which made her sign a paper against her Voices [Q1 pp.456-457]. Grace brought her back twice, after Beaurevoir and after Saint-Ouen, and she died with the cross before her eyes, calling on Jesus [Q2 pp.6-7].

Why it was beautiful to her: "I saw them with my bodily eyes as well as I see you; when they went from me, I wept. I should have liked to be taken away with them" [O-S1 p.25]; "I would be sadder than for the whole world if I knew I was not in the grace of God" [Q1 p.65]. The reader first hears it in ch. 2 and sees her choose it in ch. 3: God commanded it, so it had to be done; with a hundred fathers and mothers she would still have gone [Q1 p.129].

**Famous:** the farm girl who raised the siege of Orléans and brought Charles VII to his crowning at Reims in 1429 [C-S1; C-S3]. **Set apart:** she could not read, yet she spoke of war like lifelong knights [W-S4 p.3; W-S3 p.100]. In a cruel war she carried a banner so as not to kill, made soldiers confess, and held a dying English prisoner's head [O-S1 p.31; W-S3 pp.67, 71-72].

## 2. Portrait

Cheerful; liked a happy face [C-S2 p.390]. The village laughed at her prayer, and it made her shy [Q2 pp.418, 420]. Strong-willed early: she scolded the sexton and bribed him with wool [Q2 p.413]. Quick and dry under pressure ("You burden me over-much!"), proud of her spinning [O-S1 p.9]. Swore "by my martin" [W-S4 p.5]. A real temper: broke her sword on a camp woman, threatened to pull a notary's ears [W-S3 p.99; O-S1 p.305]. Laughed at women who brought rosaries to touch [W-S3 p.87]. Faults: impatience, the leap, the paper signed from fear. Not fearless: she wept at the news of the fire [M p.158].

Life with God: she knelt in the fields at the bell [Q2 p.420]; her page saw her pray, sometimes weeping [W-S3 p.66]; she prayed alone in a vineyard mid-battle [W-S3 p.8]. She called her Voices "my counsel" [W-S3 p.212]; when one woke her, she joined her hands and asked its help [Q1 pp.62-63].

## 3. The opening

**Choice:** a hook: a scene at the edge, in her own words. It raises the question (what revelation, and where will it lead?) and spends nothing: no stake, cross, ashes, or road from Reims.

**Draft:** On 22 February 1431 a prisoner of about nineteen faced her judges in the castle of Rouen [O-S1 pp.6, 9]. The day before, she had complained of her chains [O-S1 p.7]. Now they told her to swear again to tell the truth. "I swore yesterday," she said. "You burden me too much." Asked whether she had learned a trade, she said yes, sewing and spinning, and in those she feared no woman in Rouen. Then she warned them: "If you were well informed about me, you would wish to have me out of your hands. I have done nothing except by revelation" [O-S1 p.9].

**Thesis:** Two years before, this spinner, who knew three prayers and could not read, had raised the siege of Orléans and stood with her banner at her king's crowning [C-S2 p.391; C-S3]. Her answers survive in the trial record, and in 1455–56 about twenty people who knew her swore to her life [O-S1; Q2; Q3]. Answered in ch. 5–7 (deeds), 10–12 (faith under trial), 13–14 (the Church's verdict).

**Summary rule:** it may say a church court tried her. No fire, cross, or ashes.

**Ending:** ch. 13–14 stay short; the path, feast (30 May), and 1922 patronage go first, in one paragraph [C-S1; C-S5]. **Closing image:** Saint Peter's, 16 May 1920: more than 60,000 people, 140 from her family's line, hear Benedict XV declare a saint the girl who left her father's house without leave [C-S5; C-S1]. One quiet sentence may follow, used only here: the bull records her Voices' words near the end: do not be troubled about your martyrdom; you will enter the kingdom of Paradise [C-S1]. What she offers today (a call too big for her, failure from fear, return) is felt, not stated.

## 5. The scenes

**Turning points (slow, from inside her):** TP1 SC-02 garden; TP2 SC-03 leaving home; TP3 SC-06 vineyard; TP4 SC-11 Beaurevoir; TP5 SC-13 the paper and the return. **Climax:** SC-14, with nothing in front of it.

**Resistance per period** (compress the rest): childhood, the laughter (SC-01); call, father and Baudricourt (SC-03); Chinon, the wait and examiners as bridge (SC-04); Orléans, the captains (SC-05, SC-06); summer 1429, the duke's fear (SC-07); autumn 1429, the king (SC-09); prison, the tower (SC-11); trial, one concrete day, 24 Feb (SC-12), other sessions in three sentences.

**Relationships to their end:**
- Family: father's threat (SC-03) → later forgiveness by letter [Q1 p.129] → wish to keep their sheep (SC-08) → ennobled, Dec 1429 [C-S3] → mother at Notre-Dame (SC-15) → family line at Saint Peter's (ch. 14). Hauviette: weeps (SC-03) → testifies 1456 (ch. 13).
- Voices: garden → Melun warning (ch. 8) → disobeyed, forgiven (SC-11) → "Answer boldly" (SC-12) → "damned herself" (SC-13) → held to them to the end [Q3 p.150] → the bull's promise (last line).
- King: "give your kingdom to the King of Heaven" (SC-04) → crowned (SC-08) → breaks the bridge (SC-09) → she blames "neither my King nor any one else" [M p.128] (SC-13) → orders the 1449 inquiry [C-S10; C-S2] (ch. 13).
- Companions: d'Alençon (SC-04, SC-07, SC-09); Dunois (SC-05, SC-06, SC-08); d'Aulon, her steward (ch. 6, still serving her at Beaulieu [Q4 p.35 n.1], SC-11). All three testify in 1456 (ch. 13).

**Losses at their dates:** home and Hauviette (1428–29); pulled from the ditch, arms laid down at Saint-Denis (Sept 1429); Melun (Easter 1430); capture (23 May 1430); sold (autumn 1430); chains and English guards (1431); the news of the fire (30 May 1431).

## 6. The saint in the Church

- **Writings:** letters to the English (22 Mar 1429), Tournai, Burgundy (17 Jul 1429), Reims (1430) [O-S3 pp.95-160]; her trial answers. Ch. 4, 7, 10.
- **What she changed:** Orléans keeps 8 May from 1429 [C-S1]; indulgence, 1452 [C-S5]; the Rouen cross, 1456 [Q3 pp.361-362]; Saint Thérèse's two plays [scenes-3 FN-2]; patron of France, 1922 [C-S5]. Ch. 6, 13, 14.
- **Why famous:** Pius II's "astonishing maid" [C-S1]; Dupanloup and the bishops' petition, 1869 [C-S5; C-S3]. Ch. 13–14.
- **Path:** 1894 cause; 1902 objections; 1904 heroic virtue; 1909 beatified; 16 May 1920 canonized as a virgin; feast 30 May [C-S1; C-S5]. Ch. 14.

**Fact needs:**
1. Whether the 1456 cross was raised, and when. Q3 after p.362; Guérin p.415.
2. What people ask of Saint Joan (patronages beyond France). Pius XI's 1922 letter (AAS); C-S3.
3. Dates of Saint Thérèse's two plays. De Teil or Taylor (local library); Lisieux Carmel archive.

## 8. What we leave out

- The 4 May night as a full scene, and Saint-Pierre, Beaugency, Patay as scenes: the middle must not become a list of fights.
- Fairy-tree charges; the angel-and-crown "sign": trial detail.
- Fierbois sword, Lagny infant, dove, heart, name in the flames, prophecies: miracles section.
- Deaths of her judges; birds in her lap; Chinon bottle; Hussite letter.
- The Revolution and Napoleon: keeps the ending short.

## 9. Accuracy notes

- Ages: about 13 at the Voice, 16 at leaving, 17 in 1429, 19 in 1431 (her word, O-S1 p.6). Not Cagny's "eighteen". Birth "about 1412".
- Father's dream: "if he thought the dream would come true", he wanted her brothers to drown her; her mother's report [Q1 pp.131-132].
- Baudricourt: slaps, not whipping. First visit about Ascension 1428; back near Lent 1429. No fasting.
- At Beaulieu "her steward" spoke; Cagny does not name him; Quicherat's note names d'Aulon [Q4 p.35].
- Vineyard: "a short while". Dunois's "27 May" is 7 May.
- 24 Feb: the Voice woke her the morning before, and spoke again the night just past.
- Capture 23 May (Cagny) vs 24 May (bull); no hour. Price 10,000 gold francs (bull).
- The assault is her report to Ladvenu; Isambart: face "disfigured"; executioner in despair, not terror.
- "I die through you": Ladvenu's and Toutmouillé's memory. Maurice's question comes through Ricquier [M p.302].
- Names: Dunois, "the Bastard of Orléans"; Isabelle (also Romée).
