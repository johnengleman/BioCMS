Saint Joan Is Captured and Sold

They reached Compiègne at sunrise and slipped into the town. On Monday 23 May 1430 she rode out over the bridge with her company against the Burgundians camped in the meadow. Twice she drove them back as far as their camp, and the third time halfway there. Then the English came up and cut the road between her men and the earthwork that guarded the bridge, and her men began to fall back.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 4, pp. 33–34 (Cagny); vol. 1, p. 116 (her own account, 10 March 1431; our translation); Murray, Jeanne d'Arc, p. 55.}}

Her men begged her to get back into the town, or they would all be lost. She was angry. “Be quiet, all of you!” she said. “It depends only on you whether they are beaten. Think of nothing but striking them.” They would not listen, and they forced her back toward the bridge. The captain of the town saw the crowd of enemies about to reach his bridge. Fearing to lose the town, he had the bridge raised and the gate shut. She was left outside with a few of her men.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 4, p. 34 (Cagny; our translation).}}

She fought hard, but five or six men took hold of her at once, some of her and some of her horse. Each of them shouted, “Yield to me and give me your faith!” She answered, “I have sworn and given my faith to someone other than you, and I will keep my oath to him.” Her brother Pierre was taken with her. She was led away to the lodging of Jean de Luxembourg, a lord who fought for Burgundy.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 4, p. 34 (Cagny; our translation); vol. 2, p. 74, note 2 (Quicherat's note on Pierre d'Arc).}}

Her Voices had always told her that she must be taken prisoner, but they had not told her the day. “If I had known the hour when I would be taken,” she said later, “I would never have gone out of my own free will. But in the end I would always have obeyed their commands, whatever happened to me.”{{n: Murray, Jeanne d'Arc, p. 58 (hearing of 10 March 1431; Murray's translation, modernized by us); Quicherat, Procès, vol. 1, pp. 115–116.}}

Jean de Luxembourg kept her at his lodging for three or four days and then sent her to a castle called Beaulieu. Her steward had served her before she was taken, and he served her there in prison too. One day he said to her, “This poor town of Compiègne, which you have loved so much, will now fall into the hands of the enemies of France.” “It will not,” she answered. “None of the places that the King of Heaven has brought back to the gentle King Charles through me will be taken back by his enemies, as long as he takes care to guard them.”{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 4, pp. 34–35 and note 1 (Cagny; our translation).}}

In the months after she was taken, the king and his counselors had even less will to fight for the kingdom than before. They began to look instead for a peace with the English and the duke of Burgundy.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 4, pp. 36–37 (Cagny; our translation).}}

Later she was moved to the tower of the castle of Beaurevoir. In that tower two pieces of news reached her. All the people of Compiègne were to be put to fire and blood, down to the children of seven. And she herself had been sold to the English. She told her judges what passed between her and her saints:{{n: Murray, Jeanne d'Arc, pp. 54, 74 (Murray's translation, modernized by us); Quicherat, Procès, vol. 1, p. 150.}}

“Saint Catherine told me almost every day not to leap, and that God would help me, and the people of Compiègne too. I said to Saint Catherine, ‘Since God will help the people of Compiègne, I want to be there.’ Saint Catherine said to me, ‘Accept it, and do not lose heart. You will not be set free until you have seen the king of England.’ I answered her, ‘Truly, I do not want to see him. I would rather die than fall into the hands of the English.’”{{n: Murray, Jeanne d'Arc, p. 74 (hearing of 14 March 1431; Murray's translation, modernized by us); Quicherat, Procès, vol. 1, p. 151.}}

Saint Joan of Arc, to her judges, 14 March 1431

She asked her saints, “How can God let these good people of Compiègne die, when they have been and still are so loyal to their lord?” Her Voices forbade her many times to jump. In the end, for fear of the English, she leaped from the tower, commending herself to God and to Our Lady. She said she did it not out of despair. She hoped to save her body and to go and help the many good people who were in need.{{n: Murray, Jeanne d'Arc, pp. 54, 74 (Murray's translation, modernized by us); Quicherat, Procès, vol. 1, pp. 160–161 (our translation).}}

She was badly hurt. Some said she was dead, and when the Burgundians saw that she was alive, they scolded her for leaping. For two or three days she could neither eat nor drink. All that time Saint Catherine comforted her. Saint Catherine told her to confess and to ask God's pardon, and she promised that the people of Compiègne would have help before Saint Martin's Day in the winter. Then Saint Joan began to eat again, and she soon got well.{{n: Murray, Jeanne d'Arc, pp. 54, 74–75 (Murray's translation, modernized by us); Quicherat, Procès, vol. 1, pp. 151–152.}}

She told her judges that the leap from Beaurevoir was the thing that had most angered her saints. She had begged their mercy for it. Was it a mortal sin? “I know nothing about that,” she said, “but I leave it to Our Lord.” Compiègne was saved before Saint Martin's Day, as she had been told. But the English paid ten thousand gold francs for her, and they took her to the castle of Rouen.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 1, pp. 162, 172–173 (our translation); Benedict XV, Divina disponente (on Compiègne and on the price); Quicherat, Procès, vol. 4, p. 35 (Cagny).}}

Saint Joan Faces Her Judges in Rouen

In Rouen she was not put in a prison of the Church, guarded by churchmen, though a church court was trying her. The English kept her in irons in the castle, with English soldiers to guard her. Her judge was Bishop Pierre Cauchon of Beauvais. He could have sent her to a church prison, and he would not. At the first hearing they told her not to try to escape. “It is true that I wanted to escape,” she said, “and I still want to. Is that not lawful for every prisoner?”{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 2, pp. 7–8 (Isambart de la Pierre and Martin Ladvenu); Murray, Jeanne d'Arc, pp. 7, 162 (Murray's translation, modernized by us).}}

On Saturday 24 February they began by asking her when she had last eaten. Since yesterday afternoon, she said. When had she last heard her Voices? Yesterday and today, she said. Yesterday they had come three times: in the morning, at the evening service, and again when the bell rang for the evening Hail Mary. What had she been doing yesterday morning?{{n: Murray, Jeanne d'Arc, pp. 15–16 (hearing of 24 February 1431).}}

“I was asleep,” she said. “The Voice woke me.” Had it touched her arm? “It woke me without touching me.” Was it in her room? “Not that I know of, but it was in the castle.” Had she thanked it, and gone down on her knees? “I thanked it. I was sitting on the bed. I joined my hands and begged it to help me. The Voice said to me, ‘Answer boldly. God will help you.’” Then she turned to the bishop. “You say you are my judge. Take care what you are doing, for in truth I am sent by God, and you are putting yourself in great danger.”{{n: Murray, Jeanne d'Arc, p. 16 (Murray's translation, modernized by us); Quicherat, Procès, vol. 1, pp. 62–63.}}

She would not tell them everything. She said her Voices had given her things to tell the king, not them. “This very night they told me many things for the good of my king. I wish he could know them now, even if I had to drink no wine until Easter! He would eat his dinner more happily.” Did the Voice have a face and eyes? “You will not know that yet. There is a saying among children, that sometimes people are hanged for telling the truth.”{{n: Murray, Jeanne d'Arc, pp. 17–18 (Murray's translation, modernized by us).}}

Then the questioner asked whether she knew that she was in the grace of God. A clerk wrote down her answer:

“Asked if she knows that she is in the grace of God, she answered: ‘If I am not, may God put me there; and if I am, may God keep me in it. I would be sadder than for the whole world if I knew I was not in the grace of God.’ She said further that if she were in sin, she believed the voice would not come to her; and she wished everyone could understand it as well as she does.”{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 1, p. 65 (hearing of 24 February 1431; our translation); Murray, Jeanne d'Arc, p. 18.}}

The record of her trial at Rouen, 24 February 1431

Again and again they told her to submit to the Church. Once she answered that she would gladly submit to the Holy Father, and she asked to be taken to him. She would not submit to the judgment of her enemies. A friar named Isambart de la Pierre advised her to submit to the council of the Church then meeting at Basel. She asked what a council was. He told her it was a gathering of the whole Church, and that there were as many men of her own side in it as of the English side. “Oh!” she cried. “If there are some of our side there, I will gladly give myself up and submit to the council of Basel.” At once the bishop shouted at the friar, “Be quiet, in the devil's name!” He told the notary to take care not to write down what she had said.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 2, pp. 4–5 (Isambart de la Pierre, 1450; our translation).}}

She was questioned seventeen times in February and March, and the trial went on into May. She told them that she had embraced Saint Catherine and Saint Margaret. She would rather have been torn apart by four horses, she said, than have come into France without God's leave. Once a notary claimed she had not answered a question eight days before. The record was read and she was proved right. So she told him she would pull his ears if he made a mistake again. On the morning of Thursday 24 May they led her out to a scaffold in the cemetery of the abbey of Saint-Ouen.{{n: Guérin, Petits Bollandistes, vol. 15, p. 404; Murray, Jeanne d'Arc, pp. 26, 92, 127, 304 (Murray's translation, modernized by us).}}
