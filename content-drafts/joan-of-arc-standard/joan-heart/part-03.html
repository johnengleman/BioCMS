Saint Joan Brings Help to Orléans

At the end of April an army gathered at the town of Blois with a convoy of food for Orléans. When it set out, the priests walked ahead of the soldiers behind a banner of their own. They sang the Veni Creator, the hymn to the Holy Spirit. She told the men to put their trust in the Lord and to go to confession, and her page saw her receive Holy Communion among them. “By my martin,” she promised, “they will be well led. Have no doubt of it.”{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 3, pp. 67 (Louis de Coutes), 105 (Jean Pasquerel); vol. 4, p. 5 (Perceval de Cagny; our translation).}}

The commander inside Orléans was Dunois, known as the Bastard of Orléans. He had already sent two men to the king to learn about this girl. Now the army came up along the south bank of the Loire, and he crossed the river to meet it. Things looked bad. To bring the food into the city, boats had to sail upstream past an English fort at the church of Saint-Loup. The wind was dead against them, the boats were few, and the army looked too weak to force a way past the English.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 3, pp. 3–5 (Dunois; our translation).}}

She went straight to the point. “Are you the Bastard of Orléans?” she asked. “I am,” he said, “and I am glad you have come.” Was it he, she asked, who had advised them to come by this side of the river, instead of going straight to where Talbot and the English were? He said that he and men wiser than he had given that advice, because they thought it safer. “In God's name,” she answered, “the counsel of God our Lord is safer and wiser than yours. You thought you could deceive me, and you deceive yourselves more. I bring you better help than ever came to any knight or city, because it is help from the King of Heaven. It does not come out of love for me. It comes from God Himself, who has taken pity on the city of Orléans at the prayer of Saint Louis and Saint Charlemagne. He would not let the enemy hold the lord of Orléans and his city.”{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 3, pp. 5–6 (Dunois, testimony of 22 February 1456; our translation).}}

At once, as if in a moment, the wind turned and blew for them. The sails went up, and the boats carried the food past Saint-Loup in spite of the English. Dunois had seen the wind change, and he had seen the food come into the city against a stronger English army. From these two things he judged that what she did came from God rather than from man. From that hour he had more hope in her than before.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 3, pp. 6–7 (Dunois; our translation).}}

He asked her to cross the river and come into the city with him. She did not want to. Her soldiers had confessed their sins, and she would not leave them. So the captains took the army back to Blois to cross the river there, and she agreed to go in. She entered Orléans beside Dunois, with the French captain La Hire, carrying her white banner.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 3, pp. 6–7 (Dunois; our translation).}}

Saint Joan Is Wounded and Orléans Is Freed

On 4 May fighting began outside the walls while she was up in her room. She came down at once and turned on her page: “Ah, you bloody boy! You did not tell me that French blood was being spilled!” At the city gate she met men carrying in a badly wounded Frenchman, and she said she never saw French blood without her hair standing on end. That day the French took the English fort at Saint-Loup.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 3, pp. 68–69 (Louis de Coutes), 212–213 (Jean d'Aulon; our translation).}}

Over the next two days, more English forts fell on the far bank. Then came the Tourelles, which guarded the south end of the city's bridge. At seven in the morning on Saturday 7 May she had the trumpets sounded and carried her banner to the edge of the ditch. Early in the fight an arrow struck her between the neck and the shoulder and went in half a foot deep. She did not leave the field, and she took no medicine for the wound. The assault went on all day. By about eight in the evening there was almost no hope of winning, and Dunois wanted to pull the tired army back into the city.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 3, pp. 69–70, 213–215 (Coutes, d'Aulon); vol. 4, p. 8 (Cagny); vol. 3, p. 8 (Dunois; our translation).}}

“Then the Maid came to him and asked him to wait a little longer. She mounted her horse and rode off alone into a vineyard, well away from the crowd. There she prayed for a short while. When she came back from that place, she took her banner in her hands at once and stood on the edge of the ditch. At that moment the English shuddered and were afraid.”{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 3, p. 8 (Dunois, testimony of 22 February 1456; our translation).}}

Dunois, the Bastard of Orléans, sworn testimony of 1456

Her steward Jean d'Aulon was down in the ditch under his shield. At sunset he had handed her banner to a soldier called the Basque. When she saw her banner in another man's hands, she seized it by the end and shook it. “Ah, my banner! My banner!” she cried. “Ah, Basque!” shouted d'Aulon. “Is this what you promised me?” The Basque pulled the banner free, and at that the whole army rallied and took the earthwork in front of the fort.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 3, pp. 216–217 (Jean d'Aulon, testimony of 28 May 1456; our translation).}}

That evening the French took the Tourelles, and the English captain Glasdale and other English leaders fell into the river and drowned. “In God's name,” she said to d'Aulon, “we will enter the city tonight by the bridge.” Back in the city her wound was dressed. She ate four or five pieces of bread dipped in wine mixed with a great deal of water, and nothing else that day.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 3, pp. 9 (Dunois), 217 (d'Aulon; our translation).}}

Early on Sunday 8 May the English came out of their camps and drew up in a line of battle. She rose from her bed and put on only a coat of mail. Then she forbade anyone to attack them. They must be allowed to go. The English marched away, and no one went after them. Orléans came to keep the eighth of May as a feast each year, with a Mass, a sermon, and a procession.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 3, p. 9 (Dunois; our translation); Benedict XV, Divina disponente (on the feast at Orléans).}}

The duke of Alençon once heard her tell the king what lay ahead. She had four tasks. She must drive out the English and have the king crowned at Reims. She must free the duke of Orléans from the English and raise the siege of Orléans. They must work well that year, she said. She would last a year, and little more.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 3, p. 99 (the duke of Alençon; our translation).}}
