Saint Joan Dies in the Old Market of Rouen

It was the morning of Wednesday 30 May 1431. Brother Martin Ladvenu came into her cell with another friar to tell her that she must die that day by fire. She cried out and tore at her hair. “Alas! Will they treat me so horribly and so cruelly? Must my body, whole and never defiled, be burned to ashes today? Ah! I would rather have my head cut off seven times over than be burned like this!” Then she appealed to God, the great Judge, against the wrongs done to her.{{n: Murray, Jeanne d'Arc, p. 158 (Jean Toutmouillé, 1450; Murray's translation, modernized by us); Quicherat, Procès, vol. 2, pp. 3–4.}}

Bishop Cauchon came to the cell. She said to him at once, “Bishop, I die through you.” “Ah, Joan,” he said, “be patient. You die because you did not keep what you promised us.” “Alas,” she answered, “if you had put me in the prisons of the Church, and given me into the hands of fitting guardians of the Church, this would not have happened. For this I summon you before God.”{{n: Murray, Jeanne d'Arc, pp. 158–159 (Toutmouillé; Murray's translation, modernized by us); Quicherat, Procès, vol. 2, p. 8 (Ladvenu).}}

That morning a priest named Pierre Maurice came to see her. “Master Pierre,” she asked him, “where shall I be this evening?” He asked whether she did not have good hope in God. She said she did, and that, God willing, she would be in Paradise. She made her confession to Brother Martin and received Holy Communion.{{n: Murray, Jeanne d'Arc, pp. 174, 302 (Jean Massieu; Jean Ricquier, who heard it from Pierre Maurice; Murray's translation, modernized by us); Quicherat, Procès, vol. 3, p. 191.}}

Then they took her to the Old Market of Rouen. Brother Martin and Massieu walked at her side, and more than eight hundred soldiers went with them, carrying axes and swords. A preacher named Nicolas Midi gave a sermon, and she listened to it quietly and patiently. At the end he said to her, “Joan, go in peace. The Church can no longer defend you, and it leaves you in the hands of the secular power.”{{n: Murray, Jeanne d'Arc, pp. 174–175 (Massieu); Quicherat, Procès, vol. 3, p. 159 (Massieu; our translation).}}

She knelt and prayed. She called on the Holy Trinity, on the Virgin Mary, and on the saints of Paradise, naming some of them. She asked people of every rank, of her own side and of the other, to forgive her and to pray for her, and she forgave them the harm they had done her. She went on like this for about half an hour. One man in the crowd heard her say, “Ah, Rouen! I am very much afraid that you will suffer for my death!”{{n: Murray, Jeanne d'Arc, p. 175 (Massieu; Murray's translation, modernized by us); Quicherat, Procès, vol. 3, p. 53 (Guillaume de la Chambre; our translation).}}

She asked for a cross. An Englishman standing there broke a stick and made a little cross from the pieces, and he gave it to her. She kissed it and put it inside her clothes, against her breast. The English soldiers were growing impatient. “What, priest!” one of them shouted at Massieu. “Will you have us dine here?” They told the executioner, “Do your duty.” Then she asked for the cross from the church.{{n: Murray, Jeanne d'Arc, pp. 175–176 (Massieu; Murray's translation, modernized by us); Quicherat, Procès, vol. 3, pp. 159–160.}}

“At the end Joan had such great contrition and such beautiful repentance that it was a wonder. Her words were so devout, so pitiful, and so Catholic that everyone who watched, a great crowd, wept hot tears. The Cardinal of England and several other English were forced to weep and to feel pity. The poor woman humbly asked and begged him, since he was near her at the end, to go to the nearest church and bring her the cross. She wanted him to hold it high before her eyes until the moment of death, so that the cross on which God hung would be before her sight as long as she lived. In the flames she never stopped, to the end, calling aloud the holy name of Jesus and begging the saints of Paradise for help.”{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 2, pp. 6–7 (Isambart de la Pierre, testimony of March 1450; our translation).}}

Brother Isambart de la Pierre, who stood near her at the end

With her last breath she cried “Jesus!” once more, so loudly that everyone standing there could hear it. Almost all of them wept.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 3, p. 186 (Maugier Leparmentier; our translation).}}

When it was over, the executioner came to the friars in despair. He was afraid that God would never forgive him for what he had done “to this holy woman.” He had been told to gather up her ashes and all that was left of her, and to throw them into the Seine. And he did.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 2, p. 7 (Isambart; our translation); vol. 3, p. 160 (Massieu).}}

Saint Joan's Mother Asks for Justice

Eighteen years later King Charles entered Rouen, and he ordered an inquiry into her trial. Her mother and her two brothers asked the Pope to judge the case again. On 11 June 1455 Pope Calixtus III named three judges to hear it.{{n: Benedict XV, Divina disponente (on the inquiry and the papal rescript); Quicherat, Procès, vol. 2, p. 74.}}

On the morning of 7 November 1455 the judges sat in the cathedral of Notre-Dame in Paris. Isabelle came in with her son Pierre, who had been taken prisoner with his sister at Compiègne. She was a widow now. With great groans and sighs she threw herself down at the judges' feet and held out the Pope's letter. Humbly and most urgently, she begged them to act on it without further delay, and to do justice to the widow, her sons, and her kin.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 2, pp. 84–86 (the record of 7 November 1455; our translation); p. 74, notes 1–2.}}

Then the people who had known her spoke. In the winter the judges' men came to Domrémy, and the old sexton told them about the bell and the wool. Hauviette was a married woman now, and she told them how she had wept. Dunois, the duke of Alençon, and Jean d'Aulon told what they had seen at her side. D'Aulon had served her for a whole year. In all that time he had seen nothing in her that ought not to be in a good Christian.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 2, pp. 412–419; vol. 3, pp. 2, 90–91, 207, 219 (our translation).}}

On 7 July 1456 the judges met in the archbishop's palace at Rouen. They declared her trial, the paper she had signed, and the sentences against her null and void. Brother Martin Ladvenu was there to hear the sentence read. They ordered a procession and a sermon at Saint-Ouen. The next day there was to be a sermon in the Old Market, in the very place where she had died. A fitting cross was to be set up there, so that she would be remembered forever.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 3, pp. 361–362 (the sentence of 7 July 1456; our translation).}}

The Church Declares Saint Joan a Saint

Nearly five centuries passed. On 16 May 1920 Pope Benedict XV came into Saint Peter's Basilica in Rome. More than sixty thousand people were there, and a hundred and forty of them were descendants of her family. Before that packed and joyful crowd the Pope declared her a saint: the farm girl who, in the words of his bull, “left her family to carry out the commands of God.”{{n: Benedict XV, Divina disponente (closing section; our translation); “Canonization of Joan of Arc,” Wikipedia.}}

Her Voices had once told her: “Bear it all willingly. Do not be anxious or troubled about your martyrdom. You will enter the kingdom of Paradise.”{{n: Benedict XV, Divina disponente (account of her death: “Omnia libenter sustine: ne sollicita sis et turberis erga martyrium: regnum Paradisi ingredieris”; our translation).}}
