Saint Joan Signs a Paper, Then Takes It Back

A great crowd filled the cemetery of Saint-Ouen. The judges were there, with the Cardinal of England and several bishops and abbots. A famous preacher named Guillaume Érard took his text from the Gospel of Saint John: “A branch cannot bear fruit by itself unless it remains in the vine.” He told the people that she had cut herself off from Holy Mother Church.{{n: Murray, Jeanne d'Arc, pp. 127–128 (the record of 24 May 1431).}}

Then he turned on her king. He cried out that the man who called himself king of France was a heretic and a schismatic, a man who had broken away from the Church. Charles had joined himself to the words and deeds of a worthless woman. He said it two or three times. At last he raised his finger at her. “It is to you, Joan, that I am speaking, and I tell you that your king is a heretic and a schismatic!” She answered: “By my faith, sir, with all respect, I dare to tell you and to swear on pain of my life that he is the most noble Christian of all Christians, and the one who best loves the faith and the Church. He is not what you say.” The preacher turned to the court usher Jean Massieu, who was there with her. “Make her be quiet,” he said.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 2, p. 16 (Jean Massieu, 1450; our translation); Murray, Jeanne d'Arc, pp. 172–173.}}

When Érard called on her to submit, she would not put the blame on anyone else. “Let all I have said and done be sent to Rome, to our Holy Father the Pope,” she said, “to whom, after God, I refer myself. I did these things by God's command. I blame no one for them, not my king and not anyone else. If there is any fault in them, it is mine and no one else's.” They told her this answer was not enough, because the Pope was too far away. The bishop began to read out the sentence against her.{{n: Murray, Jeanne d'Arc, pp. 128–129 (Murray's translation, modernized by us).}}

Érard read out a paper listing the things he wanted her to take back. She said she did not understand what it meant to abjure, and she asked for advice. Massieu told her that if she went against any of those articles, she would be burned. He advised her to leave the question to the Church Universal, the whole Church. “I leave it to the Church Universal whether I should abjure or not,” she said aloud. “You will abjure at once,” Érard answered, “or you will be burned.” Halfway through the bishop's sentence, she said she would hold to all that the Church ordained. A short paper of a few lines was read to her in French, and she signed it with a cross, using a pen that Massieu handed her. She did it from fear. Afterward a much longer paper went into the record in its place, full of confessions in her name.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 2, p. 17 (Massieu); vol. 3, pp. 53, 164 (witnesses on the short paper and her fear; our translation); Murray, Jeanne d'Arc, p. 130; Benedict XV, Divina disponente (on the two papers).}}

Some of those present asked that she now be taken to the Church's prison, since the Church had judged her. “Take her back to the castle she came from,” the bishop said. That afternoon she took off the man's clothes before the churchmen and put on a woman's dress, as she had been ordered. The man's clothes were put in a sack in her cell. Five English soldiers guarded her, three inside the room at night and two at the door. At night her legs were held in two pairs of irons. A chain ran through the foot of her bed to a great beam of wood and was locked, so that she could not move from the spot.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 2, pp. 17–18 (Massieu; our translation).}}

On the morning of Trinity Sunday she said to her guards, “Unchain me, and I will get up.” One of the English took away the woman's dress she had on. They emptied the sack and threw the man's clothes at her. “Get up,” they said. “Sirs,” she said, “you know this is forbidden me. I will not put it on, without fail.” They would give her nothing else. The quarrel went on until midday. At last the needs of her body forced her to go out, and she put on the man's clothes. When she came back, they would give her no other, however much she begged. She also told Brother Martin Ladvenu that she had been beaten and torn in that prison, and that an English lord had assaulted her.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 2, pp. 18–19 (Massieu, from what she told him), 8 (Martin Ladvenu; our translation).}}

On Monday 28 May the judges came to her cell. She told them she had gone back to men's clothes because they had not kept their promise to her. They had promised that she could go to Mass and receive her Savior, and that she would be taken out of irons. “I would rather die than be in irons!” she said. “But if I may go to Mass, and am taken out of irons and put in a kinder prison, I will be good and do as the Church wills.” Then they asked whether she had heard her Voices since Thursday.{{n: Murray, Jeanne d'Arc, pp. 136–137 (Murray's translation, modernized by us).}}

“Asked what they told her, she answered that God had sent word through Saints Catherine and Margaret. The word was about the great pity of the betrayal she had agreed to in making the abjuration and recantation to save her life. They said she was damning herself to save her life. … She also said that she said what she said from fear of the fire.”{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 1, pp. 456–457 (French minute, 28 May 1431; our translation).}}

The French record of her trial, 28 May 1431

In the same answer she said, “If I said that God had not sent me, I would damn myself, for it is true that God has sent me.” In the margin beside it the clerk wrote: “A deadly answer.” Did she believe her Voices were Saint Catherine and Saint Margaret? “Yes, I believe it, and that they come from God.” She had not meant to deny them, she said. “What I said, I said from fear of the fire. … I would rather do my penance all at once, that is, die, than bear the suffering of prison any longer.”{{n: Murray, Jeanne d'Arc, pp. 137–138 and p. 137, note 1 (“Responsio mortifera”; Murray's translation, modernized by us).}}

In front of Isambart de la Pierre and several others, she said that the English had done her great wrong and violence in that prison while she wore a woman's dress. Isambart saw her weeping. Her face was wet with tears and disfigured, and he was full of pity. “If you, my lords of the Church, had taken me and kept me in your prisons,” she said, “perhaps it would not have gone like this for me.” As the bishop left the prison he called out to the English who were waiting outside: “Farewell, farewell! Be of good cheer. It is done.” On 29 May the judges declared her a relapsed heretic. Early the next morning, a friar came to her cell.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 2, pp. 5 (Isambart de la Pierre), 8 (Ladvenu; our translation); Benedict XV, Divina disponente (29 May).}}
