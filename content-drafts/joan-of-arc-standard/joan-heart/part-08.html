A Note on the Sources

Most of this life rests on two sets of court records. The first is the record of her trial at Rouen in 1431, kept in French and Latin, where her own answers survive. The second is the record of the retrial of 1455 and 1456, where about twenty people who had known her gave sworn testimony, many of them twenty years or more after the events. The friars' first statements are dated 1449 in the record, but the year then began at Easter, so this life gives March 1450. Memories differ. Perceval de Cagny was a chronicler who served the duke of Alençon. He gives her age at Chinon as about eighteen. She told her judges in February 1431 that she was about nineteen, so this life counts her as about seventeen in 1429. Cagny is also the source for the king's loss of heart after her capture, for her parting from the duke, and for her oath “by my martin”; Jules Quicherat thought the word might mean a stick or Saint Martin.

Her uncle said she stayed six weeks in his house, and she said about eight days, so this life gives no length. Her page and her steward tell the afternoon of 4 May 1429 in different orders; each sentence here follows one of them. Dunois dated her wound at Orléans to “the 27th of May”. The day was Saturday 7 May, as the other sources show. The Latin for her time in the vineyard is not clear, so this life says only “a short while”. The vineyard comes from Dunois and the struggle for the banner from d'Aulon. Cagny puts her at Beaulieu for about four months. She put her stay in the tower of Beaurevoir at about four months too, so this life gives neither length. Cagny does not name the steward who served her at Beaulieu; Quicherat's note says he was Jean d'Aulon. The price paid for her is ten thousand gold francs in the bull of 1920 and fifteen or sixteen thousand coins in Cagny. The day of her capture is 23 May in Cagny and 24 May in the bull. Quicherat's notes are the source for the capture of her brother Pierre and for her mother's later life.

Isambart de la Pierre does not say on what day he advised her to appeal to the council of Basel. The account of the guards and the man's clothes on Trinity Sunday is what she told the usher Jean Massieu, and the assault in prison is what she told Brother Martin Ladvenu. Her words to the bishop on her last morning come from Ladvenu and from Brother Jean Toutmouillé, a Dominican of Rouen who was with him in her cell. Her question to Pierre Maurice comes through the chaplain Jean Ricquier, who heard it from Maurice. Both Massieu and Isambart tell of the church cross. The short paper she signed is said to be six or seven lines long by one witness and about eight lines by the bull. No source read for this life says whether her father came to Reims, or whether the cross ordered in 1456 was set up.

How This Life Was Researched

Three research files stand behind this life, drawing on about a dozen sources in Latin, French, and English. The chief ones are the five volumes of Jules Quicherat's edition of the trial and retrial records (1841–1849) and T. Douglas Murray's English translation of 1902. The others are the bull of canonization of 1920, Guérin's French life of the saint, the Catholic Encyclopedia of 1910, and two encyclopedia articles. The evidence includes Saint Joan's own answers at seventeen hearings, her dictated letters, the sworn testimony of some twenty people who knew her, and two chronicles of her own time, one friendly and one hostile. The researchers marked about twenty details as seldom found on English pages. For this telling every scene was checked again against the scanned text of Quicherat and Murray, so that each note could give the page. Most quotations were translated afresh from the Latin and French; the rest are Murray's English, put into plain modern words.
