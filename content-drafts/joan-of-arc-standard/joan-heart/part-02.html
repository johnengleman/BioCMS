Saint Joan Leaves Home for Vaucouleurs

She said nothing about it to her parents. She was afraid above all that her father would stop her. Her mother had told her about a dream he had, in which their daughter went off with soldiers. He told her brothers that if he thought the dream would come true, he wanted them to drown her. If they would not, he said, he would drown her himself.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 1, pp. 128–129, 131–132 (hearings of 12 March 1431; our translation).}}

When she was about sixteen, Durand Laxart came to her father's house to fetch her. He was married to a cousin of hers, and she called him her uncle. As she walked past her father's house with him, she called out, “To God! I am going to Vaucouleurs.” Hauviette had not known that her friend was leaving. When she heard, she wept a great deal, because she loved Saint Joan for her goodness and was her friend.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 2, pp. 416, 418–419, 444 (Gérard Guillemette, Hauviette, Durand Laxart; our translation); vol. 3, p. 209 (Jean d'Aulon on her age).}}

Long afterward a judge asked her whether she had done right to leave without her father's and mother's permission. She said that she had obeyed them in everything else. But God had commanded this, so it had to be done. If she had had a hundred fathers and a hundred mothers, she said, she would still have gone. Some time later she wrote to her parents, and they forgave her.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 1, pp. 128–129 (hearing of 12 March 1431, French minute; our translation).}}

In her uncle's house at Burey she told him what she meant to do. She would go to the Dauphin in France and have him crowned. Then she asked him a question. Had it not been said long ago that France would be ruined by a woman, and later restored by a virgin? She begged him to go to Robert de Baudricourt and ask the captain to send her to the Dauphin.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 2, p. 444 (Durand Laxart; our translation).}}

Her uncle took her to Vaucouleurs around Ascension in 1428. The captain was not moved. More than once he told Laxart to take her back to her father's house and give her some slaps. He asked her who her lord was. “The King of Heaven,” she said. Then she left the town with her uncle.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 2, p. 444 (Laxart); p. 456 and note 1 (Bertrand de Poulengy; our translation).}}

Near the start of Lent in 1429 she came back, and this time she stayed. She lodged three weeks with a woman of the town and spun in her house, as she had at home. Baudricourt refused her again. When she saw that he would not send her to the Dauphin, she took her uncle's clothes and said she meant to leave. But the third time she went to the captain, he received her.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 1, p. 53 (“twice refused, received the third time”); vol. 2, pp. 444, 446, 456 (Laxart, Catherine Royer, Poulengy; our translation).}}

The people of Vaucouleurs bought her a man's clothes and boots, and a horse was bought for her for twelve francs. Two of Baudricourt's men agreed to take her to the king. As she left, the captain told her: “Go, and let come what may.”{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 2, pp. 444–445; vol. 3, p. 208 (d'Aulon on the two men); vol. 1, p. 55 (our translation); Murray, Jeanne d'Arc, p. 12.}}

Saint Joan Meets the King at Chinon

The ride took eleven days, through country held by the English and their allies of Burgundy. She rode in a man's clothes with her small escort. On the way she stopped at the church of Saint Catherine at Fierbois and heard three Masses in one day. Early in March 1429 she reached the castle of Chinon, where the king was.{{n: Guérin, Petits Bollandistes, vol. 15, p. 394; Quicherat, Procès, vol. 1, p. 75; vol. 4, p. 3 (Perceval de Cagny).}}

Charles did not want to see her. He kept her waiting two days before he let her in. When she came into the room where he was, she knew him among many others. She said that she knew him by the counsel of her Voice.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 3, pp. 3–4 (Dunois); Murray, Jeanne d'Arc, p. 13 (Murray's translation, modernized by us).}}

The duke of Alençon was a prince of the king's own blood. He was out at Saint-Florent hunting quail when a servant brought him the news. A girl had come to the king, saying that God had sent her to drive out the English and raise the siege of Orléans. The next day the duke rode to Chinon and found her talking with the king. As he came near, she asked who he was. The king told her he was the duke of Alençon. “You are very welcome,” she said. “The more of the blood of the king of France there are together, the better.”{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 3, p. 91 (the duke of Alençon; our translation).}}

The next morning she came to the king's Mass and bowed when she saw him. He took her into a private room with the duke and one other lord and sent everyone else away. There she asked the king for several things. Above all, she asked him to give his kingdom to the King of Heaven. She said that after that gift the King of Heaven would do for him what He had done for the kings before him. He would set him back in his former state. After dinner the king walked in the meadows, and she ran with a lance. The duke saw how she handled it, and he gave her a horse.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 3, pp. 91–92 (the duke of Alençon; our translation).}}

Before the king would trust her with an army, churchmen had to question her. For about three weeks learned men at Chinon and at Poitiers asked about her Voices and her faith. She lodged in a tower of the castle of Chinon, and a page of fourteen or fifteen stayed with her through the day. He often saw her on her knees in prayer, and sometimes she was weeping.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 3, pp. 4 (Dunois), 66 (Louis de Coutes; our translation).}}

“I know neither A nor B,” she told two of the examiners when they came to her lodging. A friar who spoke the dialect of the Limousin asked her what language the Voice spoke. “A better one than yours,” she said. He asked if she believed in God. “Truly, more than you do!” He told her that God would not have her believed without some sign. “In God's name,” she answered, “I have not come to Poitiers to show signs. Send me to Orléans, and there I will show you the signs I was sent for.” One of the churchmen said in public that they saw nothing in her but what can be in a good Christian and a true Catholic.{{n: Murray, Jeanne d'Arc, pp. 265, 306–307 (Gobert Thibaut, squire, and Brother Seguin de Seguin, Dominican, 1456; Murray's translation, modernized by us); Quicherat, Procès, vol. 3, p. 209 (d'Aulon; our translation).}}

On 22 March she dictated a letter to the English, which began with the names “Jesus Maria”. In the name of the King of Heaven, she called on them to give up the good towns they had taken in France and to go back to their own country. At Tours the king had armor made for her. She also had a banner made, of white cloth, with the image of Our Lord holding a lily. She told her judges later that she loved her banner forty times more than her sword. She had never killed a man, she said. She carried the white banner herself when she went against the enemy, so that she would not kill anyone.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 5, p. 96 (letter of 22 March 1429; our translation); vol. 3, pp. 7 (Dunois), 93–94 (Alençon); vol. 1, p. 78; Murray, Jeanne d'Arc, p. 31 (Murray's translation, modernized by us).}}
