Saint Joan Brings the King to Reims

In June the army went out under the duke of Alençon against the English towns along the Loire. Before they left, the duke's wife had been afraid for him. He had lately been a prisoner of the English, and his ransom had cost a great deal. “Lady, do not be afraid,” Saint Joan told her. “I will give him back to you safe, and in as good a state as he is now, or better.”{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 3, p. 96 (the duke of Alençon; our translation).}}

The first town was Jargeau. After some days the captains met, and the heralds cried out for the assault. “Forward, gentle duke, to the assault!” she said. He thought it was too early. “Do not doubt,” she answered. “The hour is ready when it pleases God. Act, and God will act.” Then she asked him a harder question. “Ah, gentle duke, are you afraid? Do you not know that I promised your wife to bring you back safe and sound?”{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 3, pp. 95–96 (the duke of Alençon; our translation).}}

During the assault she told him to move from the place where he stood, or a gun in the town would kill him. He moved. A little later a lord named du Lude was killed on that very spot, and the duke was filled with fear and wonder. She herself climbed a ladder with her banner in her hand. The banner was struck, and a stone broke into pieces on her light helmet and threw her to the ground. She got up and called to the soldiers: “Friends, friends, up, up! Our Lord has condemned the English. At this hour they are ours. Take heart!” The town was taken at once.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 3, pp. 96–97 (the duke of Alençon; our translation).}}

Once her page saw a Frenchman strike an English prisoner on the head so hard that the man was close to death. She got down from her horse and had the Englishman make his confession. Then she held his head and comforted him.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 3, pp. 71–72 (Louis de Coutes; our translation).}}

On 18 June the English were beaten near the village of Patay, and their captain Talbot was taken prisoner. The army turned toward Reims. Every day at twilight she went into a church and had the bells rung for about half an hour. She gathered the begging friars who followed the king's army and had them sing a hymn to the Virgin Mary while she prayed.{{n: Thurston, “St. Joan of Arc,” Catholic Encyclopedia; Quicherat, Procès, vol. 3, pp. 13, 99 (Dunois; the duke of Alençon; our translation).}}

Charles was crowned in the cathedral of Reims on Sunday 17 July 1429. Her banner stood near the altar, and for a while she held it there herself. Later a judge asked her why her banner had been brought into the church, rather than the banners of the other captains. “It had been in the toil,” she said, “so it was right that it should be in the honor.”{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 1, pp. 104, 187 (“Ipsum vexillum suum fuerat in poena: bene rationis erat quod haberet honorem”; our translation); vol. 5, p. 127 (date).}}

That same day she had a letter sent to the king's enemy, the duke of Burgundy. She asked him to make a good and lasting peace with the king. “Forgive one another with a good heart, fully, as loyal Christians ought to do,” she wrote. “And if you want to make war, go against the Saracens.” Further on, she begged him “with joined hands” to make no more war against them.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 5, pp. 126–127 (letter of 17 July 1429; our translation).}}

In August the king rode on from Reims. At La Ferté and at Crépy-en-Valois the people came out to meet him, shouting “Noël!” for joy. She rode between the archbishop of Reims and Dunois, and she said to them: “Here is a good people. I have never seen a people so glad at the coming of so noble a king. How I wish I might be so happy, when my days are done, as to be buried in this land!” The archbishop asked her, “Joan, where do you expect to die?” She answered:{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 3, p. 14 and note 1 (Dunois; our translation).}}

“Where it pleases God, for I am no more sure of the time or the place than you are. And I wish it pleased God my Creator that I might now go away, lay down my arms, and go to serve my father and mother, keeping their sheep with my sister and my brothers, who would be so glad to see me!”{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 3, pp. 14–15 (Dunois, testimony of 22 February 1456; our translation).}}

Saint Joan of Arc, on the road from Reims, August 1429

But the road did not lead home. It led to Paris.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 4, p. 26 (Cagny).}}

Saint Joan Fails to Take Paris

Paris was held by the English and their Burgundian allies. The French attacked on Thursday 8 September 1429, the feast of the birth of Our Lady. She said later that she had no revelation to go there. She went because the soldiers asked her to. At about eight in the morning the army left its camp at La Chapelle in good order. She went with two lords to the Saint-Honoré gate, and she was among the first to go down into the ditch with her banner.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 4, pp. 26 (Cagny), 464 (the Paris journal); vol. 1, p. 147; Murray, Jeanne d'Arc, p. 73.}}

The fight was long and hard, under heavy fire from the guns. After sunset a crossbow bolt went through her thigh. She called all the more for the men to come up to the walls, and said the place would be taken. The lord of Gaucourt and others took her out of the ditch against her will. “By my martin,” she said, “the place would have been taken.”{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 4, pp. 26–27 (Cagny; our translation).}}

Early the next morning, wounded as she was, she was up and asking the duke of Alençon to sound the trumpets. She would not leave until she had the city. The king had stayed behind at Saint-Denis, and now his messengers came and called her back to him. She and most of the company were very unhappy, but they obeyed. They still hoped to cross the river Seine by a bridge the duke had built near Saint-Denis, and to come at Paris from the other side. The king learned of their plan, and in the night he had the bridge broken.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 4, pp. 27–28 (Cagny; our translation).}}

Her Voice had told her to stay at Saint-Denis, and she wanted to stay. The lords took her away against her will. On 13 September the king left Saint-Denis for the Loire. When she saw that nothing could change his mind, she laid all her armor before the image of Our Lady and the relics in the abbey of Saint-Denis. Then she went along with the king, with great sorrow.{{n: Murray, Jeanne d'Arc, pp. 13–14; Quicherat, Procès, vol. 4, p. 29 (Cagny; our translation).}}

At the town of Gien the army broke up. The duke of Alençon went home to his wife and his lands, and Saint Joan stayed with the king. She was very unhappy at the parting and most of all at losing the duke, whom she loved dearly. Soon afterward he asked the king to let her ride with him into Normandy, because many men would follow him if she came. The men who ruled the king's household and his war would not allow it. The duke never had her with him again.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 4, pp. 29–30 (Cagny; our translation).}}

She spent three weeks that autumn in the town of Bourges, lodging with a woman who shared her bed almost every night. Saint Joan went often to confession and asked to be taken to the early morning prayers. She gave freely to the poor, and she said she had been sent for the comfort of the poor and the needy. When women brought their rosaries for her to touch, she laughed. “Touch them yourselves,” she said. “They will be just as good from your touch as from mine.”{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 3, pp. 85–88 (Marguerite La Touroulde; our translation).}}

At the town of Saint-Pierre-le-Moûtier the French fell back from the wall, and d'Aulon, wounded in the heel and on crutches, found her nearly alone there. She took off her helmet and told him she was not alone. She had fifty thousand of her people with her. He could see only four or five men. Then she called out, “Brushwood and hurdles, everyone, to make a bridge!” and soon after the town was taken. In December the king made her and all her family nobles.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 3, pp. 217–218 (d'Aulon; our translation); vol. 2, p. 74, note 1 (Quicherat's note on the family).}}

In Easter week of 1430 she was in the trenches before the town of Melun when her Voices told her what was coming. “You will be taken before Saint John's Day,” they said. “It must be so. Do not be troubled. Accept it, and God will help you.” She begged them that when she was taken she might die soon, without suffering long in prison. They told her to accept it all, but they did not tell her the hour. From then on she talked more often with the captains about the war, but she did not tell them what her Voices had said.{{n: Murray, Jeanne d'Arc, pp. 57–58, 73 (hearings of 10 and 15 March 1431; Murray's translation, modernized by us); Quicherat, Procès, vol. 1, p. 115.}}

In May the enemy came against the town of Compiègne. She left the town of Crépy about midnight with three or four hundred fighting men. Her men told her they were too few. “By my martin, we are enough,” she said. “I will go and see my good friends of Compiègne.”{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 4, pp. 32–33 (Cagny; our translation).}}
