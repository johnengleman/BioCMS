On the morning of Thursday 22 February 1431, a prisoner was brought into a room at the end of the great hall of the castle of Rouen. She was a farm girl of about nineteen. Her judge was the bishop of Beauvais, and forty-eight learned churchmen sat with him to hear her. The day before, she had complained that they kept her fastened in chains and irons.{{n: Murray, Jeanne d'Arc, pp. 6–8 (the hearings of 21 and 22 February 1431).}}

The bishop ordered her to swear once more that she would tell the truth about everything they asked. “I swore yesterday,” she said. “That should be enough.” He pressed her: not even a prince could refuse to swear in a matter of faith. “I swore to you yesterday,” she answered, “and that should be quite enough for you. You burden me too much!” In the end she swore to tell the truth about what touched the Faith, and no more.{{n: Murray, Jeanne d'Arc, pp. 8–9 (Murray's translation, modernized by us).}}

Then a well-known teacher of theology began the questions, and she warned him before he started. “You may well ask me some things I will answer truthfully, and some I will not. If you were well informed about me, you would wish to have me out of your hands. I have done nothing except by revelation.” He asked whether she had learned a trade as a girl. Yes, she said. She had learned to sew and to spin, and at sewing and spinning she feared no woman in Rouen.{{n: Murray, Jeanne d'Arc, p. 9 (Murray's translation, modernized by us).}}

Two years before, this spinner had raised the siege of the city of Orléans. She had stood with her banner near the altar at Reims while her king was crowned. Yet she knew only the three prayers her mother had taught her, and she could not read. Before that morning's questions were over, the judges made her tell them where it had all begun. It began in her father's garden at noon on a summer day, when she was about thirteen.{{n: Murray, Jeanne d'Arc, pp. 6, 10; Quicherat, Procès, vol. 1, pp. 52, 104; Thurston, “St. Joan of Arc,” Catholic Encyclopedia.}}

Saint Joan Grows Up in Domrémy

Her father was Jacques d'Arc and her mother was Isabelle. They were farm people in the village of Domrémy, good Catholics with a good name among their neighbors. At home their daughter was called Jeannette. She spun and sewed and helped in the house, and sometimes she went out with her father to the plow.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 2, pp. 412, 417–418, 420 (the Domrémy witnesses of 1456); Murray, Jeanne d'Arc, p. 6.}}

Her closest friend was Hauviette, a girl three or four years younger. Hauviette often slept at the d'Arc house, out of love for her friend. On a Sunday in Lent each spring the children of the village walked out to an old tree they called the Ladies' Tree. They carried small loaves, ate them under the branches, danced, and drank from a spring nearby, and the two girls went together.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 2, pp. 413–414, 417–418 (Hauviette and other Domrémy witnesses; our translation).}}

She was cheerful and liked to see a happy face. When poor people came to the house, she gave them her bed and slept on the floor herself. She went to church gladly and often, to Mass and to the evening prayer, and the sexton found out how much that evening bell meant to her. When he did not ring it, she scolded him and told him it was not well done. Then she promised him wool if he would take care to ring it.{{n: Guérin, Petits Bollandistes, vol. 15, p. 390; Benedict XV, Divina disponente (account of her childhood); Quicherat, Procès, vol. 2, pp. 412–413 (Perrin Drapier, the sexton; our translation).}}

The village noticed her prayer, and not always kindly. People told her she went to church too much, and it often made her shy. When she heard the church bell ring out in the fields, she knelt down where she was. Sometimes at play she drew a little apart from the others to speak with God. Some of the children she played with laughed at her.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 2, pp. 418, 420 (Hauviette; Jean Waterin; our translation).}}

Saint Joan Hears a Voice in Her Father's Garden

On that February morning in Rouen she told her judges how it had started, and a clerk wrote her words down in Latin:

“She also said that when she was about thirteen, she had a voice from God to help her guide herself. The first time she was very much afraid. The voice came about noon, in summer, in her father's garden. She heard it on her right side, toward the church, and she seldom heard it without a light.”{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 1, p. 52 (hearing of 22 February 1431; our translation).}}

The record of her trial at Rouen, 22 February 1431

After she had heard it three times, she knew it was the voice of an angel. It taught her to be good and to go to church. Later she told her judges who it was. It was Saint Michael, and she saw him before her eyes with the angels of heaven all around him. “I saw them with my bodily eyes as well as I see you,” she said. “When they went from me, I wept. I would have liked them to take me with them.”{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 1, p. 52; Murray, Jeanne d'Arc, p. 25 (hearing of 27 February 1431; Murray's translation, modernized by us).}}

From then on she wanted more than anything to do what God asked of her through these Voices, and to stay in His grace. The first time she heard the Voice, she promised God to keep her virginity for as long as it pleased Him. Later Saint Catherine and Saint Margaret came to her as well, and she knew them by the greeting they gave her. “There is not a day when I do not hear this Voice,” she told her judges, “and I have great need of it. But I have never asked it for any reward except the salvation of my soul.”{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 1, p. 128; Murray, Jeanne d'Arc, pp. 13, 24 (Murray's translation, modernized by us).}}

Then the Voice began to ask more. Two or three times a week it told her that she must leave and go into France, and that she could not stay where she was. It told her to raise the siege of Orléans. That city lay on the river Loire, and an English army was camped around it. The English had taken many of the good towns of France. The king she would serve was Charles. He had not yet been crowned, and people still called him the Dauphin.{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 1, pp. 52–53; vol. 3, pp. 3–5 (Dunois); vol. 5, p. 96 (her letter to the English, on “the good towns”); vol. 2, p. 444 (Durand Laxart, who calls Charles “Dalphinus”).}}

The Voice told her to go to Robert de Baudricourt, the captain of the town of Vaucouleurs, who would give her men to take her to the Dauphin. “I answered that I was only a poor girl,” she said, “who knew nothing about riding or fighting.”{{n: Quicherat, Procès, vol. 1, p. 53 (“una pauper filia”); Murray, Jeanne d'Arc, p. 11 (Murray's translation, modernized by us).}}
