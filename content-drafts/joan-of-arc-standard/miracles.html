This list gathers the Church’s memory of God’s works through Saint Joan of Arc: the healings, the prophecies, and the wonders of her life and her death. The accounts come from her own answers at her trial in 1431 and from the sworn words of witnesses between 1450 and 1456. They also come from the bull of 1920 by which Pope Benedict XV declared her a saint, and from the old Lives of her. The five healings that the Church approved for her cause come first. Each status line says where the account comes from.

Miracles approved by the Church

The Church approved five healings for Saint Joan’s cause. Three were used for her beatification in 1909, and two for her canonization in 1920. Beatification is the step before canonization, when the Church declares a person blessed. Canonization is when the Church names a person a saint. Pope Benedict XV listed all five healings in his letter of 1920, which is called a bull. The bull calls them miracles worked through Saint Joan’s prayers, and it calls each healing instant and perfect. It gives no dates or places.

Four healings were proposed for the beatification. Pope Saint Pius X approved three of them in a decree of December 1908 and set the fourth aside. Pope Benedict XV approved two more healings for the canonization in a decree of 6 April 1918.

Sister Thérèse is healed of a stomach ulcer

Through the prayers of Saint Joan, God healed Sister Thérèse of Saint Augustine instantly and perfectly. The bull of 1920 records the healing. Her illness was a long-lasting ulcer of the stomach.

Status: approved by the Church for the beatification. Pope Saint Pius X confirmed the healing in a decree of December 1908.

Source: Benedict XV, bull Divina disponente, 16 May 1920, vatican.va.

Sister Julie is healed of a breast ulcer

Through the prayers of Saint Joan, God healed Sister Julie Gauthier of Saint Norbert instantly and perfectly. The bull of 1920 records the healing. Her illness was a painful, fungus-like ulcer on her left breast.

Status: approved by the Church for the beatification. Pope Saint Pius X confirmed the healing in a decree of December 1908.

Source: Benedict XV, bull Divina disponente, 16 May 1920, vatican.va.

Sister Jeanne Marie is healed of bone tuberculosis

Through the prayers of Saint Joan, God healed Sister Jeanne Marie Sagnier instantly and perfectly. The bull of 1920 records the healing. Her illness was a long-lasting tuberculosis of the bone and the tissue around it.

Status: approved by the Church for the beatification. Pope Saint Pius X confirmed the healing in a decree of December 1908.

Source: Benedict XV, bull Divina disponente, 16 May 1920, vatican.va.

Maria Antonia is healed of a foot disease

Through the prayers of Saint Joan, God healed Maria Antonia Mirandelle instantly and perfectly. The bull of 1920 records the healing. Her illness was a perforating disease of the sole of her foot.

Status: approved by the Church for the canonization. Pope Benedict XV confirmed the healing in a decree of 6 April 1918.

Source: Benedict XV, bull Divina disponente, 16 May 1920, vatican.va.

Thérèse Belin is healed of tuberculosis

Through the prayers of Saint Joan, God healed Thérèse Belin instantly and perfectly. The bull of 1920 records the healing. She had tuberculosis of the belly lining and of the lungs, and lasting damage to the mitral valve of the heart.

Status: approved by the Church for the canonization. Pope Benedict XV confirmed the healing in a decree of 6 April 1918.

Source: Benedict XV, bull Divina disponente, 16 May 1920, vatican.va.

The bull adds that the Church’s inquiries describe many other graces of soul and body that God granted through her help, and wonderful healings as well. It does not name them.

During Saint Joan’s life

Saint Joan announces a defeat near Orléans, 1429

The Catholic Encyclopedia says Saint Joan announced a French defeat near Orléans on 17 February 1429. It says she was at Vaucouleurs at the time. The defeat is now called the Battle of the Herrings. Officials confirmed it a few days later.

Status: a prophecy, told by the Catholic Encyclopedia (1910).

Source: Herbert Thurston, “St. Joan of Arc,” Catholic Encyclopedia, vol. 8 (1910), New Advent.

Saint Joan reaches Chinon through enemy country, 1429

The bull of 1920 says Saint Joan left Vaucouleurs on 13 February 1429 and reached Chinon after eleven days. It says she crossed many rivers at fords among her enemies in a wonderful way. The king heard of this from Robert de Baudricourt, the captain of Vaucouleurs. After that he agreed to receive her. Guérin’s Life also gives eleven days.

Status: stated in the bull of 1920 and in Guérin’s Life.

Source: Benedict XV, bull Divina disponente, 16 May 1920, vatican.va. Also Paul Guérin, Les Petits Bollandistes, vol. 15, 30 May, “Jeanne d’Arc, la Pucelle d’Orléans”, p. 394.

Saint Joan knows the king at Chinon, 1429

Saint Joan told her judges that she knew the king among the others when she came into his room. She said her Voice showed her who he was. Guérin’s Life says Charles stood apart in plain clothes, and Saint Joan walked straight to him. Charles pointed to another man and said that he himself was not the Dauphin. She answered that he was. The bull of 1920 says that the king told those near him that Saint Joan had told him secrets known to God alone.

Status: Saint Joan’s own words at her trial, with the scene as Guérin’s Life and the Catholic Encyclopedia tell it.

Source: Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc, vol. 1, p. 56, Internet Archive. Also Paul Guérin, Les Petits Bollandistes, vol. 15, 30 May, “Jeanne d’Arc, la Pucelle d’Orléans”, p. 395; Benedict XV, bull Divina disponente, 16 May 1920, vatican.va; Herbert Thurston, “St. Joan of Arc,” Catholic Encyclopedia, vol. 8 (1910), New Advent.

The secret sign for the king at Chinon

The Catholic Encyclopedia says Saint Joan made a secret sign known to Charles. It says the sign led him to believe in her. At her trial in 1431 she said the king had a sign of her deeds before he would believe her. Asked what revelations the king had received, she answered: “You will not have them from me yet this year.” On 13 March 1431 she told her judges what the sign was. She said an angel brought the king a crown and promised him the whole kingdom of France, through God’s help and her work.

Status: told by Saint Joan at her trial in 1431, and reported by the Catholic Encyclopedia (1910).

Source: Herbert Thurston, “St. Joan of Arc,” Catholic Encyclopedia, vol. 8 (1910), New Advent. Also Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc, vol. 1, pp. 75, 139–140, Internet Archive; T. Douglas Murray, trans., Jeanne d’Arc, Maid of Orleans (1902), pp. 69–70, Internet Archive.

Saint Joan promises the siege will end, 1429

Lord Gaucourt told the retrial in 1456 that churchmen examined Saint Joan for three weeks or more at Poitiers and at Chinon. He said they asked her what sign she would show so that people would believe her. She answered that the sign would be the end of the siege of Orléans. The siege ended on 8 May 1429.

Status: sworn testimony of Lord Gaucourt at the retrial of 1456.

Source: Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc, vol. 3, pp. 16–18, Internet Archive.

The sword with five crosses, Fierbois, April 1429

Saint Joan told her judges that her Voices showed her where a sword lay at the church of Saint Catherine at Fierbois. She sent a letter to the churchmen there, and an arms dealer from Tours went to look for it. The sword was in the ground and rusty, and it had five crosses on it. Its rust fell off at once when the churchmen rubbed it. The bull of 1920 says it was found as she had said, and Guérin says she told the king of it only after much urging.

Status: told by Saint Joan at her trial and stated in the bull of 1920.

Source: Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc, vol. 1, pp. 75–76, Internet Archive; Benedict XV, bull Divina disponente, 16 May 1920, vatican.va. Also Paul Guérin, Les Petits Bollandistes, vol. 15, 30 May, “Jeanne d’Arc, la Pucelle d’Orléans”, p. 396.

A Lyon letter records her predictions, April 1429

The Catholic Encyclopedia reports a letter written at Lyon on 22 April 1429 by a man called the Sire de Rotslaer. The letter says Saint Joan told people she would save Orléans and be wounded by an arrow without dying. It says she also told them the king would be crowned at Reims that summer. She was wounded on 7 May, and the siege ended on 8 May. Charles was crowned at Reims on 17 July 1429.

Status: a prophecy written down before the events. The Catholic Encyclopedia says the letter was delivered at Brussels and registered there before any of the predictions came true.

Source: Herbert Thurston, “St. Joan of Arc,” Catholic Encyclopedia, vol. 8 (1910), New Advent.

Saint Joan foretells her wound at Orléans, 1429

Guérin’s Life says Saint Joan told Charles before she left for Orléans that her saints had shown her she would be wounded there. She said the wound would not stop her work. Guérin says she also announced on the morning of 7 May that she would be wounded that day. He writes that the prediction came true.

Status: told in Guérin’s Life. See also the Lyon letter above.

Source: Paul Guérin, Les Petits Bollandistes, vol. 15, 30 May, “Jeanne d’Arc, la Pucelle d’Orléans”, pp. 396, 398.

The wind turns on the Loire, spring 1429

The commander Dunois swore in 1456 that the wind was against the boats that had to carry food upstream to Orléans. Saint Joan told him that she brought better help than ever came to any knight or city, because it came from the King of Heaven. He said the wind changed at once, as in a moment, and the boats passed the English-held church of Saint-Loup. He said three things made him judge her deeds to be from God rather than from man. One was the wind, and another was the food that reached the city in spite of the English. The third was her account of a vision of Saint Louis and Charlemagne praying for the king and the city.

Status: sworn testimony of Dunois at the retrial of 1456.

Source: Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc, vol. 3, pp. 5–7, Internet Archive.

Saint Joan says the wind will change, 1429

Lord Gaucourt told the retrial in 1456 that he agreed with Dunois about the change of wind. He said Saint Joan had declared beforehand that the weather and the wind would soon change, and that the change came right after her words. He added that she had also said the food would come into the city freely.

Status: sworn testimony of Lord Gaucourt at the retrial of 1456.

Source: Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc, vol. 3, p. 18, Internet Archive.

Saint Joan’s arrow wound, Orléans, 7 May 1429

Dunois swore in 1456 that an arrow struck Saint Joan between the neck and the shoulder in the attack on the fort at the bridge. It went in half a foot, and she did not stop fighting or take any medicine. Guérin says she pulled the arrow out herself after her saints came to her and comforted her. The attack was on 7 May, and the siege ended on 8 May.

Status: sworn testimony of Dunois, with a vision told in Guérin’s Life.

Source: Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc, vol. 3, p. 8, Internet Archive; vol. 4, p. 8, Internet Archive; Paul Guérin, Les Petits Bollandistes, vol. 15, 30 May, “Jeanne d’Arc, la Pucelle d’Orléans”, p. 398.

Saint Joan promises to take the bridge fort

The chronicler Perceval de Cagny wrote that no captain thought the fort at the bridge of Orléans could be taken in a month, even with half as many men again. He says Saint Joan told those with her that she would take it the next day and come back into the town over the bridges. The attack came on Saturday 7 May, and the French took the fort that day. The steward Jean d’Aulon told the retrial in 1456 that she also said that day: “In God’s name, we will enter the town tonight by the bridge.” He says the French then entered Orléans over the bridge.

Status: reports by Cagny and by d’Aulon, who testified in 1456. Guérin also says she entered the town by the bridge, as she had announced.

Source: Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc, vol. 4, pp. 8–9, Internet Archive; vol. 3, p. 217, Internet Archive. Also Paul Guérin, Les Petits Bollandistes, vol. 15, 30 May, “Jeanne d’Arc, la Pucelle d’Orléans”, p. 398.

An English captain’s death foretold, Orléans, 1429

The Journal of a Paris citizen says people said that Saint Joan ordered an English captain at Orléans to leave the siege with his men or suffer for it. He insulted her, and she answered that the English would soon leave but that he would never see it. She added that many of his men would be killed. The Journal says this came true, because the captain drowned.

Status: told in the Journal of a Paris citizen for 1429, as a thing people were saying. Quicherat’s note names the captain as William Glasdale, and Dunois testified in 1456 that Glasdale drowned when the fort fell.

Source: Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc, vol. 4, p. 463, Internet Archive; vol. 3, p. 9, Internet Archive.

The English leave Orléans, 8 May 1429

Dunois swore that on the morning after the fort fell the English came out of their camps and formed lines for battle. Saint Joan rose from her bed and put on only a coat of mail. She would not allow any attack and said the English must be let go. They marched away, and nobody chased them. Guérin gives her words as: “Let them go. My Lord does not want a fight today.”

Status: sworn testimony of Dunois at the retrial of 1456, also told in Guérin’s Life.

Source: Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc, vol. 3, p. 9, Internet Archive; Paul Guérin, Les Petits Bollandistes, vol. 15, 30 May, “Jeanne d’Arc, la Pucelle d’Orléans”, p. 398.

Soldiers say Orléans was won by a miracle

The duke of Alençon swore in 1456 that he was not at Orléans in May 1429. Later he saw the English forts there. He said he believed the fort at the bridge and the fort of the Augustins fell by miracle more than by force of arms. He said he would have dared to hold either fort for six or seven days against any force, even with a few men. By his account, soldiers and captains at Orléans credited nearly everything done there to a miracle of God.

Status: sworn testimony of the duke of Alençon at the retrial of 1456. He gives the soldiers’ words as he heard them.

Source: Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc, vol. 3, p. 94, Internet Archive.

Saint Joan warns a duke at Jargeau, 1429

The duke of Alençon swore in 1456 that during the attack on Jargeau Saint Joan told him to leave the place where he stood. She said a war machine in the town would kill him if he stayed. He moved, and a little later a lord named du Lude was killed on that spot. The duke said he felt great fear and amazement. Earlier Saint Joan had told the duke’s wife not to be afraid, because she would bring him back safe.

Status: sworn testimony of the duke of Alençon at the retrial of 1456.

Source: Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc, vol. 3, pp. 95–97, Internet Archive.

A stone strikes Saint Joan at Jargeau, 1429

The duke of Alençon swore that Saint Joan was on a ladder at Jargeau holding her banner. A stone broke on her light helmet and threw her to the ground. She got up and said: “Friends, friends, up, up! Our Lord has condemned the English. At this hour they are ours. Take heart!” The town was taken at once, and more than 1,100 English were killed in the chase.

Status: sworn testimony of the duke of Alençon. Guérin tells it too.

Source: Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc, vol. 3, p. 97, Internet Archive; Paul Guérin, Les Petits Bollandistes, vol. 15, 30 May, “Jeanne d’Arc, la Pucelle d’Orléans”, p. 400.

Saint Joan promises victory at Patay, 1429

The duke of Alençon swore in 1456 that the English army was coming on the day of the battle of Patay, 18 June 1429. Many of the king’s men were afraid, but Saint Joan said: “In God’s name, we must fight them. If they were hanging from the clouds we would have them, because God sends them to us so that we may punish them.” He says she was sure of victory. She also said in French that on that day the noble king would have the greatest victory he had had in a long time. The duke said the English were beaten and killed without great difficulty. The English captain Talbot was taken prisoner.

Status: sworn testimony of the duke of Alençon at the retrial of 1456.

Source: Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc, vol. 3, pp. 98–99, Internet Archive.

Verses of a Maid from the Bois-Chenu, 1429

Dunois testified that a paper was sent to the English commander Suffolk fifteen days after he was taken prisoner at Jargeau. It held four lines of verse. They said that a Maid would come from the Bois-Chenu and ride on the backs of the archers and against them. The Catholic Encyclopedia says a prophecy of a maiden from the Bois-Chenu was known in the countryside.

Status: sworn testimony of Dunois about a popular prophecy.

Source: Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc, vol. 3, p. 15, Internet Archive; Herbert Thurston, “St. Joan of Arc,” Catholic Encyclopedia, vol. 8 (1910), New Advent.

A restless horse stands still at Selles, 1429

Guy de Laval and his brother André wrote to their mother and grandmother on 8 June 1429. Guy says he saw Saint Joan mount her horse at Selles-en-Berry on the Monday evening. The big black horse was very wild at the door of her lodging and would not let her mount. Saint Joan said, “Take him to the cross.” The cross stood in front of the church nearby. Then she mounted without the horse moving, as if he were tied.

Status: told in a letter of 8 June 1429 by Guy de Laval, who says he saw it.

Source: Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc, vol. 5, pp. 107–108, Internet Archive.

Saint Joan says she will last a year

The duke of Alençon swore in 1456 that he heard Saint Joan tell the king she would last a year and not much more. He said she named four tasks for that year. Two were to drive out the English and to crown the king at Reims. Two more were to free the duke of Orléans and to raise the siege of Orléans. Saint Joan was captured at Compiègne in May 1430, a little over a year after Orléans.

Status: a prophecy in sworn testimony at the retrial of 1456. Guérin reads her capture as its fulfilment.

Source: Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc, vol. 3, p. 99, Internet Archive; Paul Guérin, Les Petits Bollandistes, vol. 15, 30 May, “Jeanne d’Arc, la Pucelle d’Orléans”, pp. 399, 402.

A dead baby shows life at Lagny

Saint Joan told her judges about a baby at Lagny who was three days old. People said he had shown no life in that time, and he was as black as her coat. She prayed with the girls of the town before the statue of Our Lady, and the baby yawned three times as his color began to return. He was baptized, died soon after, and was buried in blessed ground. Asked whether the town said her prayer had done it, she answered: “I did not ask about it.”

Status: told by Saint Joan at her trial. The bull of 1920 calls it the raising of a dead infant so that he could be baptized. The trial record gives no date.

Source: Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc, vol. 1, pp. 105–106, Internet Archive; Benedict XV, bull Divina disponente, 16 May 1920, vatican.va.

Saint Joan foretells her capture at Melun, 1430

Saint Joan told her judges that in Easter week of 1430 her Voices spoke to her in the trenches at Melun. The Voices were Saint Catherine and Saint Margaret. They told her she would be taken before Saint John’s Day, and that it must be so. She was not to be dismayed but to accept it willingly, and God would help her. They did not tell her the hour. Guérin gives 23 May 1430 as the day she was captured at Compiègne. The bull adds that at a Mass at Compiègne she told those near her that she would soon be betrayed and handed over to death.

Status: Saint Joan’s own account at her trial, repeated in the bull of 1920, in Guérin’s Life, and in the Catholic Encyclopedia.

Source: Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc, vol. 1, p. 115, Internet Archive; Benedict XV, bull Divina disponente, 16 May 1920, vatican.va. Also Paul Guérin, Les Petits Bollandistes, vol. 15, 30 May, “Jeanne d’Arc, la Pucelle d’Orléans”, p. 402; Herbert Thurston, “St. Joan of Arc,” Catholic Encyclopedia, vol. 8 (1910), New Advent.

Compiègne is freed before Martinmas, 1430

Saint Joan told her judges that Saint Catherine spoke to her after her leap from the tower of Beaurevoir. The saint told her to confess and ask God’s pardon, and said that Compiègne would have help before Saint Martin’s Day in winter, which is 11 November. A note in Murray’s translation of the trial says a relief army set out on 25 October, and the siege was lifted in early November. The bull of 1920 says her Voices foretold that Compiègne would be freed before that feast, and that this came true. The chronicler Cagny writes that her steward told her at Beaulieu that Compiègne would fall, and she answered that it would not. Quicherat’s note says this steward was Jean d’Aulon.

Status: a prophecy told by Saint Joan and stated in the bull of 1920.

Source: Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc, vol. 1, pp. 151–152, Internet Archive; T. Douglas Murray, trans., Jeanne d’Arc, Maid of Orleans (1902), p. 74, note 1, Internet Archive; Benedict XV, bull Divina disponente, 16 May 1920, vatican.va. Also Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès, vol. 4, p. 35, Internet Archive.

Her Voice says to answer boldly, Rouen 1431

Saint Joan told her judges on 24 February 1431 that her Voice had woken her the morning before. She said she sat up on her bed, joined her hands, and asked its help. The Voice told her to answer boldly and said that God would help her. Guérin says her saints stayed with her and comforted her through her imprisonment.

Status: Saint Joan’s own words at her trial. The comfort of her saints is told in Guérin’s Life.

Source: Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc, vol. 1, pp. 62–63, Internet Archive; Paul Guérin, Les Petits Bollandistes, vol. 15, 30 May, “Jeanne d’Arc, la Pucelle d’Orléans”, p. 404.

Saint Joan foretells a loss greater than Orléans

At her trial on 1 March 1431, Saint Joan said the English would lose a bigger prize than Orléans within seven years. She said they would lose everything in France, through a great victory that God would send the French. Asked how she knew, she said it was by a revelation and that she did not know the day or the hour. Murray’s note says the English lost Paris in 1436, and the Catholic Encyclopedia dates the loss to 12 November 1437. Both dates fall within seven years.

Status: a prophecy recorded at the trial. The Catholic Encyclopedia and the bull of 1920 report it as fulfilled.

Source: Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc, vol. 1, p. 84, Internet Archive; T. Douglas Murray, trans., Jeanne d’Arc, Maid of Orleans (1902), p. 38, note 1, Internet Archive; Herbert Thurston, “St. Joan of Arc,” Catholic Encyclopedia, vol. 8 (1910), New Advent; Benedict XV, bull Divina disponente, 16 May 1920, vatican.va.

Her Voices rebuke her, Rouen, 28 May 1431

On 28 May 1431 Saint Joan told the court what her Voices had said. Saint Catherine and Saint Margaret had told her that she had done a great wrong. Four days before, she had signed a short paper at Saint-Ouen in which she took back what she had said. She said her Voices told her she was damning herself to save her life. She also said she had spoken from fear of the fire. The bull of 1920 says that in her last hour she understood what her Voices had foretold: “Bear it all willingly. Do not be anxious or troubled about your martyrdom. You will enter the kingdom of Paradise.”

Status: Saint Joan’s own words at her trial, with a passage in the bull of 1920.

Source: Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc, vol. 1, pp. 456–457, Internet Archive; Benedict XV, bull Divina disponente, 16 May 1920, vatican.va.

Birds eat bread from Saint Joan’s lap

The Journal of a Paris citizen records a story that Saint Joan’s supporters told about her childhood. They said that when she was very small and tended sheep, the birds of the woods and fields came when she called. They ate bread from her lap as if they were tame.

Status: told by her supporters and recorded in the Journal of a Paris citizen for 1429.

Source: Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc, vol. 4, p. 463, Internet Archive.

After Saint Joan’s death

These accounts begin on the day she died, 30 May 1431. The last tells of the years that followed.

The name of Jesus, Rouen, 30 May 1431

Brother Isambart de la Pierre said under oath in 1450 that Saint Joan never stopped calling on the name of Jesus in the flames. He said she spoke it once more as she bowed her head. Maugier Leparmentier of the archbishop’s court at Rouen told the retrial in 1456 that she cried “Jesus” more than six times and again with her last breath. The bull of 1920 and Guérin say many who were there saw the name of Jesus written in the flames. The priest Thomas Marie told an inquiry in 1452 that he had heard this from many people.

Status: sworn testimony of Brother Isambart (1450) and of Maugier Leparmentier (1456) for her cries. The name in the flames is told by the bull of 1920, by Guérin, and by the priest Thomas Marie (1452), who heard it from many.

Source: Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc, vol. 2, pp. 6–7, 63, Internet Archive; vol. 3, pp. 185–186, Internet Archive; T. Douglas Murray, trans., Jeanne d’Arc, Maid of Orleans (1902), p. 211, Internet Archive; Benedict XV, bull Divina disponente, 16 May 1920, vatican.va. Also Paul Guérin, Les Petits Bollandistes, vol. 15, 30 May, “Jeanne d’Arc, la Pucelle d’Orléans”, p. 412.

A soldier sees a white dove, Rouen 1431

Brother Isambart de la Pierre told the inquiry of 1452 about an English soldier who hated Saint Joan. He said the man saw her spirit leave her like a white dove flying away from France. According to Isambart this was the same soldier who had sworn to bring wood for her fire (next entry). The bull of 1920 says the soldier stood stunned and still, and afterward said he saw a dove flying out of the flames. Guérin says he fainted and thought he saw a white dove.

Status: told by Brother Isambart de la Pierre at the inquiry of 1452, and repeated in the bull of 1920 and in Guérin’s Life. The soldier is not named.

Source: T. Douglas Murray, trans., Jeanne d’Arc, Maid of Orleans (1902), p. 191, Internet Archive; Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc, vol. 2, p. 63, Internet Archive; Benedict XV, bull Divina disponente, 16 May 1920, vatican.va. Also Paul Guérin, Les Petits Bollandistes, vol. 15, 30 May, “Jeanne d’Arc, la Pucelle d’Orléans”, p. 412.

A soldier who hated Saint Joan repents

Brother Isambart de la Pierre said in 1452 that an English soldier hated Saint Joan greatly. The man had sworn to bring a bundle of wood for her fire. He did so, and he heard her call on the name of Jesus as she died. He was stunned, and his companions led him to a nearby tavern. That afternoon he told a Dominican friar that he had done a grave wrong and was sorry. Isambart was present.

Status: told by Brother Isambart de la Pierre at the inquiry of 1452.

Source: T. Douglas Murray, trans., Jeanne d’Arc, Maid of Orleans (1902), p. 191, Internet Archive.

The heart that would not burn, Rouen, 1431

Brother Isambart de la Pierre said under oath in 1450 that the executioner came to him and to Brother Martin Ladvenu just after the burning. The man was in despair, and he feared that God would never pardon what he had done “to this holy woman.” He told them that oil, sulphur, and charcoal had not burned Saint Joan’s heart and entrails to ashes. Isambart said the man was as astonished as if he had seen a clear miracle. In 1452 Isambart said the man also feared he was damned for burning a saint. The bull of 1920 says her heart stayed unharmed and full of blood. Guérin says the fire could not consume her heart and entrails. Both say the English had her heart thrown into the Seine with her ashes.

Status: told by Isambart in 1450 and 1452, and stated in the bull of 1920.

Source: Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc, vol. 2, p. 7, Internet Archive; T. Douglas Murray, trans., Jeanne d’Arc, Maid of Orleans (1902), pp. 161–162, 191, Internet Archive; Benedict XV, bull Divina disponente, 16 May 1920, vatican.va. Also Paul Guérin, Les Petits Bollandistes, vol. 15, 30 May, “Jeanne d’Arc, la Pucelle d’Orléans”, p. 412.

Guérin tells how her judges died

Guérin’s Life lists the ends of the men behind Saint Joan’s trial. Bishop Cauchon died suddenly in his barber’s hands, and the prosecutor Estivet was found dead on a dung heap near Rouen. The vice-inquisitor Le Maistre vanished, and the priest Loyseleur died suddenly in a church at Basel. The preacher Midi died of leprosy. Guérin adds that the Duke of Bedford died of grief and shame and that King Henry VI lost his throne twice and was killed. He links these deaths to her warning to her judges. She had told them that they could not do to her what they threatened without harm to their own bodies and souls.

Status: told in Guérin’s Life. The bull of 1920 says that those guilty of her death died shamefully.

Source: Paul Guérin, Les Petits Bollandistes, vol. 15, 30 May, “Jeanne d’Arc, la Pucelle d’Orléans”, pp. 413–414; Benedict XV, bull Divina disponente, 16 May 1920, vatican.va.

A Note on the Sources

A few dates and details differ between sources. The Catholic Encyclopedia says Saint Joan was at Vaucouleurs when she announced the defeat on 17 February 1429. The bull of 1920 says she left that town on 13 February. The bull gives 24 May 1430 for her capture, and Guérin and the chronicler Cagny give 23 May. Dunois’s testimony, as Quicherat prints it, dates her arrow wound to 27 May, while the other sources give 7 May. The Catholic Encyclopedia places the wound in her breast, and Dunois places it between the neck and the shoulder.

The Journal of a Paris citizen was written by an opponent of Saint Joan. Its writer added a Latin note to the story of the birds, in veritate apocryphum est, which means “in truth, it is not genuine.” The Catholic Encyclopedia says it is most commonly believed that the secret sign she gave the king settled a doubt he had about his birth.
