# Joan of Arc — scene cards, group 1 (SC-01 to SC-05)

Method note. I read the Latin and French of Quicherat (public domain) as OCR text from archive.org: vol. 1 `procsdecondam01joanuoft`, vol. 2 `procsdecondam02joanuoft`, vol. 3 `procsdecondam03joanuoft`, vol. 4 `procsdecondam04joanuoft`. The OCR has letter errors. In each quotation I corrected letter noise only (for example "puisabat" to "pulsabat"). Any doubtful word is marked [OCR "..."]. Check every quotation against the page scan before print. Printed page numbers are Quicherat's. All translations into plain English are mine. I did not use Murray for these cards. Rights for all Quicherat texts: public-domain.

Note for the writer. Quicherat vol. 1 prints Joan's trial answers mostly as the Latin minute, with some French. The French original of the 22 February hearing is not in the volume I read. So the "poor girl" line exists only in Latin: "pauper filia".

## SC-01 — The sexton and the wool (Domrémy, about 1420s)
- Status: full
- When and where: Domrémy, Joan's childhood; no year given. Told in 1456 (Domrémy inquiry, late January; the record gives Thursday 29 January for the first sittings) by neighbors. I did not re-read the year on the page.
- Who was there: Joan; Perrin Drapier ("Perrinus Drapparius"), then sexton (a footnote glosses "matricularius" as "Marguillier"), about 60 at the inquiry, a farm-village neighbor. Hauviette, wife of Gérard de Syonne, about 45 at the inquiry, Joan's friend, three or four years younger. Jean Waterin of Greux, about 45.
- What happened:
  1. Drapier says he often saw Joan come to church for Mass and for compline [W-S2 p.413].
  2. When he did not ring the compline bell, Joan blamed and scolded him, saying it was not well done [W-S2 p.413].
  3. She then promised him wool, so that he would take care to ring the bell [W-S2 p.413].
  4. Hauviette: people told Joan she went to church too devoutly, and she often felt shy about it. The priest said she confessed often [W-S2 p.418].
  5. Waterin: in the fields, when she heard the bell, she knelt. At play she sometimes drew apart and spoke to God, "as it seemed to him". He and others laughed at her [W-S2 p.420].
- Details: spinning, house work, going to the plow with her father, and herding her father's animals in turn [W-S2 pp.418, 420]. Drapier adds that she did not swear by God or the saints [W-S2 p.412].
- Words spoken (Drapier reporting Joan, in Latin indirect speech): see Testimony. There is no direct quotation of Joan in this scene. Waterin: "Ad Deum!" belongs to SC-03.
- Testimony (Perrin Drapier, Quicherat vol. 2 p.412-413, Latin; public-domain):
  "et sæpe videbat dictam Johannam venire ad ecclesiam, in missis et completoriis; et, dum ipse testis non pulsabat completorias, ipsa Johanna eumdem testem causabat et vituperabat, dicendo quod non erat bene factum; et ipsa Johanna tunc promiserat eidem testi dare lanas, ad finem ut diligentiam haberet pulsandi completorias."
  plain: "He often saw Joan come to church, to Mass and to the evening prayer. When he did not ring the bell for the evening prayer, Joan blamed him and scolded him. She said it was not well done. Then she promised to give him wool, so that he would take care to ring it."
  Second passage (Hauviette, p.418): "ac ibat libenter et sæpe ad ecclesiam et loca sacra, et sæpe habebat verecundiam eo quod gentes dicebant sibi quod nimis devote ibat ad ecclesiam"
  plain: "She went gladly and often to church and holy places. She was often embarrassed, because people told her she went to church too devoutly."
- Inner life: none in this scene. The neighbors only describe her.
- What changed: nothing in the plot. It shows her will and her prayer before the call.
- Not in the sources: that the bell was missed because of work or sleep; the weather or the hour; what the wool was for; that Drapier gave in or apologized; the village's reaction. The record has only the scolding and the promise.
- Sources: W-S2 pp.412-413, 417-421 (read in Latin). Footnote on Drapier's office, p.413 n.1.

## SC-02 — The Voice in the garden (Domrémy, summer, age about 13)
- Status: full
- When and where: summer, about noon, in her father's garden at Domrémy. Joan says she was about 13. She told it at Rouen on 22 February 1431 (Quicherat vol. 1 p.52; the same answer is summarized on p.216-217).
- Who was there: Joan alone. The Voice and a light, as she tells it.
- What happened:
  1. At about 13 she "had a voice from God to help her govern herself". The first time she was very afraid [W-S1 p.52].
  2. It came about noon, in summer, in her father's garden [W-S1 p.52].
  3. She heard it on the right side, toward the church, and seldom without a light on the same side [W-S1 p.52].
  4. After she heard it three times, she knew it was the voice of an angel [W-S1 p.52].
  5. The Voice taught her to behave well and go to church. It said she must go to France [W-S1 p.52].
  6. It said this two or three times a week. It said she must leave and that she could not stay where she was. It told her to raise the siege of Orléans [W-S1 pp.52-53].
  7. It told her to go to Robert de Baudricourt at Vaucouleurs, who would give her men. She answered that she was a poor girl who knew nothing of riding or making war [W-S1 p.53].
- Details: the Latin minute is not consistent on fasting. On p.52 she "had fasted the day before" ("jejunaverat die praecedenti"). On p.216 the same answer reads "she was fasting, and had not fasted the day before". Tell the writer to leave fasting out. She says the light was seldom absent [p.52]. On 27 February she names St Michael as the first voice, seen "before her eyes" with angels, and says she wept when they left [W-S1 p.73].
- Words spoken, "a poor girl" (Latin, Joan's answer in the 22 Feb minute, p.53): "ipsa Johanna tunc respondit quod erat una pauper filia quae nesciret equitare, nec ducere guerram."
  plain: "Joan then answered that she was a poor girl who did not know how to ride or to lead a war."
- Inner life: she says the Voice seemed "worthy" and that she believed it was sent from God (p.52): "sibi videbatur esse digna vox, et credit quod eadem vox erat missa a parte Dei". plain: "The voice seemed worthy to her, and she believed it was sent from God." Also, from p.128: on the first hearing she vowed to keep her virginity as long as it pleased God.
- Testimony (Joan, Quicherat vol. 1 p.52, Latin minute; public-domain):
  "Ulterius confessa fuit quod, dum esset aetatis xiii annorum, ipsa habuit vocem a Deo, pro se juvando ad gubernandum. Et prima vice, habuit magnum timorem. Et venit illa vox, quasi hora meridiana, tempore aestivo, in horto patris sui... Audivitque vocem a dextro latere versus ecclesiam, et raro eam audit sine claritate."
  plain: "She also said that when she was about thirteen she had a voice from God to help her guide herself. The first time she was very afraid. The voice came about noon, in summer, in her father's garden. She heard the voice on her right side, toward the church, and she seldom heard it without a light."
- What changed: she knows she must go to France. She fears her father and tells no one at home (see SC-03).
- Not in the sources: what she was doing in the garden; the words of the first Voice beyond "behave well, go to church, you must go to France"; the church bell ringing; her father or mother in the garden; the shape or face of any being on the first day (she refused to say, p.52).
- Sources: W-S1 pp.52-53, 73, 128, 216-217. Joan's trial answers in Latin; the French original of the garden passage is not in the volume.

## SC-03 — "To God! I am going to Vaucouleurs" (Domrémy and Vaucouleurs, 1428-1429)
- Status: full
- When and where: Domrémy and Greux, then Burey and Vaucouleurs. Dates are unclear. Poulengy puts her first visit to Baudricourt "around the Ascension" and says Quicherat's note makes this 1428 [W-S2 p.456 n.1]. She came back "near the start of Lent" [p.456]. Catherine Royer says she stayed three weeks in her house [p.446]. Laxart: she stayed six weeks at his house in Burey [p.443]. The outline's "13 Feb" date is not in these passages.
- Who was there: Joan; her uncle (cousin by marriage), Durand Laxart of Burey; Robert de Baudricourt, captain of Vaucouleurs; Gérard Guillemette and Jean Waterin, neighbors; Hauviette; Catherine, wife of Henri Royer of Vaucouleurs; the priest Jean Fournier; Bertrand de Poulengy.
- What happened:
  1. Guillemette saw Joan pass her father's house with Laxart and heard her say "Ad Deum, ego vado ad Vallis-Colorem" [W-S2 p.416]. Waterin saw her leave Greux and heard "Ad Deum!" [p.421].
  2. Hauviette did not know Joan was leaving. She wept much when she learned, "because she loved her so much for her goodness, and she was her friend" (OCR "cliliiïcbat" read as "diligebat") [p.418-419].
  3. Laxart: Joan said it had been said that France would be ruined by a woman and restored by a virgin. She asked him to tell Baudricourt to send her to the Dauphin [p.444].
  4. Laxart: Baudricourt told him several times to take her back to her father and "give her slaps" ("alapas") [p.444]. The outline's "whip" is wrong: "alapa" is a slap or blow.
  5. Poulengy: Baudricourt asked who her lord was. She answered, "the King of Heaven" [p.456].
  6. Joan (1431, in French): she disobeyed her parents only in this leaving; later she wrote to them and they forgave her [W-S1 p.129].
  7. Joan (1431, Latin): Baudricourt refused her twice and received her the third time [W-S1 p.53]. At her leaving he said, "Go, and let what may come, come" [p.55].
- Details: townspeople had clothes, boots, and a horse made for her. The horse cost twelve francs [W-S2 p.445]. She stayed in the Royer house three weeks and spun there [p.446].
- Words spoken:
  - Joan, to her father's house: "Ad Deum, ego vado ad Vallis-Colorem." plain: "To God! I am going to Vaucouleurs." [W-S2 p.416, Latin; Guillemette]
  - Joan: "Nonne alias dictum fuit quod Francia per mulierem desolaretur, et postea per virginem restaurari debebat?" plain: "Was it not said once that France would be ruined by a woman and later restored by a virgin?" [W-S2 p.444, Laxart]
  - Joan, French, 1431: "elle a bien obey à eulx, excepté de ce parlement; mais depuis leur en a escript, et luy ont pardonné." plain: "She obeyed them well in all other things, except in this leaving. Later she wrote to them, and they forgave her." [W-S1 p.129]
  - Baudricourt, as Joan reports (Latin): "Vade, et quod inde poterit venire, veniat." plain: "Go, and let come what may." [W-S1 p.55]
- Inner life: asked if she thought she did right to leave without her parents' leave, she said that since God commanded it, it had to be done. If she had had a hundred fathers and mothers, she would have left [W-S1 p.129, French: "puis que Dieu le commandoit, s'elle eust c pères et c mères... si fust-elle partie"]. She also says she feared her father would stop her [p.128]. She said her mother told her the father had dreamed she would go off with soldiers and said he would drown her himself first [p.131, secondhand].
- Testimony (Durand Laxart, vol. 2 p.444, Latin; public-domain):
  "Qui Robertus pluries eidem testi dixit quod reduceret eam ad domum sui patris, et daret ei alapas ; et quando dicta Puella vidit quod ipse Robertus eam ad locum ubi erat ipse Dalphinus duci facere nolebat, ipsa recepit vestes ipsius testis, et dixit quod volebat recedere"
  plain: "Robert often told the witness to take her back to her father's house and give her slaps. When the Maid saw that Robert would not have her taken to the Dauphin, she took the witness's clothes and said she wanted to leave."
- What changed: she leaves home without her parents' leave and wins escort from the garrison.
- Not in the sources: what she told her parents (she says she told them nothing, p.129); a tearful goodbye with her mother or father; the date she left; the words Baudricourt said to her face, other than the one line she reports; how she knew Baudricourt at first sight beyond her statement that the Voice told her (p.53). Laxart's text says he led her to Vaucouleurs; Quicherat's note says it should read Saint-Nicolas [p.444 n.1].
- Sources: W-S1 pp.53, 55, 128-131; W-S2 pp.416, 418-419, 421, 443-448, 455-458.

## SC-04 — A duke arrives at Chinon (March 1429)
- Status: full
- When and where: Chinon, in the king's lodging, March 1429. Cagny gives "the sixth day of the month of March" (the "[vi]" is Quicherat's bracket) [W-S4 p.3]. D'Alençon deposed on 31 May (I could not read the day-number cleanly; the year is 1456).
- Who was there: Joan; King Charles VII; Jean, duke of Alençon, then a prince of the blood, about 50 at the deposition; the lord de La Trémoille (named in the king's private room).
- What happened:
  1. D'Alençon was at Saint-Florent, strolling out to chase quail ("ad fugandum aux cailles"). A servant told him a maid had come to the king, saying she was sent by God to drive out the English and raise the siege of Orléans [W-S3 p.91].
  2. Next day he went to Chinon and found Joan speaking with the king [p.91].
  3. As he came near, Joan asked who he was. The king said he was the duke of Alençon [p.91].
  4. She told him he was very welcome and that the more of the king's blood gathered together, the better [p.91].
  5. Next day she came to the king's Mass, bowed on seeing him, and the king led her to a room. D'Alençon and La Trémoille stayed; the rest were told to leave [p.91].
  6. She made several requests, among them that the king give his kingdom to the King of Heaven. The King of Heaven would then do for him what he had done for his predecessors [p.91-92].
  7. After dinner the king walked in the meadows, and Joan ran with a lance. Seeing how she carried it, d'Alençon gave her a horse [p.92].
- Details: Cagny says she was "of about 18", of plain farm people, and spoke of the war like knights and squires [W-S4 p.3]. She was examined by clerics, then by knights; she asked for armor and horses; the king kept her until May [p.3].
- Words spoken (French inside the Latin deposition, W-S3 p.91): "Vous soyez le très bien venu. Quanto plures erunt de sanguine regis Franciae insimul, tanto melius." plain: "You are very welcome. The more of the king of France's blood there are together, the better."
- Testimony (the duke of Alençon, vol. 3 p.91-92, Latin; public-domain):
  "Tunc ipsa Johanna fecit regi plures requestas, et inter alias quod donaret regnum suum Regi coelorum, et quod Rex coelorum, post hujusmodi donationem, sibi faceret prout fecerat suis praedecessoribus, et eum reponeret in pristinum statum"
  plain: "Then Joan made several requests to the king. Among others, she asked that he give his kingdom to the King of Heaven. After this gift, the King of Heaven would do for him as he had done for his predecessors and set him back in his former state."
- What changed: the duke believes in her. He gives her a horse and later rides with her.
- Not in the sources: the duke's thoughts or feelings; the king's face or answer; what Joan wore; what the lance looked like; weather; the room. D'Alençon says he does not remember "many other things" said before dinner [p.92].
- Sources: W-S3 pp.90-93; W-S4 p.3.

## SC-05 — The wind turns on the Loire (spring 1429)
- Status: full
- When and where: on the left bank of the Loire, upstream of Orléans, across from the church of Saint-Loup, held by many English (Quicherat note, vol. 3 p.5 n.1). Dunois deposed on 22 February 1456 (record year "1455", with the last digit in editor's brackets). The deposition gives no day for the scene. The outline's 29 April is not in this passage.
- Who was there: Joan; Jean, Bastard of Orléans (later count of Dunois), the deposing witness, about 51; Brother Nicolas de Giresme; the captains with the convoy: the marshals de Rais and de Boussac, the admiral de Culant, La Hire, Ambroise de Loré; the archbishop of Reims, Regnault de Chartres.
- What happened:
  1. Joan came with the army and the supplies from Blois to the Loire. The army looked too weak to carry the supplies past the English. Boats were hard to get. The wind was "totally contrary" to boats going upstream [W-S3 p.5].
  2. Joan asked, "Are you the Bastard of Orléans?" He answered yes and said he was glad she had come [p.5].
  3. She asked if he had advised that she come by this side and not go straight where Talbot and the English were. He said he and wiser men had given that advice, thinking it safer [p.5].
  4. She answered that the counsel of God was safer and wiser than theirs. She brought the best help any city had ever had, from the King of Heaven [p.5-6].
  5. At once, as in a moment, the wind turned and favored them. The sails were set; Dunois and Brother Nicolas boarded the boats and passed Saint-Loup against the will of the English [p.6].
  6. Dunois asked her to cross the Loire and enter Orléans. She hesitated because she did not want to leave her men, who were well confessed. He got the captains to cross at Blois [p.6-7].
  7. She entered Orléans with him, carrying her white banner, with La Hire [p.7].
- Details: her banner was white and had an image of the Lord holding a lily flower [p.7].
- Words spoken (Latin, W-S3 p.5-6; Dunois renders Joan's words in Latin and begins her answer with a French oath):
  - Joan: "Estis vos Bastardus Aurelianensis?" plain: "Are you the Bastard of Orléans?"
  - Bastard: "Ita sum, et laetor de adventu vestro." plain: "I am, and I am glad you have come."
  - Joan: "En nom Dieu, consilium Dei Domini nostri est securius et sapientius quam vestrum." plain: "In God's name, the counsel of God our Lord is safer and wiser than yours."
- Testimony (Dunois, vol. 3 p.6, Latin; public-domain):
  "Vos credidistis me decipere, et vosmet ipsum plus decipitis, quia ego adduco vobis meliorem succursum quam venerit unquam cuicumque militi aut civitati, quia est succursus a Rege coelorum. Non tamen procedit amore mei, sed ab ipso Deo, qui, ad requestam sancti Ludovici et sancti Karoli Magni, habuit pietatem de villa Aurelianensi"
  plain: "You thought you were deceiving me, and you deceive yourselves more. I bring you better help than has ever come to any knight or city, because it is help from the King of Heaven. It does not come out of love for me. It comes from God himself, who, at the request of Saint Louis and Saint Charlemagne, took pity on the city of Orléans."
- Inner life: she says the help is God's and not hers. She said this before the wind turned.
- What changed: the boats pass; Dunois "had good hope of her, more than before" and persuades her into the city.
- Not in the sources: a date; Joan's voice (tone or volume); a gesture; that Dunois answered with anger or shame; the strength or direction of the wind in miles; that the wind turned only because she prayed (he says only "at once, as in a moment"). Dunois reports her words in Latin, so any French wording comes from his memory, 27 years later.
- Sources: W-S3 pp.2-7.
