# Joan of Arc — notes: WITNESSES (Rehabilitation testimony, chronicles, chronology)

Researcher: witnesses focus. Date of work: 2026-10-01. Every fact below comes from text I read in this session (Quicherat volumes on archive.org, read as raw OCR). No fact from memory. Items I know but did not find are in "Other: to check".

## 1. Identity (partial: only what these sources show)

- Names in the sources: "Johanna" / "Johanneta" / "Jehanne" / "la Pucelle" (Latin trial text); "Johanna la Pucelle" (Domrémy witnesses) [S2 p.414].
- Village: Domrémy ("Dompnum-Remigium", "Dompno-Remigio"); neighbour hamlet Greux ("Greu"); parish church of St Remigius (Rémi) [S2 p.412, p.415].
- Parents: Jacques d'Arc and Isabelle ("Ysabelleta"), farm workers, "boni catholici", good name [S2 p.412, p.417-418].
- Godfather Jean Morel; godmothers Jeannette (wife of Thévenin le Charron/Rotarius) and Jeannette (widow of Thiesselin, of Vitel/Neufchâteau) [S2 p.412, 415].
- Tradition type, feast days, tier: not in my sources. First agent / other agents must supply.

## 3. Timeline (one line per event)

Dates in 15th-century style are as the witness gave them. `NEW` = I do not expect popular English pages to have it (my judgment, not tested).

- Childhood, Domrémy. Joan is "bona, simplex et dulcis filia"; goes often to church and is teased for going too much; spins, does house work, sometimes herds her father's animals [Hauviette, S2 p.417-418]. Hauviette says Joan was older than she by "tribus aut quatuor annis" and that she slept in Joan's father's house "amorose" [S2 p.417]. NEW
- Childhood, church bells. Perrin Drapier, then sexton, says Joan scolded him when he did not ring compline, and promised him wool as a reward to make him diligent [S2 p.412-413]. NEW
- Childhood, fairy tree. Four witnesses say the tree is "l'Arbre des Dames"; the children went there each year on "Laetare Sunday" (the Sunday "des Fontaines") with small loaves, ate, danced, and drank at the Fountain of the Rannorum; no witness says he saw fairies [S2 p.413-414, 416, 418, 420-421]. Hauviette says she herself went with Joan [S2 p.418].
- Childhood, raid. When armed men came, the Domrémy people fled to Neufchâteau with their animals; Joan and her parents went too and came back after 3-4 days (Drapier) or 4-5 days (Guillemette), Joan helping the innkeeper "la Rousse" in between [S2 p.414, 416-417].
- Age 13 (her own words at Rouen): the first voice was St Michael, whom she saw "before her eyes", with angels. "She did not come to France except by God's order" [S1 p.73].
- Departure from Domrémy. Gérard Guillemette saw Joan pass her father's house with her uncle Durand Laxart and heard her say "Ad Deum, ego vado ad Vallis-Colorem" ("To God, I am going to Vaucouleurs") [S2 p.416]. Hauviette did not know of the departure and wept much because she loved Joan for her goodness [S2 p.418-419]. Jean Waterin saw her leave Greux and heard her say "Ad Deum" [S2 p.421].
- Vaucouleurs. Rouen coppersmith merchants ("chauderonniers") Nicolas Saussart and Jean Chando later told Jean Moreau of Rouen that Joan insisted to Baudricourt he must take her to the king; he agreed [S3 p.191-192, second-hand].
- Chinon. Cagny: in the first months, a maid "de l'eage de xviii ans ou environ" came to the king at Chinon "[le vi] jour dudit mois de mars" (the "[vi]" and the year "[1428]" are editor's brackets: Quicherat's note; the real year is 1429 by modern count, 1428 is Easter-style dating) [S4 p.3]. The king, his household and "all others, of whatever rank" were amazed that she talked of the war "as well as knights and squires who had been in the war all the time" [S4 p.3, paraphrase]. 
- Chinon. D'Alençon was hunting quail at Saint-Florent when a messenger told him a maiden had come to the king. Next day he went to Chinon and found Joan speaking with the king [S3 p.91]. Joan asked who he was, and the king said "the duke of Alençon". Joan: "Vous soyez le très bien venu." and then "Quanto plures erunt de sanguine regis Franciae insimul, tanto melius." [S3 p.91] NEW (quail).
- Chinon, next day. At the king's Mass, Joan bowed to the king; he took her to a room with d'Alençon and La Trémoille; she asked him to give his kingdom to the King of Heaven [S3 p.91-92, paraphrase]. After dinner she ran with a lance in the meadow; d'Alençon, seeing it, gave her a horse [S3 p.92]. NEW
- Chinon, Coudray. Louis de Coutes (page, 14-15 years old, in Gaucourt's service) says Joan was lodged in a tower of the castle of Coudray; he lodged there with her by day; at night she had women with her; he often saw her kneeling in prayer, sometimes weeping [S3 p.66]. NEW
- Poitiers. D'Aulon says the king was then in Poitiers; Joan was brought by two gentlemen of Baudricourt, Bertrand and Jean de Metz; she spoke to the king in secret; theologians examined her; one said publicly "they saw nothing in her but what can be in a good Christian and true Catholic" [S3 p.208-209]. D'Aulon puts her age at "seize ans ou environ" [S3 p.209]. The "report" of the queen of Sicily and her ladies: Joan was "une vraye et entière pucelle" [S3 p.209-210]. D'Alençon: the Poitiers clerics found nothing against the Catholic faith [S3 p.93].
- Tours. D'Alençon's horse is seen by Coutes in Tours at the house of "Lapau"; Joan is given armor and "un estat" by the king; Coutes becomes her page there [S3 p.66-67]. D'Alençon: the king had armor made for her [S3 p.93-94].
- Blois. Joan leaves Blois with the convoy armed; Coutes says she told the men to trust the Lord and to confess; he saw her receive the Eucharist in the company [S3 p.67]. Cagny: she told them "Par mon martin, ilz seront bien menez" [S4 p.5].
- Orléans, arrival. Dunois: the city was besieged; he was in command; he sent de Villars and Jamet du Tillay to the king; they told him the king first refused to see her, kept her waiting two days, then had her examined for 3 weeks to a month [S3 p.3-4]. NEW detail: the wait. On the Loire bank: Joan: "Estis vos Bastardus Aurelianensis?" He: "Ita sum, et laetor de adventu vestro." Joan then asked if he had advised the army to go by the north bank, not toward Talbot [S3 p.5]. Wind change: Dunois says the contrary wind changed at once, sails were set, and the boats passed Saint-Loup [S3 p.6]. Joan carried a white banner "in quo erat figura Domini nostri tenens florem lilii in manu sua" [S3 p.7]. Entered Orléans with La Hire [S3 p.7].
- Orléans, 4 May (Cagny): she called the captains after dinner, was ready and on horseback before any, had her trumpet sounded, and had the Burgundy gate opened [S4 p.6].
- Orléans, the night of 4 May (d'Aulon, with Coutes): Joan lay down to rest with her hostess; d'Aulon rested on a bed in her room. Suddenly she got up and woke him: her counsel told her to go against the English but she did not know whether to the bastilles or against Fastolf. Then a noise arose; she ran out, met a page on a horse, made him get down, and rode to the Burgundy gate without waiting for d'Aulon [S3 p.212]. Coutes tells the same night: she came back from Dunois angry that there would be no assault that day; she went to the boulevard and told the English in the next one to leave in God's name; the "Bastard of Granville" insulted her [S3 p.68]. Then, upstairs, she came down and said to Coutes the words in Quote 3. He passed her banner out of the window [S3 p.68-69]. Same line, two witnesses.
- Orléans, 4 May. At the gate, she saw a wounded Frenchman carried in; d'Aulon: "jamais n'avoit veu sang de François que les cheveulx ne luy levassent ensur" [S3 p.213]. Saint-Loup was taken. Coutes: after, she dined little, "bis in die" (twice a day), often only a mouthful of bread [S3 p.69].
- Orléans, 5-6 May. Saint-Jean-le-Blanc and the bastille of the Augustins taken [S3 p.69-70, 213-215]. D'Aulon tells of two knights who quarreled "arrogant words" and ran together to the palisade; the gunner Jean le Canonnier shot an English giant dead; the Spaniard Alphonse de Partada is named [S3 p.214-215]. NEW
- Orléans, 6 May. Joan opens the Burgundy gate against the advice of lords; the boulevard of the bridge is attacked "from the first hour till night" [S3 p.70-71]. Dunois dates the wound "vicesima septima die maii" (27th of May, as the OCR reads; the true date is 7 May, an error in the Latin or the OCR: flag): an arrow "between neck and shoulders, half a foot deep"; she did not stop and took no medicine [S3 p.8]. NEW dating problem.
- Orléans, the evening, Dunois: the army was tired; Dunois wanted to withdraw. Joan asked him to wait a little, rode alone to a vineyard, prayed about a quarter of an hour, came back, took her banner, and stood on the edge of the ditch; the English trembled and the French took the boulevard [S3 p.8]. D'Aulon: the banner scene: he gave the banner to "le Basque", entered the ditch with his shield above him; Joan took the banner by the end, cried "Haa! mon estandart! mon estandart!" and shook it; d'Aulon cried "Ha, Basque! est ce que tu m'as promis?" The Basque pulled it free; the troops rallied and took the boulevard [S3 p.216-217]. Same line, two witnesses.
- Orléans, 7 May evening. Joan, wounded, was dressed by d'Aulon; she said "On entrera ennuyt en la ville par le pont" [S3 p.217]. Dunois: she ate four or five "vipas" (sops of bread) in wine with much water and no other food that day [S3 p.9]. NEW. Next morning the English left their camp in order; Joan rose from bed, armed herself in one garment ("jasseran", a mail coat), and forbade attack: let them go [S3 p.9]. The commander Glasdale (Classidas) drowned [S3 p.9, with S4 p.463].
- Loches, after Orléans: Dunois says Joan went with him to the king at Loches [S3 p.9] (rest of passage not read).
- Jargeau, June. D'Alençon: Joan said "Avant, gentil duc, à l'assault!" and when he thought it too early: "Nolite dubitare, hora est parata quando placet Deo" and "operate, et Deus operabitur". She then said "A! gentil duc, times-tu? Nonne scis quod ego promisi uxori tuae te reducere sanum et incolumem?" [S3 p.95-96]. The wife of d'Alençon had feared for him, he had been a prisoner and the ransom was great [S3 p.96]. NEW. Joan told him to move from a place or "la machine" would kill him; soon after, Monseigneur du Lude was killed there [S3 p.96-97]. Joan on a ladder with her banner was hit on the helmet by a stone; she fell, rose, and cried "Amys, amys, sus! sus! Nostre Sire a condempné les Angloys. Ista hora sunt nostri" [S3 p.97]. More than 1,100 killed during the pursuit [S3 p.97]. Coutes says Suffolk and "La Poule" were taken at Jargeau [S3 p.72].
- Beaugency and Patay: d'Alençon was acting lieutenant; the constable Richemont arrived. Joan to him: "Ha beau connestable, vous n'estes pas venu de par moy; sed quia venistis, vos bene veneritis." [S3 p.98] Before Patay: "En nom Dieu, il les fault combatre; s'ilz estaient pendus aux nues nous les arons" [S3 p.98-99, with OCR damage; check]. Talbot taken; d'Alençon told him he had not expected this that morning; Talbot: "fortuna guerrae" [S3 p.99].
- Compassion for the enemy. Coutes: a Frenchman struck an English prisoner on the head almost to death; Joan got off her horse, had him confess, held his head and comforted him [S3 p.71-72]. NEW
- Prediction of one year. D'Alençon: she said to the king she would last "per annum et non multum plus"; she named four tasks: drive out the English, crown the king at Reims, free the duke of Orléans, raise the siege of Orléans [S3 p.99].
- Reims, 17 July 1429 (the date is not in the passages I read; coronation is named in S3 p.72, Coutes was present). Christine de Pizan finished her poem 31 July 1429 [S5 p.3, 14].
- After Reims, Bourges. Marguerite La Touroulde: Joan stayed three weeks in her house in Bourges (on the order of d'Albret), slept "almost every day" with her in the same bed, confessed often, and asked to go to Matins [S3 p.85-86]. NEW
- Paris, 8 September 1429. Cagny: assault on the Porte Saint-Honoré; Joan took her banner and was among the first into the ditch by the Marché aux Pourceaux; she was struck in the thigh by a crossbow bolt after sunset; she urged all to approach; Gaucourt and others took her out against her will: "Par mon martin, la place eust esté prinse" [S4 p.26-27]. Next morning she got up early and asked d'Alençon to sound the trumpets: she would not leave until she had the city [S4 p.27]. Hostile account of the same day in the Bourgeois [S4 p.464-466]: she stood on the edge of the ditches with her banner and cried "Rendez vous, de par Jhesus, à nous tost"; a man shouted "paillarde! ribaude!" and shot her through the leg; her standard-bearer was shot between the eyes and died. Same line, two witnesses.
- Saint-Pierre-le-Moûtier (autumn 1429, month not in my passage). D'Aulon, wounded in the heel and on crutches, saw Joan almost alone by the wall after the French fell back; she took off her helmet and said she was not alone but had 50,000 of her people; he saw only four or five men with her; she called "Aux fagoz et aux cloies tout le monde, affin de faire le pont!"; the town fell soon after [S3 p.217-218]. NEW
- 23 May 1430, Compiègne. Cagny: left Crépy at about midnight with 300-400 fighters, "Par mon martin, nous suymes assez; je iray voir mes bons amis de Compiengne"; arrived at sunrise [S4 p.32-33]. Skirmish in the meadow; Burgundian ambush from the Mont de Clairoy; the captain raised the bridge and closed the gate; she was taken by five or six men. Told to surrender her faith, she said: "Je ay juré et baillé ma foy à autre que à vous et je luy en tendray mon serement" [S4 p.34]. Bourgeois: captured 23 May 1430 by Jean de Luxembourg [S4 p.467]. NEW (Cagny's replies).
- Prison after capture. Cagny: held 3-4 days by Luxembourg, then at the castle of Beaulieu in Vermandois about 4 months; then sold to Bedford for 15-16 thousand "saluz" (his own number) [S4 p.34-35]. A steward told her Compiègne would fall; she answered it would not [S4 p.35].
- Rouen trial, 1431. Ladvenu and Isambart say the bishop of Beauvais, Cauchon, put her in a secular prison (guarded by the English) when he could have used a Church prison [S2 p.7-8]. The questions were too hard for "a poor woman, simple enough that she barely knew Pater noster and Ave Maria" [S2 p.8, paraphrase of the OCR].
- Rouen, abjuration and relapse. Isambart: told to submit to the Church; she asked to be taken to the Pope; Isambart advised the council of Basel; she asked what a council was; when told it included her own side as many as the English, she cried she would submit; Cauchon shouted "Taisez-vous, de par le dyable!" and told the notary not to write it; the English threatened to throw Isambart in the Seine [S2 p.4-5]. NEW
- Rouen, after relapse. She said publicly she had put on men's clothes again because the English had done her wrong and violence in prison; Isambart saw her weeping, her face wet with tears and disfigured [S2 p.5]. Ladvenu: she told him she was beaten and torn, and that "un millourt d'Angleterre l'avoit forcée" ("an English lord had forced her") [S2 p.8]. Cauchon, leaving the prison, said to Warwick and the English "Farowelle, Farowelle, il en est faict, faictes bonne chière" [S2 p.8; Isambart p.5]. Same line, two witnesses.
- 30 May 1431, death at the Old Market. Bourgeois: "La vigille du Saint-Sacrement ... le trentiesme jour de may ... mil quatre cens trente et ung" a sermon was given at Rouen, and she was burned that day [S4 p.467-468].
- Death scene. Isambart: great contrition; "tous ceulx qui la regardoient... pleuroient à chaudes larmes", the English cardinal and others wept; she asked for the cross from the nearby church and had it held before her eyes to the end; in the flames she kept saying "Jhesus" and calling on the saints; at the last she bowed her head and said the name once more [S2 p.6-7]. NEW detail: the cross.
- Death, no secular sentence. Both friars say the secular judges did not sentence her; two sergeants made her step down and delivered her to the executioner [S2 p.6, 8-9].
- After death. Isambart and Ladvenu: the executioner came to them in terror at what he had done; he said that despite oil, sulphur and charcoal he could not burn "les entrailles et le cuer" to ashes; "un miracle tout évident" [S2 p.7]. Ladvenu: the executioner said he had never feared an execution as this one: the fame, and the cruel way of tying her high on a plaster scaffold [S2 p.9]. NEW. Legend/status: report of a witness, not an approved miracle.

## 4. Character (one line each)

- Pious, frequent confession and Mass; the village teased her for going too often to church [Hauviette, S2 p.418].
- Bells: she knelt in the fields when she heard the bell [Waterin, S2 p.420].
- Simple and ignorant except in war; "multum simplex et ignorans... nisi in facto guerrae" [Touroulde, S3 p.87].
- Skilled in war: lance, army order, artillery; the men wondered at it as if she had twenty or thirty years of experience [d'Alençon, S3 p.100].
- Free with alms; loved giving to the poor; said the Mass was for the comfort of the poor [Touroulde, S3 p.87-88].
- Disliked dice; laughed at women who brought rosaries to be touched [Touroulde, S3 p.87].
- Anger at oaths: she rebuked soldiers who swore, especially Coutes himself [S3 p.99-100]. Anger at camp women: chased one at Saint-Denis with drawn sword and broke the sword [Coutes, S3 p.99]. Cagny/Bourgeois: she used a stick on men (Quicherat's note quoting the Bourgeois) [S4 p.4 note].
- Chastity under close watch: always a woman slept with her; in the field she slept in her clothes [Coutes, S3 p.70]. D'Aulon and Coutes both say that seeing her body in the war camp did not rouse desire [S3 p.100; d'Aulon S3 p.219]. An Orléans witness who says he agrees with Dunois (name not read; S3 p.17-18, probably Gaucourt: check) writes: "eratque castissima" and says a woman always slept in her room [S3 p.18].
- Temper and impatience: angry at Dunois's delay on 4 May and at La Hire leading the vanguard at Beaugency [S3 p.68, 71].
- Faith in her voices and fearless on the field, but she hated shed French blood [S3 p.213].
- Eating: little. Two meals a day, often a mouthful of bread [Coutes, S3 p.69].
- Hostile witness: the Bourgeois de Paris calls her "créature qui esloit en forme de femme" [S4 p.464], but also records her as "dame Jehanne" and says she died "quelque mauvaiseté ou bonté qu'elle eust faite" [S4 p.468] (editor's reading of the passage).
- Praised by Christine de Pizan: "Hée ! quel honneur au féminin sexe" [S5 p.14].

## 5. Quotations (originals; our own English; locators; rights `public-domain`)

1. "Estis vos Bastardus Aurelianensis?" — "Are you the Bastard of Orléans?" Dunois, Orléans, April 1429 [S3 p.5].
2. "En nom Dieu, consilium Dei Domini nostri est securius et sapientius quam vestrum." — "In God's name, the counsel of God our Lord is surer and wiser than yours." Dunois [S3 p.5-6].
3. "Ha, sanglant garson, vous ne me disiez pas que le sanc de France feust répandu!" — "Ah, bloody boy, you did not tell me French blood was being spilled!" Coutes [S3 p.68]. OCR reads "djriez" for "disiez"; I corrected it.
4. "Bastart, Bastart, ou nom de Dieu, je te commande que tantost que tu scauras la venue dudit Ffastolf, que tu le me faces sçavoir" — "Bastard, Bastard, in God's name I order you to tell me the moment you learn that Fastolf is coming." D'Aulon [S3 p.212]. He adds that she said she would have his head cut off if he failed.
5. "Haa ! mon estandart ! mon estandart !" — "Ah, my banner! my banner!" D'Aulon [S3 p.216].
6. "Aux fagoz et aux cloies tout le monde, affin de faire le pont !" — "Bring brushwood and hurdles, everyone, to make the bridge!" D'Aulon [S3 p.218].
7. "Avant, gentil duc, à l'assault!" — "Forward, gentle duke, to the assault!" D'Alençon, Jargeau [S3 p.96].
8. "Amys, amys, sus! sus! Nostre Sire a condempné les Angloys." — "Friends, friends, up! up! Our Lord has condemned the English." D'Alençon [S3 p.97].
9. "A! gentil duc, times-tu? Nonne scis quod ego promisi uxori tuae te reducere sanum et incolumem?" — "Ah, gentle duke, are you afraid? Do you not know I promised your wife to bring you home safe?" D'Alençon [S3 p.96] (his Latin version of her French).
10. "Vous soyez le très bien venu." — "You are most welcome." Joan to d'Alençon, Chinon [S3 p.91].
11. "Al beau connestable, vous n'estes pas venu de par moy; sed quia venistis, vos bene veneritis." — "Ah, fair constable, you did not come from me; but since you came, you are welcome." D'Alençon [S3 p.98]. OCR reads "Al" for "A".
12. "Il y a es livres de nostre Seigneur plus que es vostres." — "There is more in our Lord's books than in yours." Joan on the clerics, told to Touroulde [S3 p.86].
13. "Tangatis vosmet, quia ita bona erunt ex tactu vestro sicut ex meo." — "Touch them yourselves; they will be as good from your touch as from mine." Touroulde [S3 p.87]. OCR reads "ila" for "ita"; I corrected it.
14. "Par mon martin, ilz seront bien menez; n'en faictes double." — "By my martin, they will be well led; have no doubt." Cagny [S4 p.5]. The word "martin" is explained by Quicherat (note S4 p.4; he says it may be a stick or "Monsieur saint Martin"; undecided).
15. "Par mon martin, la place eust esté prinse." — "By my martin, the place would have been taken." Cagny, Paris, 8 Sept 1429 [S4 p.27].
16. "Par mon martin, nous suymes assez; je iray voir mes bons amis de Compiengne." — "We are enough; I will go and see my good friends of Compiègne." Cagny [S4 p.33].
17. "Je ay juré et baillé ma foy à autre que à vous et je luy en tendray mon serement." — "I have sworn and given my faith to another than you, and I will keep my oath to him." Cagny, Compiègne [S4 p.34].
18. "Si vous, Messeigneurs de l'Eglise, m'eussiez menée et gardée en vos prisons, par advanture ne me fust-il pas ainsi." — "If you, my lords of the Church, had led me and kept me in your prisons, perhaps it would not have gone so with me." Isambart [S2 p.5].
19. "Hélas! je meurs par vous, car se m'eussiez baillée à garder es prisons de l'Église, je ne fusse pas icy." — "Alas, I die through you, for if you had given me to keep in the Church's prisons, I would not be here." Ladvenu [S2 p.8].
20. "Rendez vous, de par Jhesus, à nous tost." — "Surrender to us quickly, in the name of Jesus." Bourgeois de Paris, hostile account of Paris [S4 p.465].
21. "Hée ! quel honneur au féminin / Sexe !" — "Ah, what honor to the female sex!" Christine de Pizan, 1429 [S5 p.14, stanza 34].
22. "Je, Christine, qui ay plouré / Unze ans en abbaye close" — "I, Christine, who wept eleven years in a closed abbey." Christine [S5 p.4, stanza 1].
23. "Ad Deum, ego vado ad Vallis-Colorem." — "To God! I am going to Vaucouleurs." Joan to her father, per Guillemette [S2 p.416].
24. "Ego vidi eos oculis meis corporalibus, aeque bene sicut ego video vos" — "I saw them with my bodily eyes as well as I see you." Joan on St Michael and the angels, Rouen [S1 p.73].
25. (Paraphrase, no quote marks) Joan told her judges she did not come to France except by God's order [S1 p.73].

## 6. Scenes (at most 10)

1. Domrémy, the sexton and the wool: Joan scolds Perrin for skipping compline and promises him wool [S2 p.412-413].
2. Chinon, the quail hunt: d'Alençon is at Saint-Florent when a servant brings news; next day, "Vous soyez le très bien venu" [S3 p.91].
3. The meadow at Chinon: Joan runs with a lance; d'Alençon gives her a horse [S3 p.92].
4. The Loire bank, the wind: Dunois asks if she is the Bastard; the wind turns and the sails fill [S3 p.5-6].
5. Orléans night: d'Aulon dozing, Joan gets up, wakes him, runs into the street, makes a page get off his horse [S3 p.212]. With Coutes at the window and the banner [S3 p.68-69].
6. The vineyard at Les Tourelles: Joan prays a quarter of an hour, returns, plants her banner by the ditch; the English falter [S3 p.8].
7. Tug-of-war with the banner: d'Aulon, the Basque and Joan [S3 p.216-217].
8. Jargeau: "Avant, gentil duc!"; the stone on her helmet; "Amys, amys, sus!" [S3 p.96-97].
9. Bourges, the bed and the rosaries: Touroulde and Joan share a bed for three weeks [S3 p.85-87].
10. Rouen, the cross and the executioner: the crucifix from the church held before her eyes; the heart that would not burn [S2 p.6-7].

## 7. Miracles (witness reports; none approved by me; status = reported by a witness at the Rehabilitation unless stated)

- April 1429, Loire, Orléans: wind changes at once so that boats can go upstream; reported by Dunois [S3 p.6]; the second Orléans witness (name not read) agrees on the wind [S3 p.17-18]; status: witness testimony in Rehabilitation, not an investigated miracle.
- 27 May [sic, OCR; probably 7 May] 1429, Les Tourelles: arrow wound heals without medicine; she keeps fighting; Dunois [S3 p.8]. Same source: the English "tremble" when she stands at the ditch.
- Jargeau, June 1429: the warning about the "machina" that killed Monseigneur du Lude where d'Alençon had stood; d'Alençon [S3 p.96-97]. Reported by a witness.
- 30 May 1431, Rouen: heart and bowels not consumed by fire, per the executioner; Isambart and Ladvenu [S2 p.7]. Status: reported by two friars at the 1449/1450 preliminary inquiry.
- Joan's virginity: queen of Sicily and ladies examined her at Poitiers [d'Aulon, S3 p.209-210]; Touroulde saw her often in bath and steam-room and believed her a virgin [S3 p.88]. Not a miracle: record only.

## 8. Other

- Dating warnings:
  - Isambart and Ladvenu "juré... le v^e jour de mars, l'an... mil quatre cens quarante-neuf" [S2 p.4]. In a year that begins at Easter this is March 1450 by the modern count (my inference; not stated in the text). Check.
  - Dunois's testimony is printed "m. cccc. l[v.], die xxii. februarii" (the "v" is in brackets; Quicherat's reading) [S3 p.2]; d'Alençon "die iii mensis maii anni... mcccclvi" [S3 p.91]; d'Aulon, 28 May 1456 at Lyon, before the Dominican Jean des Prés [S3 p.207].
  - Dunois's age "li annorum vel circa" (about 51) [S3 p.3]; d'Alençon "l annorum" (about 50) [S3 p.90]; Coutes "xlii" and in 1429 "xiv vel xv" [S3 p.65-66]; Touroulde "lxiv" [S3 p.85]; Hauviette "xlv", Drapier "lx", Guillemette "xl", Waterin "xlv" [S2 p.412-421]. Ages are "or about".
  - Cagny's chronology (March 1429 Chinon; "xxiiie jour de mey" of "1431") has errors; Quicherat shows the year in brackets. Use Cagny for scenes, not for dates [S4 p.3, 35].
  - Dunois's "27 May" vs the standard 7 May: check on the Latin page.
