This list gathers the healings, prophecies, and wonders attributed to Joan of Arc, with their sources and their status. Some come from Joan’s own answers at her trial in 1431, some from the sworn testimony of witnesses at the inquiry of 1450–1456, some from the bull of her canonization, and some from later pious tradition. It makes no claim of scientific proof, and it does not dismiss any account. Only the five cures in the first section were examined and approved by the Church. Every other entry is a report, and its status line says who made it.

Miracles approved by the Church

For Joan’s beatification four cures were proposed, and Pius X approved three of them in a decree of the third Sunday of Advent 1908; the fourth was dispensed. Two more cures were approved for her canonization in a decree of 6 April 1918. The bull of 1920 names the people healed and their illnesses, and calls each cure instant and perfect. It does not give the dates or places of the cures.

Sister Thérèse of Saint Augustine: a chronic stomach ulceration

Sister Thérèse of Saint Augustine was healed instantly and perfectly of a chronic ulceration of the stomach (chronica ulceratione stomachi), according to the bull of 1920. A modern fan site describes her illness as leg ulcers; the bull’s diagnosis is used here.

Status: approved by the Church for the beatification (decree of 1908).

Source: Benedict XV, bull Divina disponente, 16 May 1920, vatican.va.

Sister Julie Gauthier of Saint Norbert: an ulcer of the breast

Sister Julie Gauthier of Saint Norbert was healed instantly and perfectly of a painful fungoid ulcer of the left breast (ulcere fungoso eretistico in sinu sinistro), according to the bull of 1920.

Status: approved by the Church for the beatification (decree of 1908).

Source: Benedict XV, bull Divina disponente, 16 May 1920, vatican.va.

Sister Jeanne Marie Sagnier: tuberculosis of the bone

Sister Jeanne Marie Sagnier was healed instantly and perfectly of a chronic tuberculous osteoperiostitis, a disease of the bone and its lining (osteoperiostite chronica tuberculari), according to the bull of 1920.

Status: approved by the Church for the beatification (decree of 1908).

Source: Benedict XV, bull Divina disponente, 16 May 1920, vatican.va.

Maria Antonia Mirandelle: a perforating disease of the foot

Maria Antonia Mirandelle was healed instantly and perfectly of a perforating disease of the sole of the foot (morbo plantari perforante), according to the bull of 1920.

Status: approved by the Church for the canonization (decree of 6 April 1918).

Source: Benedict XV, bull Divina disponente, 16 May 1920, vatican.va.

Thérèse Belin: tuberculosis and a damaged heart valve

Thérèse Belin was healed instantly and perfectly of tuberculosis of the peritoneum and the lungs, and of an organic lesion of the mitral valve of the heart (tuberculosi peritoneali ac pulmonali necnon laesione organica orificii mitralici), according to the bull of 1920.

Status: approved by the Church for the canonization (decree of 6 April 1918).

Source: Benedict XV, bull Divina disponente, 16 May 1920, vatican.va.

The bull adds that the processes describe many other graces, spiritual and bodily, and wonderful healings, but it does not list them.

During Joan’s life

A defeat announced at Vaucouleurs, February 1429

While she was still at Vaucouleurs, Joan announced that the French had just been defeated near Orléans, in the fight later called the Battle of the Herrings. The news arrived days later and confirmed it. The Catholic Encyclopedia reports the episode.

Status: historical claim of prophecy, reported by the Catholic Encyclopedia (1910).

Source: Herbert Thurston, “St. Joan of Arc,” Catholic Encyclopedia, vol. 8 (1910), New Advent.

Eleven days through enemy country, February 1429

Joan rode from Vaucouleurs to Chinon in eleven days, crossing many rivers and country held by the English and Burgundians. The bull of 1920 says the king heard from Baudricourt that she had come through prodigiose, like a wonder, and that he then agreed to receive her.

Status: stated in the papal bull of 1920; the eleven days are also in Guérin’s Life.

Source: Benedict XV, bull Divina disponente, 16 May 1920, vatican.va. Also Paul Guérin, Les Petits Bollandistes, vol. 15, 30 May, “Jeanne d’Arc, la Pucelle d’Orléans”, p. 394.

The king recognized among his courtiers, Chinon, March 1429

When Joan was brought before Charles at Chinon, the old French Lives say she went straight to him among his courtiers. He told her he was not the Dauphin, and she told him that he was. At her trial she said she knew him “by the counsel of my Voice.” The bull of 1920 says only that she revealed to him certain secrets known to God alone.

Status: traditional narrative, accepted by the Catholic Encyclopedia; Joan’s own words at trial.

Source: Paul Guérin, Les Petits Bollandistes, vol. 15, 30 May, “Jeanne d’Arc, la Pucelle d’Orléans”, p. 395; T. Douglas Murray, trans., Jeanne d’Arc, Maid of Orleans (1902), p. 13, Internet Archive; Herbert Thurston, “St. Joan of Arc,” Catholic Encyclopedia, vol. 8 (1910), New Advent.

The secret sign given to the king, Chinon, 1429

Joan gave Charles a private sign that convinced him. The Catholic Encyclopedia says what it was is unknown, and that the most common belief is that it touched a doubt about his birth. At her trial in 1431, asked what revelations the king had received, Joan first refused (“You will not have them from me this year”) and later described the sign in the form of an allegory of an angel bringing the king a crown.

Status: unresolved; Joan’s own account at trial.

Source: Herbert Thurston, “St. Joan of Arc,” Catholic Encyclopedia, vol. 8 (1910), New Advent. Also T. Douglas Murray, trans., Jeanne d’Arc, Maid of Orleans (1902), pp. 59–72, Internet Archive; Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc, vol. 1, p. 75, Internet Archive.

The sword with five crosses, Fierbois, April 1429

Joan’s voices told her that a sword lay in the ground at the church of Saint Catherine at Fierbois. She wrote to the churchmen there and sent an armorer of Tours to look for it. It was found in the ground, rusty, with five crosses on it, and the priests sent it to her. She told her judges she had never seen the man who went to look for it, and that the sword lay behind the altar, she believed. Guérin adds that she told the king of it only after much urging.

Status: stated by Joan at her trial and in the papal bull of 1920.

Source: Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc, vol. 1, pp. 75–76, Internet Archive; Benedict XV, bull Divina disponente, 16 May 1920, vatican.va. Also Paul Guérin, Les Petits Bollandistes, vol. 15, 30 May, “Jeanne d’Arc, la Pucelle d’Orléans”, p. 396.

Predictions recorded in a letter from Lyon, 22 April 1429

A letter written at Lyon by the Sire de Rotslaer on 22 April 1429 records what Joan was then saying she would do: raise the siege of Orléans, be wounded by a shaft without dying of it, and see the king crowned at Reims that summer. The Catholic Encyclopedia says the letter was registered at Brussels before the events, and cites the original manuscript.

Status: documented prophecy, reported by the Catholic Encyclopedia.

Source: Herbert Thurston, “St. Joan of Arc,” Catholic Encyclopedia, vol. 8 (1910), New Advent.

Her wound at Orléans foretold

Guérin’s Life says Joan told Charles before Orléans that she would be wounded there and not hindered, and that on the morning of 7 May she announced she would be wounded that day.

Status: told in a nineteenth-century Life; compare the Lyon letter above.

Source: Paul Guérin, Les Petits Bollandistes, vol. 15, 30 May, “Jeanne d’Arc, la Pucelle d’Orléans”, pp. 396, 398.

The wind on the Loire, April 1429

When Joan met Dunois on the bank of the Loire, the wind was against the boats carrying food for Orléans. Dunois testified under oath in 1456 that the wind changed at once, “as if in a moment,” as she spoke, so that the sails were raised and the boats passed the English bastille of Saint-Loup. A second Orléans witness agreed about the wind. Dunois said that this, among other things, made him judge her deeds to be from God rather than from man. He named the food that reached the city against the wishes of the English, and a vision that Joan said she had, in which Saint Louis and Charlemagne prayed to God for the king and the city.

Status: sworn testimony at the inquiry of 1456; not investigated as a miracle.

Source: Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc, vol. 3, pp. 5–8, 17–18, Internet Archive.

The arrow wound at Les Tourelles, 7 May 1429

During the assault on the earthwork at the bridge of Orléans, an arrow struck Joan between the neck and the shoulder and went in half a foot deep. Dunois testified that she did not stop fighting and took no medicine. Guérin’s Life says she pulled the arrow out herself and was comforted by a vision of two saints. The Catholic Encyclopedia says she was wounded in the breast. Dunois’s Latin deposition, as printed, dates the wound to the twenty-seventh of May, which cannot be right, since the siege ended on 8 May; Murray’s translation corrects it to 7 May in a note, as do the Catholic Encyclopedia and Guérin.

Status: historical fact with a reported vision; witness testimony and pious tradition.

Source: Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc, vol. 3, p. 8, Internet Archive; Paul Guérin, Les Petits Bollandistes, vol. 15, 30 May, “Jeanne d’Arc, la Pucelle d’Orléans”, p. 398; Herbert Thurston, “St. Joan of Arc,” Catholic Encyclopedia, vol. 8 (1910), New Advent.

The English withdraw without a fight, 8 May 1429

On the morning after the earthwork fell, the English came out of their camps in order. Dunois testified that Joan rose from her bed, armed in a coat of mail only, and forbade any attack: let them go. They marched away and the siege ended. Guérin presents their retreat as made at her word.

Status: historical event; the reading of it as a wonder is Guérin’s.

Source: Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc, vol. 3, p. 9, Internet Archive; Paul Guérin, Les Petits Bollandistes, vol. 15, 30 May, “Jeanne d’Arc, la Pucelle d’Orléans”, p. 398.

A warning at Jargeau, June 1429

During the assault on Jargeau, Joan told the duke of Alençon to move from the place where he stood, or a machine would kill him. Soon afterward a lord named du Lude was killed on that spot. D’Alençon told the story under oath in 1456.

Status: sworn testimony at the inquiry of 1456.

Source: Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc, vol. 3, pp. 96–97, Internet Archive.

A stone on her helmet at Jargeau, June 1429

As Joan climbed a ladder with her banner at Jargeau, a stone struck her helmet and threw her to the ground. She rose at once and cried, “Friends, friends, up! up! Our Lord has condemned the English.” The town was taken.

Status: sworn testimony of the duke of Alençon; told also by Guérin.

Source: Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc, vol. 3, p. 97, Internet Archive; Paul Guérin, Les Petits Bollandistes, vol. 15, 30 May, “Jeanne d’Arc, la Pucelle d’Orléans”, p. 400.

Verses of a Maid from the Bois-Chenu, 1429

Dunois testified that when the English commander Suffolk was a prisoner after Jargeau, a paper reached him with four lines of verse saying that a Maid would come from the Bois-Chenu and ride on the backs of the archers. A prophecy of a maiden from the Bois-Chenu was known in the countryside.

Status: sworn testimony of Dunois; popular prophecy.

Source: Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc, vol. 3, p. 15, Internet Archive; Herbert Thurston, “St. Joan of Arc,” Catholic Encyclopedia, vol. 8 (1910), New Advent.

A restive horse led to the cross, Selles, June 1429

A story repeated in a letter from the brothers Guy and André de Laval, dated at Selles in June 1429, tells that Joan’s horse, a fine black war-horse, was so restive at the door that he would not stand still. “Take him to the Cross,” she said, and there he stood “as though he were tied” while she mounted. Murray’s note adds that local tradition says the old iron town cross could be seen from her lodging.

Status: popular account, cited from a contemporary letter in a note by Murray.

Source: T. Douglas Murray, trans., Jeanne d’Arc, Maid of Orleans (1902), p. 30, note 1, Internet Archive.

“A year and not much more,” 1429

The duke of Alençon testified that Joan told the king she would last “a year and not much more,” and named four tasks for that year: drive out the English, crown the king at Reims, free the duke of Orléans, and raise the siege of Orléans. She was captured at Compiègne in May 1430, a little over a year after Orléans. Guérin ties the prophecy to her capture.

Status: prophecy in sworn testimony; fulfilled, as her supporters saw it, by her capture.

Source: Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc, vol. 3, p. 99, Internet Archive; Paul Guérin, Les Petits Bollandistes, vol. 15, 30 May, “Jeanne d’Arc, la Pucelle d’Orléans”, pp. 399, 402.

A dead newborn at Lagny, 1430

Joan told her judges that at Lagny a baby three days old, who had died without baptism, was brought before the statue of the Virgin. The child was as black as her coat. Joan went and prayed with the girls of the town, and life returned to the child, who yawned three times while the color began to return. He was baptized, then died and was buried in consecrated ground. Asked whether the village said it was done through her prayer, she answered, “I did not enquire about it.” The bull of 1920 says she raised a dead infant to life so that he could be baptized.

Status: told by Joan at her trial; described in the papal bull as a raising from the dead.

Source: Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc, vol. 1, p. 105, Internet Archive; T. Douglas Murray, trans., Jeanne d’Arc, Maid of Orleans (1902), p. 52, Internet Archive; Benedict XV, bull Divina disponente, 16 May 1920, vatican.va.

Her capture foretold at Melun, April 1430

On the ramparts of Melun, Joan’s voices told her that she would be taken prisoner before the feast of Saint John. She was captured at Compiègne on 23 May 1430 (some sources say 24 May).

Status: Joan’s own account; repeated in the bull, Guérin, and the Catholic Encyclopedia.

Source: Benedict XV, bull Divina disponente, 16 May 1920, vatican.va. Also Paul Guérin, Les Petits Bollandistes, vol. 15, 30 May, “Jeanne d’Arc, la Pucelle d’Orléans”, p. 402; Herbert Thurston, “St. Joan of Arc,” Catholic Encyclopedia, vol. 8 (1910), New Advent.

Compiègne freed before Martinmas, 1430

After her capture a steward of her captors told Joan that Compiègne would fall, and she said it would not. The bull of 1920 says her voices had told her the town would be freed before the feast of Saint Martin, and that it was.

Status: prophecy stated in the papal bull; Joan’s reply reported by the chronicler Perceval de Cagny.

Source: Benedict XV, bull Divina disponente, 16 May 1920, vatican.va. Also Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc, vol. 4, p. 35, Internet Archive.

Comfort in her prison, Rouen, 1431

Joan told her judges on 24 February 1431 that on the previous morning her voice had woken her in her prison and told her, “Answer boldly; God will help thee.” Guérin’s Life says her voices and her saints visited and comforted her throughout her imprisonment.

Status: Joan’s own words at trial; pious tradition in Guérin.

Source: T. Douglas Murray, trans., Jeanne d’Arc, Maid of Orleans (1902), pp. 16–17, Internet Archive; Paul Guérin, Les Petits Bollandistes, vol. 15, 30 May, “Jeanne d’Arc, la Pucelle d’Orléans”, p. 404.

A greater prize than Orléans, 1 March 1431

At her trial on 1 March 1431, Joan said that within seven years the English would lose “a greater wager than they have already done at Orleans.” The Catholic Encyclopedia counts the loss of Paris as the fulfilment and dates it 12 November 1437; Murray’s note dates the loss of Paris to 1436. Either date falls within the seven years.

Status: prophecy recorded at the trial; reported as fulfilled by the Catholic Encyclopedia.

Source: T. Douglas Murray, trans., Jeanne d’Arc, Maid of Orleans (1902), hearing of 1 March 1431, Internet Archive; Herbert Thurston, “St. Joan of Arc,” Catholic Encyclopedia, vol. 8 (1910), New Advent.

The voices after her abjuration, May 1431

After she signed the abjuration at Saint-Ouen on 24 May 1431, Joan said her voices rebuked her for giving in and told her she had damned herself to save her life. The bull of 1920 says that in her last hour she understood what her voices had foretold: that she should take everything willingly and not be anxious or troubled about her martyrdom, for she would enter the kingdom of Paradise.

Status: Joan’s own words at trial; repeated in the papal bull.

Source: T. Douglas Murray, trans., Jeanne d’Arc, Maid of Orleans (1902), p. 137, Internet Archive; Benedict XV, bull Divina disponente, 16 May 1920, vatican.va.

Birds that ate from her lap

The hostile Journal of a citizen of Paris repeats a story that birds came to eat from Joan’s lap, but gives it only as hearsay, with the Latin note in veritate apocryphum est, “in truth it is apocryphal.”

Status: legend; doubted by the source that records it.

Source: Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc, vol. 4, p. 463, Internet Archive.

After Joan’s death

The first of these accounts come from the day she died, 30 May 1431. The last is a legend of the years that followed.

The name of Jesus, Rouen, 30 May 1431

Witnesses at the inquiry said Joan called on Jesus again and again in the flames, more than six times by one account, and said the name once more as she bowed her head at the last. The bull of 1920 and Guérin add that many saw the name of Jesus written in the flames.

Status: her cries are in sworn testimony; the name seen in the flames is reported in the bull and Guérin.

Source: Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc, vol. 2, pp. 6–7, Internet Archive; T. Douglas Murray, trans., Jeanne d’Arc, Maid of Orleans (1902), p. 301, Internet Archive; Benedict XV, bull Divina disponente, 16 May 1920, vatican.va. Also Paul Guérin, Les Petits Bollandistes, vol. 15, 30 May, “Jeanne d’Arc, la Pucelle d’Orléans”, p. 412.

A white dove from the fire, Rouen, 30 May 1431

Isambart de la Pierre reported that an English soldier saw Joan’s spirit leave her, “as it were a white dove, going away from France.” By Isambart’s account this was the same soldier who had sworn to bring wood for her fire (next entry). The bull and Guérin say he fainted and thought he saw a white dove. The soldier is not named.

Status: reported by a witness at the inquiry; popular.

Source: T. Douglas Murray, trans., Jeanne d’Arc, Maid of Orleans (1902), p. 191, Internet Archive; Benedict XV, bull Divina disponente, 16 May 1920, vatican.va. Also Paul Guérin, Les Petits Bollandistes, vol. 15, 30 May, “Jeanne d’Arc, la Pucelle d’Orléans”, p. 412.

The soldier with the bundle of wood, Rouen, 30 May 1431

An English soldier who hated Joan greatly had sworn to bring a bundle of wood for her fire. Isambart de la Pierre said the man was stunned when he heard her cry the name of Jesus, and that in the afternoon he confessed to a Dominican friar that he had gravely erred. In the same account he is the soldier who saw the white dove.

Status: reported by a witness at the inquiry, years after the event.

Source: T. Douglas Murray, trans., Jeanne d’Arc, Maid of Orleans (1902), pp. 162, 191, Internet Archive.

The heart that would not burn, Rouen, 30 May 1431

After the execution the executioner came in terror to the friars Isambart de la Pierre and Ladvenu. He told them that despite oil, sulphur, and charcoal he could not burn Joan’s heart and entrails to ashes, and Isambart said he was much astonished at it, “as a most evident miracle.” Isambart also reported that the executioner said he feared he was damned because he had burnt a saint. Guérin adds that the heart was found whole and full of blood. On the orders of the English, the ashes and the heart were thrown into the Seine.

Status: reported by two friars at the early inquiry (1449–1450); stated in the papal bull.

Source: Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc, vol. 2, pp. 7, 9, Internet Archive; T. Douglas Murray, trans., Jeanne d’Arc, Maid of Orleans (1902), pp. 161–162, 191, Internet Archive; Benedict XV, bull Divina disponente, 16 May 1920, vatican.va. Also Paul Guérin, Les Petits Bollandistes, vol. 15, 30 May, “Jeanne d’Arc, la Pucelle d’Orléans”, p. 412.

The fate of her judges

Guérin’s Life lists the deaths of those who condemned Joan as a kind of judgment: Cauchon dying suddenly in a barber’s hands, Le Maistre vanishing, Estivet found dead in a ditch, L’Oiseleur dying suddenly at Basel, and Midi struck with leprosy, together with the fates of Bedford and Henry VI. The bull of 1920 says only that the guilty died shamefully.

Status: popular tradition in a pious source; treat as legend.

Source: Paul Guérin, Les Petits Bollandistes, vol. 15, 30 May, “Jeanne d’Arc, la Pucelle d’Orléans”, pp. 413–414; Benedict XV, bull Divina disponente, 16 May 1920, vatican.va.
