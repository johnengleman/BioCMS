Thomas Aquinas wrote theology for teaching: the Summa contra Gentiles, the Summa Theologiae (still unfinished when he stopped writing in December 1273), commentaries on Scripture and on Aristotle, and disputed questions. Some of his sermons survive only as listeners took them down. The themes below give his own words, with the work and place, and a fact from his life that shows how he lived what he taught. Translations are our own unless a public-domain translator is named.

Faith and reason

Thomas held that our salvation needed a teaching revealed by God, besides the philosophy that human reason works out. Theology uses philosophy and the other sciences as handmaids, he wrote, because of the weakness of our minds and through no lack of its own. He put the principle in one sentence near the start of the Summa Theologiae.

“Since grace does not destroy nature but perfects it, natural reason must serve faith.”

— Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I, q. 1, a. 8, ad 2 (our translation)

This is the source of the familiar phrase “grace perfects nature.” He wrote it in the opening question of the Summa, which asks what theology is. He lived it by setting Aristotle and the whole of reason to work for the faith, and on his deathbed he left everything he had said to the correction of the Church.

What we can know of God

Thomas believed that the mind can reason from the world to God, from motion and from the order of nature. His “five ways” begin with what anyone can see, that “some things in this world are moved,” and the fifth ends, “and this we call God.” Yet he insisted that about God we cannot know what he is, only what he is not, and that even a little knowledge of God is worth more than great certainty about small things.

“The least that can be known of the highest things is more to be desired than the most certain knowledge of the smallest things.”

— Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I, q. 1, a. 5, ad 1 (our translation)

In the same reply he compares the human mind before the things of God to an owl’s eye dazzled by the sun. He lived this teaching most plainly in December 1273, when, by one sworn account, he stopped writing and said that all he had written seemed like straw beside what he had seen.

Love, mercy, and friendship with God

To love, Thomas wrote, is to will good to someone. God loves everything that exists, and his love pours out and creates the goodness in things. Mercy belongs to God most of all in what it does; among the virtues that concern our neighbor, mercy is the greatest. Charity, the love that unites us to God, is a kind of friendship.

“Charity is a kind of friendship of man with God.”

— Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II, q. 23, a. 1 (our translation)

He added that it would be ridiculous to speak of friendship with wine or with a horse. In his own life the friend we see most clearly is his companion Reginald of Piperno, who heard his secrets and kept them.

Law, justice, and the common good

Thomas defined law as reason’s ordering of a community toward its common good. The first precept of the natural law, which he called the light of understanding placed in us by God, is that good is to be done and pursued, and evil avoided. Justice is the constant and lasting will to give each person what is due. He also wrote that Jews and pagans must in no way be forced to believe, because belief is an act of the will; in the same article he allowed that heretics and apostates might be compelled.

“Law is an ordinance of reason for the common good, made by the one who has care of the community, and promulgated.”

— Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I-II, q. 90, a. 4 (our translation)

The definition comes from his treatise on law in the second part of the Summa. Kings sought his counsel: Louis IX of France asked his advice in hard cases, and Charles of Anjou paid him to teach at Naples.

Handing on what is contemplated

Thomas belonged to the Order of Preachers, and he wrote that it is greater to hand on to others what one has contemplated than only to contemplate. Christ himself, he wrote, chose a life of preaching and teaching, handing on to others what he contemplated.

“As it is greater to illuminate than only to shine, so it is greater to hand on to others what one has contemplated than only to contemplate.”

— Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II, q. 188, a. 6 (our translation)

This is the true source of the popular phrase about giving others “the fruits of contemplation.” He lived it in Naples in 1273, where he lectured each day and preached through Lent on the Our Father in his own Neapolitan, to crowds that a witness said were almost the whole city.

Rest and play

Thomas thought rest was a moral matter. The soul’s rest is delight, and a person who never relaxes will break. He retold the story of St. John the Evangelist and the bow that would break if it were always bent, and named a virtue for good humor in play, eutrapelia. Play, he wrote, should be used like sleep and other rest, once serious duties are done.

“The mind of man would break if it were never relaxed from its tension.”

— Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II, q. 168, a. 2 (our translation)

The man who wrote this was known for being so absorbed that a bowl could be set before him and taken away without his noticing; he also left the friars’ garden recreation to be alone.

The Eucharist

By his own deathbed words, Thomas had “taught and written much” on the Eucharist, and, by the witness of Ptolemy of Lucca, he composed the office for the feast of Corpus Christi at the command of Pope Urban IV. Modern scholars judge his authorship of those hymns, and of the prayer Adoro te devote, probable but not certain. Their theme is that the senses fail before the sacrament, and faith alone suffices.

“Sight, touch, and taste are deceived in you, but by hearing alone it is safe to believe.”

— Adoro te devote, ascribed to Thomas Aquinas (our translation)

At Fossanova, receiving Communion for the last time, he professed that the Host was true God and true man, and said that he had studied, kept watch, and worked for love of him, leaving all he had said about the sacrament to the correction of the Roman Church.

The love of wisdom

For Thomas, study was a joy. It belongs to the wise man to set things in order, he wrote, and truth must be the last end of the whole universe. A small error at the start becomes a great one at the end, so the beginning must be right.

“Of all human pursuits, the pursuit of wisdom is the more perfect, the more sublime, the more useful, and the more agreeable.”

— Thomas Aquinas, Summa contra Gentiles I.2, tr. Joseph Rickaby, 1905

He wrote this at the opening of the Summa contra Gentiles. He showed what he meant on a walk near Paris: told that he could sell the whole city to the king, he answered that he would rather have Chrysostom on Matthew.
