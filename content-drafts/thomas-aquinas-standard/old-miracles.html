This list gathers the healings, visions, and other wonders attributed to St. Thomas Aquinas, with their sources and their status. The first group is the ten miracles set out in the bull of canonization, Redemptionem misit, issued by Pope John XXII on 18 July 1323 after the inquiries at Naples (1319) and Fossanova (1321). The other accounts come from sworn witnesses at Naples in 1319, from the early lives by Peter Calo, William of Tocco, and Bernard Gui (written about 1300 to after 1323), from Ptolemy of Lucca, who knew Thomas, and from later writers. Each entry says which, and whether the account is approved, sworn, hearsay, legend, or contested. The list makes no claim of scientific proof, and it does not dismiss any account. Tocco’s catalogue numbers 57 accounts and Gui’s more than a hundred; those not told here were not read in detail for this page.

Miracles approved by the Church: the ten in the bull of 1323

The fragrant, unspoiled body at Fossanova, 1274

Thomas died at the Cistercian abbey of Fossanova on 7 March 1274 and was buried before the high altar. Fearing that the Dominicans or the pope would take the body, the monks moved it secretly by night to the chapel of St. Stephen. About seven months later Thomas appeared in a dream to the head of the house and said, “Bring me back to my former place.” When the grave was opened, a fragrance “as of many spices” filled the chapel and the cloister, and the body lay whole in its habit, though Thomas had been a large, heavy man and the ground was wet; only the tip of the nose showed a small crack.

In the fourteenth year after his death the tomb was opened again at the request of his sister, the Countess of San Severino. Abbot Peter of Monte San Giovanni found the fragrance again and the body whole except the tip of the nose, and cut off the right hand for her. The bull says that some smelled the fragrance after seven years and others after almost fourteen. Abbot Nicholas and Peter of Monte San Giovanni swore to these events in 1319.

Status: approved by the Church: the first miracle in the bull of canonization (1323). The body is not claimed to be incorrupt today; the bones are at Toulouse.

Source: John XXII, bull Redemptionem misit, 18 July 1323, §9, English translation, FSSPX News, https://fsspx.news/en/news/seventh-centenary-canonization-st-thomas-aquinas-4-28938. Also Tocco, ch. 66–68, and the Naples process, pp. 278, 336–337, in the Fontes editions above.

A surgeon cured of ten years’ gout

A surgeon who had suffered from gout for ten years was cured at Thomas’s tomb. Tocco names him Raynaldus, a doctor of San Lorenzo, and says that after his cure he rode a horse.

Status: approved by the Church in the bull of canonization (1323).

Source: John XXII, bull Redemptionem misit, 18 July 1323, §10, English translation, FSSPX News, https://fsspx.news/en/news/seventh-centenary-canonization-st-thomas-aquinas-4-28938. Tocco’s account: William of Tocco, Ystoria sancti Thome de Aquino, in Prümmer, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 1, p. 145, https://archive.org/details/prummerfontesvitaestho.

A man struck down after a terrifying dream

A man was paralyzed after a frightening vision in his sleep and lay like a corpse. He was carried to the tomb at Fossanova and was cured.

Status: approved by the Church in the bull of canonization (1323).

Source: John XXII, bull Redemptionem misit, 18 July 1323, §11, English translation, FSSPX News, https://fsspx.news/en/news/seventh-centenary-canonization-st-thomas-aquinas-4-28938.

The cleric who scorned the hand, Salerno, c. 1316

Some forty-two years after Thomas’s death, Tocco writes, a cleric of Salerno mocked the relic of Thomas’s right hand. He was seized with trembling, and the bull adds that a cyst appeared on his head. When he kissed the hand he was cured, and the fragrance of the relic clung to his hood.

Status: approved by the Church in the bull of canonization (1323), which tells it without names.

Source: John XXII, bull Redemptionem misit, 18 July 1323, §12, English translation, FSSPX News, https://fsspx.news/en/news/seventh-centenary-canonization-st-thomas-aquinas-4-28938. Tocco, ch. 69, pp. 142–143.

A speechless cleric with angina

A cleric who had lost his voice from angina was carried to the tomb. His wife could not go with him into the church, because women were not allowed inside. The bull counts his recovery among the ten miracles.

Status: approved by the Church in the bull of canonization (1323).

Source: John XXII, bull Redemptionem misit, 18 July 1323, §13, English translation, FSSPX News, https://fsspx.news/en/news/seventh-centenary-canonization-st-thomas-aquinas-4-28938.

Seven weeks of fever, ended in one night

A person who had suffered a fever every day for seven weeks made a vow to Thomas and was cured in a single night.

Status: approved by the Church in the bull of canonization (1323).

Source: John XXII, bull Redemptionem misit, 18 July 1323, §14, English translation, FSSPX News, https://fsspx.news/en/news/seventh-centenary-canonization-st-thomas-aquinas-4-28938.

A deaf mother of a two-month-old child

A woman with a baby two months old had lost her hearing. She was cured overnight.

Status: approved by the Church in the bull of canonization (1323).

Source: John XXII, bull Redemptionem misit, 18 July 1323, §15, English translation, FSSPX News, https://fsspx.news/en/news/seventh-centenary-canonization-st-thomas-aquinas-4-28938.

A girl’s swollen throat and the relics

A girl suffered from a swelling of the throat. Relics of Thomas, wrapped in a cloth, were applied to her, and she was cured and ate bread.

Status: approved by the Church in the bull of canonization (1323).

Source: John XXII, bull Redemptionem misit, 18 July 1323, §16, English translation, FSSPX News, https://fsspx.news/en/news/seventh-centenary-canonization-st-thomas-aquinas-4-28938.

A lay brother healed asleep on the tomb

A lay brother of Fossanova had carried his arm in a sling for three months. He fell asleep on Thomas’s tomb and woke cured.

Status: approved by the Church in the bull of canonization (1323).

Source: John XXII, bull Redemptionem misit, 18 July 1323, §17, English translation, FSSPX News, https://fsspx.news/en/news/seventh-centenary-canonization-st-thomas-aquinas-4-28938.

A four-year-old with a tumor

A child of four had a tumor on the back and legs. The child was laid on the tomb and was cured without surgery.

Status: approved by the Church in the bull of canonization (1323).

Source: John XXII, bull Redemptionem misit, 18 July 1323, §18, English translation, FSSPX News, https://fsspx.news/en/news/seventh-centenary-canonization-st-thomas-aquinas-4-28938.

During Thomas’s life

Lightning at Roccasecca, c. 1225

When Thomas was an infant, lightning struck the tower of the family castle at Roccasecca. His little sister, asleep in the tower, was killed, and so were the horses in the stable. His mother Theodora ran to the bed where the nurse slept with the baby and found him unharmed. Tocco says his account of the early years came from Theodora herself.

Status: legend, told by Tocco about ninety years later.

Source: William of Tocco, Ystoria sancti Thome de Aquino, in Prümmer, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 1, p. 67, https://archive.org/details/prummerfontesvitaestho.

The hermit’s greeting to Theodora

Before Thomas was born, a hermit called Brother Bonus greeted his mother: “Rejoice, lady, for you are with child and will bear a son whom you will call Thomas.” The hermit said the parents would mean him for a monk and abbot, but he would be a Friar Preacher without equal. In February 1318 Thomas’s niece, Lady Catherine of Morra, an old woman by then, swore before Tocco, a judge, and a notary that she had heard this from Theodora.

Status: legend in Tocco and Calo; sworn hearsay in 1318–1319.

Source: Naples canonization process, 1319, in Laurent, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 4, n. 62, p. 350, https://archive.org/details/fontesvitaesthom00pr. Also Tocco, pp. 66–67, and Calo, p. 18, in Prümmer.

The Hail Mary in the baby’s fist

As an infant Thomas is said to have clutched a paper with the Hail Mary written on it, and to have cried until he was given a scrap of writing. Tocco sets the story at a public bath in Naples; Gui sets it at home.

Status: legend; the two early lives disagree on the place.

Source: William of Tocco, Ystoria sancti Thome de Aquino, in Prümmer, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 1, pp. 59–60, 67–68, https://archive.org/details/prummerfontesvitaestho.

A ray of light from the boy’s face, Naples

While Thomas was a student at Naples, Tocco writes, a Dominican saw a ray of light shining from the boy’s face three times.

Status: legend, in Tocco only.

Source: William of Tocco, Ystoria sancti Thome de Aquino, in Prümmer, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 1, p. 70, https://archive.org/details/prummerfontesvitaestho.

The belt of chastity, in captivity

While his family held him captive, a girl dressed as a courtesan was shut in his room. Thomas drove her out with a burning brand, burned a cross into the wall with it, prayed, and slept. In his sleep two angels girded him with a cord, saying, “Behold, from God we gird you with the belt of chastity that no temptation can undo,” and he cried out in pain. Tocco says Thomas told no one until near death, and then only Reginald of Piperno. Thomas of Cantimpré, writing in Thomas’s lifetime, says only that women were shut in with him and that he stood firmer than before.

Status: reported: Tocco’s life, and sworn hearsay in 1319 (Tocco, from Robert of Sezze, from his uncle Stephen). The angels are not in the earliest account.

Source: Naples canonization process, 1319, in Laurent, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 4, n. 61, p. 349, https://archive.org/details/fontesvitaesthom00pr. Also Tocco, pp. 75–76, and Cantimpré, Bonum universale de apibus I.20, https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_UM5bV2aYqGAC.

The verse about the mountains, Paris, c. 1256

Ordered to become a master of theology, Thomas pleaded that he was too young, prayed, and fell asleep. An old friar of his Order appeared and gave him the verse “Watering the mountains from his heights: the earth shall be filled with the fruit of your works” (Psalm 103:13 in the Vulgate). Gerard de Frachet, before 1260, tells it as a dream in which someone handed Thomas a book with the verse. Thomas told the story himself to the prior of Fossanova at Maenza in 1274.

Status: reported by Thomas himself, as a sworn witness heard it in 1274 and repeated it in 1319.

Source: Naples canonization process, 1319, in Laurent, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 4, n. 49, p. 331, https://archive.org/details/fontesvitaesthom00pr. Also Tocco, pp. 84–85.

A tooth lost in prayer, Paris

William of Tocco swore in 1319 that he had often heard, from Thomas’s nephew Count Thomas of San Severino and from Ptolemy of Lucca, that at Paris an extra tooth had grown in overnight and hindered Thomas’s speech on the eve of a disputation. As he prayed, the tooth fell into his hand, and he carried it with him for a long time.

Status: sworn hearsay, 1319 (Tocco’s deposition); also told by Calo.

Source: Naples canonization process, 1319, in Laurent, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 4, p. 347, https://archive.org/details/fontesvitaesthom00pr.

Raised from the floor in prayer, Salerno and Naples

At Salerno, Tocco writes, a Brother James and his companion saw Thomas raised two cubits from the floor in prayer after Matins. At Naples the sacristan of the friary saw him raised two cubits in the chapel of St. Nicholas. Tocco and Gui name the sacristan Dominic of Caserta; Calo names him James.

Status: pious tradition in the early lives; no sworn witness of 1319 tells it.

Source: William of Tocco, Ystoria sancti Thome de Aquino, in Prümmer, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 1, ch. 33–34, pp. 107–108, https://archive.org/details/prummerfontesvitaestho. Also Calo, p. 38, and Gui, p. 189.

The voice from the crucifix, Naples

In the same scene the sacristan heard the crucifix speak: “Thomas, you have written well of me. What reward will you take from me for your labor?” Thomas answered, “Lord, nothing but yourself.” Gui’s wording of the answer differs slightly (“I will take no other reward but yourself”). Tocco says Thomas was then writing the third part of the Summa and wrote little afterward.

Status: legend: first written about 1317–1323; not in the sworn testimony of 1319.

Source: William of Tocco, Ystoria sancti Thome de Aquino, in Prümmer, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 1, p. 108, https://archive.org/details/prummerfontesvitaestho. Also Calo, p. 38, and Gui, p. 189.

Christ over the notebook, Paris

Tocco tells a separate scene at Paris in which Christ stood over Thomas’s notebook on the Eucharist and said, “You have written well of this sacrament of my Body,” and Thomas rose a cubit from the floor. Later writers often merged this scene with the crucifix at Naples.

Status: legend, in Tocco.

Source: William of Tocco, Ystoria sancti Thome de Aquino, in Prümmer, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 1, p. 126, https://archive.org/details/prummerfontesvitaestho.

Peter and Paul and the passage of Isaiah

After fasting and praying over a hard passage of Isaiah, Thomas was heard by Reginald of Piperno talking in the dark at night. He then had Reginald light a candle and dictated as if reading from a book. Pressed by Reginald, he said that the apostles Peter and Paul had come to him, and he ordered him “on God’s behalf” to tell no one during his life.

Status: sworn hearsay in 1319, from Reginald; also in Tocco.

Source: Naples canonization process, 1319, in Laurent, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 4, n. 59, pp. 346–347, https://archive.org/details/fontesvitaesthom00pr. Also Tocco, pp. 105–106.

The demon in the friary, Naples

John Blasi swore in 1319 that he saw Thomas at Naples raise his fist and rush at a demon, crying, “Why have you come here to tempt me?”

Status: reported by a sworn eyewitness, 1319.

Source: Naples canonization process, 1319, in Laurent, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 4, n. 70, p. 362, https://archive.org/details/fontesvitaesthom00pr.

The relics of St. Agnes at Molara

Ptolemy of Lucca travelled with Thomas from Rome and stopped at Molara, a castle of Cardinal Richard. Thomas had a tertian fever and his companion a continuous one. Thomas sent his companion the relics of St. Agnes that he wore, brought from Rome, and the sick man recovered.

Status: reported by an eyewitness, Ptolemy of Lucca; Tocco repeated it at second hand in 1319.

Source: Ptolemy of Lucca, Historia ecclesiastica nova XXIII.10, as quoted in Prümmer, fasc. 1, p. 45 n. 5, https://archive.org/details/prummerfontesvitaestho; Muratori, RIS XI, https://archive.org/details/RerumItalicarumScriptores11.

The woman who touched his cloak, Rome

While Thomas preached at St. Peter’s in Rome during Holy Week, a woman suffering from a flow of blood touched his cloak. She afterward told his companion that she had been healed.

Status: reported in Tocco, from the woman’s own account to Thomas’s companion.

Source: William of Tocco, Ystoria sancti Thome de Aquino, in Prümmer, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 1, pp. 126–127, https://archive.org/details/prummerfontesvitaestho.

A star in a sickroom, Naples, 1274

John Coppa, a notary of Naples, swore in 1319 that in 1274, the year Thomas died, he went with his brother Bonfilius, a Dominican, to visit Thomas lying ill in his cell at Naples. A star of great brightness came in through the window and stood over the bed for about as long as it takes to say a Hail Mary, white and almost silver, and then vanished. Coppa said his brother, who was dead by 1319, had told him he saw it too.

Status: reported by a sworn eyewitness, 1319; his late brother is named as a second witness.

Source: Naples canonization process, 1319, in Laurent, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 4, pp. 391–392, https://archive.org/details/fontesvitaesthom00pr.

Fresh herrings at Maenza, 1274

On his last journey Thomas fell ill at Maenza, the castle of his niece Francesca. He lost his appetite and asked for fresh herring, which he had eaten in France. The doctor, John of Piperno, found a basket of fresh herring among the sardines on a mule-load from Terracina. Peter of Monte San Giovanni, an eyewitness, swore that Thomas ate them; Tocco says he refused, as David refused water, and Abbot Nicholas, repeating what he had heard, also said that he would not eat.

Status: reported by sworn witnesses; contested in its details.

Source: Naples canonization process, 1319, in Laurent, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 4, pp. 279, 333–334, https://archive.org/details/fontesvitaesthom00pr. Also Tocco, ch. 56, pp. 129–130.

At his death and burial, 1274

Stars over Fossanova

Calo, Tocco, and Gui write that a monk of Fossanova saw in a dream, at the hour of Thomas’s death, a star fall on the monastery and two other stars join it. They add that a star like a comet had shone over the monastery for three days and vanished at his death.

Status: vision and legend, in the early lives; no sworn witness of 1319 tells it.

Source: William of Tocco, Ystoria sancti Thome de Aquino, in Prümmer, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 1, p. 133; also Calo, p. 48, and Gui, https://archive.org/details/prummerfontesvitaestho.

St. Paul leads him out of the schools, Naples

At Naples, Brother Paul of Aquila dreamed that St. Paul came into the schools and led Thomas out. The hour was later found to match the hour of Thomas’s death.

Status: vision reported in Calo, Tocco, and Gui.

Source: William of Tocco, Ystoria sancti Thome de Aquino, in Prümmer, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 1, pp. 133–134, https://archive.org/details/prummerfontesvitaestho.

Rainerius of Pisa’s vision at Anagni

On the third day after Thomas’s death, Tocco writes, Rainerius of Pisa at Anagni saw Thomas in a vision saying Mass.

Status: vision reported in Tocco.

Source: William of Tocco, Ystoria sancti Thome de Aquino, in Prümmer, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 1, pp. 137–138, https://archive.org/details/prummerfontesvitaestho.

The blind subprior, Fossanova

John of Ferentino, subprior of Fossanova, had been blind for about twenty days. He put his eyes to the eyes of the dead Thomas and saw again. Peter of Monte San Giovanni swore to it as an eyewitness.

Status: reported by a sworn eyewitness, 1319; not among the ten miracles of the bull.

Source: Naples canonization process, 1319, in Laurent, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 4, n. 51, pp. 335–336, https://archive.org/details/fontesvitaesthom00pr. Also Tocco, ch. 61, pp. 134–135.

The mule at the bier

Thomas’s niece Francesca, who did not dare to enter the monastery, mourned at the gate where the bier had been carried. The mule Thomas had ridden broke its rope, came to the bier, and fell dead “with no other illness.”

Status: legend, in Calo, Tocco, and Gui; no sworn witness of 1319 tells it.

Source: William of Tocco, Ystoria sancti Thome de Aquino, in Prümmer, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 1, ch. 62, p. 135; also Calo and Gui, https://archive.org/details/prummerfontesvitaestho.

Sweet-smelling earth at the tomb

When the body was brought back from St. Stephen’s chapel, Tocco writes, a mason named Jacobus lifted the earth that had fallen by the chest, and it too was fragrant. A nobleman carried some of it away as a relic.

Status: reported in Tocco.

Source: William of Tocco, Ystoria sancti Thome de Aquino, in Prümmer, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 1, p. 140, https://archive.org/details/prummerfontesvitaestho.

After his death

A leper of Piperno

A leper, Thomas of Piperno, was cured after Thomas appeared to him at night and said, “Get up and be healed.” Tocco records it in his catalogue of the miracles gathered for the canonization.

Status: reported in Tocco’s catalogue (nos. 1–57); not among the ten in the bull.

Source: William of Tocco, Ystoria sancti Thome de Aquino, in Prümmer, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 1, catalogue, pp. 145–160, https://archive.org/details/prummerfontesvitaestho.

A man paralyzed by a demonic apparition

A man paralyzed after the apparition of a demon in the shape of an ape was cured, and he became a lay brother. Tocco records it in his catalogue of the miracles gathered for the canonization.

Status: reported in Tocco’s catalogue (nos. 1–57); not among the ten in the bull.

Source: William of Tocco, Ystoria sancti Thome de Aquino, in Prümmer, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 1, catalogue, pp. 145–160, https://archive.org/details/prummerfontesvitaestho.

A man lame from birth

A man lame from birth was able to stand. Tocco records it in his catalogue of the miracles gathered for the canonization.

Status: reported in Tocco’s catalogue (nos. 1–57); not among the ten in the bull.

Source: William of Tocco, Ystoria sancti Thome de Aquino, in Prümmer, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 1, catalogue, pp. 145–160, https://archive.org/details/prummerfontesvitaestho.

Storms stilled at Fossanova

Storms at Fossanova were stilled when the monks went in procession to the tomb. Tocco records it in his catalogue of the miracles gathered for the canonization.

Status: reported in Tocco’s catalogue (nos. 1–57); not among the ten in the bull.

Source: William of Tocco, Ystoria sancti Thome de Aquino, in Prümmer, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 1, catalogue, pp. 145–160, https://archive.org/details/prummerfontesvitaestho.

Lightning and hail turned away

Lightning and hail were stopped when people called on Thomas. Tocco records it in his catalogue of the miracles gathered for the canonization.

Status: reported in Tocco’s catalogue (nos. 1–57); not among the ten in the bull.

Source: William of Tocco, Ystoria sancti Thome de Aquino, in Prümmer, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 1, catalogue, pp. 145–160, https://archive.org/details/prummerfontesvitaestho.

A sinking man saved

A man sinking in water was saved after calling on Thomas. Tocco records it in his catalogue of the miracles gathered for the canonization.

Status: reported in Tocco’s catalogue (nos. 1–57); not among the ten in the bull.

Source: William of Tocco, Ystoria sancti Thome de Aquino, in Prümmer, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 1, catalogue, pp. 145–160, https://archive.org/details/prummerfontesvitaestho.

A knight and his horse saved in a fall

A knight and his horse, falling from a rock, were saved. Tocco records it in his catalogue of the miracles gathered for the canonization.

Status: reported in Tocco’s catalogue (nos. 1–57); not among the ten in the bull.

Source: William of Tocco, Ystoria sancti Thome de Aquino, in Prümmer, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 1, catalogue, pp. 145–160, https://archive.org/details/prummerfontesvitaestho.

The storm off Monte Argentario, c. 1318

Tocco and Robert of Benevento, carrying the records of the miracles to the pope, were caught in a storm that drove their galley toward the rocks of Monte Argentario. They called on Mary, St. Dominic, and St. Peter Martyr, and last of all on Thomas, to save the records, and a new wind turned the ship away. Tocco tells it of himself.

Status: reported by Tocco, an eyewitness.

Source: William of Tocco, Ystoria sancti Thome de Aquino, in Prümmer, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 1, pp. 147–148, https://archive.org/details/prummerfontesvitaestho. Also Gui, pp. 255–256.

Healings during the inquiry, 1319

Gui records that Archbishop Humbert of Naples, one of the judges, had long suffered a swelling and ulcer of the leg. He prayed to Thomas for strength to carry out the inquiry, rose from his bed cured, and was later cured overnight of an acute fever. Gui also records (no. 89) that Mary, wife of the pope’s nephew Arnald, given up by her doctors with dropsy, was cured after Thomas appeared to her in a vision. Conway adds that the bishop of Viterbo was cured of fever; he gives no source.

Status: reported in Gui’s list (nos. 87 and 89); the cure of the bishop of Viterbo is unsourced.

Source: Bernard Gui, Vita S. Thomae, in Prümmer, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 1, no. 87, p. 249, https://archive.org/details/prummerfontesvitaestho. Conway, St. Thomas Aquinas, ch. 9, https://books.thomistica.net/Conway/conway04.htm.

A stillborn child baptized

Gui records that Mary’s child was born dead. Her mother, thinking the child alive, baptized it, then found it dead and prayed to Thomas with tears. The child appeared to come back to life and soon died again, baptized.

Status: reported in Gui (no. 89).

Source: Bernard Gui, Vita S. Thomae, in Prümmer, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 1, no. 89, p. 251, https://archive.org/details/prummerfontesvitaestho.

A doctor rebuked in a vision

Gui records a doctor who credited a cure to nature and was rebuked in an apparition.

Status: reported in Gui (no. 90).

Source: Bernard Gui, Vita S. Thomae, in Prümmer, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 1, no. 90, p. 251, https://archive.org/details/prummerfontesvitaestho.

A mother raised at Tagulano, after 1323

After the canonization, Gui writes, a dead mother at Tagulano in Piedmont was revived when her son Rufinus, a Dominican, and the family prayed to Thomas. Together with the child in no. 89, it is one of two accounts of the dead brought back to life in these lives, and the account of the mother is missing from one manuscript (Cod. Vat. 1218).

Status: contested; a single manuscript tradition.

Source: Bernard Gui, Vita S. Thomae, in Prümmer, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 1, no. 100, p. 254, https://archive.org/details/prummerfontesvitaestho.

The hand at Salerno

A Count Thomas of San Severino gave the right hand to the Dominicans of Salerno. Tocco tells of a friar who noticed its fragrance only when he approached it a second time.

Status: reported in Tocco.

Source: William of Tocco, Ystoria sancti Thome de Aquino, in Prümmer, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 1, p. 144, https://archive.org/details/prummerfontesvitaestho.

The body in the chest at Fondi, 1348

After the body passed to the Count of Fondi in 1348, Conway writes, Thomas was seen to walk out of the chest that held him, before the count’s mother and the bishop of Fondi, and to lie down again.

Status: legend, in a 1911 biography without an early source.

Source: Placid Conway, St. Thomas Aquinas, 1911, ch. 10, https://books.thomistica.net/Conway/conway04.htm.

Fifty-five miracles at Pavia

A manuscript note to Gui’s life adds that fifty-five miracles were worked at Pavia.

Status: uncorroborated note.

Source: Bernard Gui, Vita S. Thomae, in Prümmer, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 1, p. 256, note, https://archive.org/details/prummerfontesvitaestho.

“As many miracles as articles”

A saying attributed to Pope John XXII, or in another version to a cardinal, claims that Thomas worked as many miracles as he wrote articles. Conway puts it in the pope’s canonization sermon of 1323. No early source for the line was found in Tocco or the canonization documents read for this page.

Status: tradition; attribution contested.

Source: Placid Conway, St. Thomas Aquinas, 1911, ch. 9, https://books.thomistica.net/Conway/conway04.htm; “Thomas Aquinas,” Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Aquinas.

Bernard Gui ended his list with these words: “Many other miracles God has worked through the merits of St. Thomas Aquinas in different parts of the world, which are not written in this treatise.”
