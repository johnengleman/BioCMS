The Church remembers Saint Thomas Aquinas as a man through whom God worked many wonders, in his life and at his tomb. This list gathers those wonders as the oldest witnesses told them: healings, visions, and signs. Each account names who told it and when. The first group holds the ten miracles named in the bull of canonization. A bull is a formal letter from the pope, and canonization is the Church’s act of declaring that someone is a saint in heaven. The Church approved these ten miracles when it made Saint Thomas a saint. Pope John XXII issued this bull on 18 July 1323, after inquiries at Naples in 1319 and at Fossanova in 1321. In it he quoted a psalm: the Lord has brought his saint to light. For each inquiry the pope appointed inquisitors. They questioned the witnesses under oath, and notaries wrote down what the witnesses said. The other accounts come from witnesses who swore oaths at Naples and at Fossanova. They also come from the early Lives by Peter Calo, William of Tocco, and Bernard Gui, from Ptolemy of Lucca, and from later writers. Tocco’s list holds 57 numbered items and Gui’s holds 102, and many of them are not told here.

Miracles approved by the Church

Sweet smell and whole body at Fossanova, 1274

Saint Thomas died at Fossanova abbey on 7 March 1274 and was buried before the high altar. The monks feared that his body would be taken away, so they moved it in secret to the chapel of Saint Stephen. About seven months after his death they opened the tomb, and a sweet smell filled the chapel and the cloister. The body was whole in its robe, even though Saint Thomas had been a heavy man and the grave was wet and deep. Only the tip of the nose showed a small crack. The prior and some of the monks put on their vestments, and the whole community carried him back in procession to his first tomb. On the next day they sang the Mass of a confessor, not the Mass for the dead. In the Church’s calendar a confessor is a saint who was not a martyr. In the bull the pope wrote that the sweet smell told of the purity of his flesh, which was pleasing to God.

Status: approved by the Church. It is the first of the ten miracles in the bull. Abbot Nicholas and the monk Peter of Monte San Giovanni swore to these events in 1319. Abbot Nicholas also told what the monks said at the abbey: Saint Thomas had appeared to the prior in a dream and said, “Bring me back to my first place.”

The tomb is opened again, fourteen years later

Saint Thomas’s sister Theodora wanted his right hand as a relic, and she asked Abbot Peter of Monte San Giovanni for it. In the fourteenth year after Saint Thomas died, Peter opened the tomb with iron tools. The same sweet smell came out. The body was whole in its robe, and only the tip of the nose was a little eaten away. Peter had thought he could pull off the right hand, but he had to cut it off and carry it to Theodora.

Status: the bull counts this smell with the first miracle. It says that many witnesses noticed a similar scent when the monks looked at the body again, some after seven years and others after almost fourteen. At Naples in 1319 Peter of Monte San Giovanni told of a second opening seven years after the first one. Peter swore that he had become abbot by then. At the second opening he had the body moved to a stone tomb above the ground, on the left side of the altar. The same sweet smell came out. The body and the robes were whole, and only a little of the tip of the right thumb was missing. As at the first opening, the monks sang the Mass of a confessor. Brother Nicholas of Piperno told in 1319 of a later opening for Theodora, almost fourteen years after the death. Tocco tells the story of the hand. He says that Theodora kept it with her other relics and that it was still whole when he wrote. She was the Countess of San Severino. Gui tells it too.

A surgeon’s gout is healed at the tomb

A surgeon had suffered from gout for ten years and could not walk without crutches or help. He trusted himself to God and to Saint Thomas and lay down on the tomb. After he prayed he was well again, and he began to jump and run. He praised God, who had healed him of so long an illness through the merits of his saint. Tocco names him as Raynaldus, a doctor who lived at the castle of San Lorenzo near the abbey. He adds that Raynaldus then ran to his horse and mounted it.

Status: approved by the Church. It is the second miracle in the bull. Peter of Monte San Giovanni was then abbot. He swore in 1319 that he saw it and that the monks rang the bells and sang the Te Deum. The Te Deum is a hymn of praise to God. Brother Octavian of Babuco swore in 1319 that Raynaldus came to the monastery riding a donkey. Octavian heard Raynaldus make a vow: “I vow to God and to Saint Thomas of Aquino to have a wax figure made, if he frees me.” Brother Peter of Fundis swore that he had heard that Raynaldus also vowed to return to the Order of Friars Minor, the Franciscans. Raynaldus had once been a member of that order.

A man paralyzed by a nightmare is healed

A man was terrified by a horrible nightmare. Afterward he was paralyzed from his hands to his feet, and his mouth and face were twisted. He could not speak or feel anything, and he looked like a corpse. He did not even feel a fire placed next to his limbs. Then he was taken to the tomb of Saint Thomas, and soon he left it completely healed.

Status: approved by the Church. It is the third miracle in the bull.

Source: John XXII, bull Redemptionem misit, 18 July 1323, §11, English translation, FSSPX News, https://fsspx.news/en/news/seventh-centenary-canonization-st-thomas-aquinas-4-28938.

A mocker of the saint’s hand is healed

A chaplain showed a man several relics and told him that the most precious was the hand of Saint Thomas. The man laughed at the relics. He said: “He is no saint. He is only a brother of the Order of Preachers.” Then he began to tremble. His head felt as if it were shut in a thick, heavy box. He said he was sorry and asked the priest’s pardon. When he kissed the hand, the trembling and the swelling of his head left him. He smelled a strong sweet scent from the hand, and it stayed on him for a long time. The bull says he felt the power of God twice through the saint: he fell ill when he scorned him, and he was healed the moment he repented.

Status: approved by the Church. It is the fourth miracle in the bull, which tells it without names. Tocco says that the man was a cleric of Salerno. He places it about 1316, in the forty-second year after Saint Thomas died. Gui names him as Matthew of Macia, a canon of Salerno. The man swore to it himself in 1319. He gave his name as Abbot Thomas of Mathia, a canon of Salerno, and he named the chaplain who showed the relics as Matthew de Adiutorio. He prayed: “O blessed Thomas, I hold you for a saint, and I am sorry for what I said, and sorry that I lied. I commend myself to you. Help me.” He says that in later temptations he called on Saint Thomas and felt them end. Maffeus Aconzaiocus swore that he was there. He saw the man tremble and heard him say that he could not see. After confession the man honored the hand and was healed at once.

A cleric who lost his voice is healed

A healthy cleric was seized at table by a severe throat illness called angina, and it lasted two days. He could not move his tongue and lost his voice, and the doctors’ many remedies made him worse. He turned to Saint Thomas with devotion, and he wrote down with his own hand that he should be carried to the tomb. His wife objected, because women were not allowed in the church of the monastery. So he himself begged Saint Thomas earnestly for the favor. From that moment he could move his tongue, and soon he was completely well.

Status: approved by the Church. It is the fifth miracle in the bull. Peter Letus was a notary of Piperno. He swore in 1321 that it happened to him in September 1320. He had sat down to eat on a Sunday, and as he was about to eat the first apple the sickness struck him. Doctors let his blood thirteen times and tried many other remedies, but the sickness grew worse. He had no voice from Sunday evening until Wednesday morning. On Wednesday he wrote that he should be carried to the tomb, and he gave the note to his son-in-law Peter. His wife Jacoba objected, because women could not enter the church of Fossanova. So he was not carried. He commended himself to Saint Thomas in his heart. The bull says that he had himself carried to the tomb. Peter’s own oath and Gui say that his wife objected and that he was not carried. Then his tongue began to move, and by the third hour he spoke clearly and was healed. His son-in-law and his daughter Oddolina swore to what they saw. Gui tells it too.

Seven weeks of fever end after a vow

A man had suffered daily fevers and liver and stomach pain for more than seven weeks. On a Friday he grew worse, and he heard of the miracles worked through Saint Thomas. On the Saturday he gave himself devoutly to Saint Thomas in his wife’s presence, as she had advised. By Sunday morning he was entirely delivered from all his sickness.

Status: approved by the Church. It is the sixth miracle in the bull. Nicholas Grassone of Sermoneta swore to it in 1321. His sickness began at the end of September. It also gave him pain in the spleen and the head. On the Friday he heard of the cures of Bartholomew Cambella the farrier and of Peter Craparius. On Saturday evening his wife Mabilia urged him to turn to Saint Thomas, and he vowed himself in her presence. The bull says that this was Saturday afternoon. On Sunday morning he told her: “I am freed through the merits of blessed Thomas of Aquino, to whom I vowed myself.” He came to Fossanova on the day of his oath and kept his vow. The inquisitors and notaries saw that he was well. Gui tells it too.

A deaf mother is healed overnight

For four months a woman’s deafness had grown worse, until she could barely hear the loud cries of her baby son. He was two months old, and she feared for his life. At her husband’s suggestion she gave herself wholly to the saint of God, so that he would take away her deafness. She made that vow and fell asleep that night. The next day she found herself healed through the merits of the saint, as she had hoped.

Status: approved by the Church. It is the seventh miracle in the bull. John Limate of Sonnino swore in 1321 that the woman was his wife, Theodora Manicone. She had been nearly deaf for four months and could hardly hear him even when he called her ten times. They feared for their baby son Amatus. He was about two months old, and she could not hear him cry. One evening in November 1320 she vowed herself to Saint Thomas, with her husband present. The next morning she was healed. Theodora swore to the same account herself, and she said that her husband had urged her to make the vow. Gui tells it too.

A girl who could not swallow is healed

A girl’s throat was so closed that she could not take solid or liquid food, and she could breathe only with great difficulty. Her mother persuaded her, and she commended herself devoutly to Saint Thomas. The next day they took her to the monastery of Fossanova. They placed on her throat relics of Saint Thomas wrapped in a cloth, and she felt better at once. She ate bread and was completely well.

Status: approved by the Church. It is the eighth miracle in the bull. Gemma Rubea of Piperno swore in 1321 that the girl was her daughter Adelasia, who was about twelve. The illness began in April 1321 and lasted about twenty days. By the last night she could take no food or drink. The surgeon Peter Cinus judged it deadly. One night her mother told her to commend herself to Saint Thomas. The next morning her mother took her to Fossanova, where relics were brought out to her in a small case. On the road home she ate bread and was well. Adelasia swore to the same account. Gui tells it too.

A lay brother wakes healed on the tomb

A lay brother of Fossanova had such pain in his right arm and shoulder that he kept the arm in a sling for three months. The medicine he asked for was not available, and the pain grew worse. He humbly made a vow to Saint Thomas for his healing. Then he lay down on the saint’s grave and fell asleep there. Another monk woke him later, and the arm was out of the sling. He raised both hands to scratch his head as people do on waking, and he understood that he was healed. He told the other monks and hid the miracle from no one.

Status: approved by the Church. It is the ninth miracle in the bull. Brother Manuel swore to it in 1321. He had worn the arm in a cloth sling for three and a half months. He promised the community a meal worth twenty soldi every year on the saint’s feast day, if Saint Thomas freed him. James of Piperno was then a monk and assistant sacristan. He found Manuel asleep on the tomb and woke him. Manuel woke with both hands raised to his head. He said: “I am rich, because I am freed through the prayers and merits of blessed Thomas of Aquino, to whom I commended myself.” James swore to what he saw. Gui tells it too. Tocco’s list says that the pain lasted fourteen months and that Manuel vowed to fast on the saint’s vigil every year.

A four-year-old with a tumor is healed

A four-year-old child had a tumor, and his back, legs, and feet were so red and sore that he complained whenever anyone touched them. He had not been able to move for a month because he was afraid of the pain. The doctors saw no cure except surgery. His mother asked Saint Thomas with devotion to heal him without an operation. She took him to the monastery and laid him on the tomb, and soon he got up healed. The bull says that when nature fails and medicine cannot help, help is found in God, who is glorious and works his wonders through his saints.

Status: approved by the Church. It is the tenth miracle in the bull. In 1321 the boy’s father Leonard James, his mother Romica, and both grandmothers swore to the account. The surgeons Andrew Papaleo and Peter Cinus said that the boy could not be healed without cutting, and that he would be crippled for life if they cut him. The father carried the boy to the tomb and laid him on it, because the mother could not enter the church. According to the mother and the grandmothers, the boy got up by himself after a short time. The father tells it a little differently. After a while he took the boy from the tomb and stood him on the ground. The boy walked back to his mother without pain, holding his father’s hand. On the next day the boy walked through Piperno. Gui tells it too.

During Saint Thomas’s life

Lightning at Roccasecca spares the infant Saint Thomas

Tocco writes that a terrible storm rose suddenly over the family castle at Roccasecca. Lightning struck the tower and killed the horses in the stable. It also killed Saint Thomas’s sister, who was sleeping in the tower. His mother Theodora was more worried about the boy than about her daughter. She came trembling to the bed where the nurse slept with him, and she found him unhurt and thanked God.

Status: told by Tocco in the early fourteenth century. He says he had the story of Saint Thomas’s early years from the faithful account of Saint Thomas’s mother.

Source: William of Tocco, Ystoria sancti Thome de Aquino, pp. 67 and 69, in Prümmer, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 1, https://archive.org/details/prummerfontesvitaestho.

A hermit foretells Saint Thomas’s birth

A hermit named Brother Bonus came to Theodora at her castle of Roccasecca. He said: “Rejoice, lady, for you are with child and will bear a son whom you will call Thomas.” He said that she and her husband would think of making the boy a monk at Monte Cassino, but that God would plan otherwise. Her son would be a friar of the Order of Preachers, so devout and so learned that no one in his time could match him.

Status: told by Saint Thomas’s niece Lady Catherine of Morra. She swore in February 1318 before Tocco, a judge, and a notary that she had heard it from Theodora. Tocco reported her oath in the Naples process of 1319. Calo and Tocco tell it in their Lives, and Calo’s version does not name the hermit.

Source: Naples canonization process, 1319, no. 62, p. 350, in Laurent, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 4, https://archive.org/details/fontesvitaesthom00pr. Also Tocco, pp. 66–67, and Calo, p. 18, in Prümmer, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 1, https://archive.org/details/prummerfontesvitaestho.

A baby grips the Hail Mary on paper

Tocco writes that Theodora took her baby to a bath in Naples with other ladies. The baby picked up a small paper that he found there, and he cried loudly when the nurse tried to open his hand. At home his mother opened the hand and found a paper with only the Hail Mary on it. After that the baby cried until someone gave him a scrap of writing, and then he put it in his mouth.

Status: told by Calo, Tocco, and Gui. Calo and Tocco set it at a bath. Gui sets it when the nurse undresses the baby for a bath, and he tells of no outing. In 1319 Peter Caracchulus swore that Lady Constance Fanisari told this story about eight years before. She told it in a house at Naples before many women, and she said that she herself saw it happen.

Source: William of Tocco, Ystoria sancti Thome de Aquino, in Prümmer, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 1, pp. 59–60 and 67–68, https://archive.org/details/prummerfontesvitaestho. A friar sees light shine from a student

Tocco writes that Saint Thomas studied at Naples as a young man. His learning amazed a Dominican friar. That friar had a vision in which a light like the rays of the sun came from the student’s face. In the vision the light lit the eyes of people far away, and the same vision came to the friar three times.

Status: told by Tocco and Gui. Tocco adds that the vision came three times.

Source: William of Tocco, Ystoria sancti Thome de Aquino, in Prümmer, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 1, p. 70, https://archive.org/details/prummerfontesvitaestho. Also Gui, p. 170.

Angels give Saint Thomas a belt of chastity

Saint Thomas’s brothers held him prisoner in the castle of Monte San Giovanni and sent a beautiful girl, dressed to tempt him, into his room. He took a burning stick from the fire and drove her out, and with its end he burned a cross on the wall. Saint Thomas prayed with tears that he would never feel any urge that could harm his purity. He fell asleep, and two angels came to him in a vision and said: “On God’s behalf we put the belt of chastity around you. No temptation can undo it.” He woke with a cry of pain. He told no one why until near the end of his life, and then he told his companion.

Status: told by Tocco, and sworn to in 1319. Tocco swore that he heard it at Anagni in December 1318 from Brother Robert of Sezze and Robert’s uncle, Brother Stephen. Thomas of Cantimpré wrote in Saint Thomas’s lifetime. He says only that women were shut in with Saint Thomas and that he stood firmer than before. He does not mention the stick or the angels.

A dream gives Saint Thomas his text

Saint Thomas told the prior of Fossanova this story at Maenza in 1274. At Paris he had been ordered to prepare to become a master of theology, and he felt too young for it. He prayed beside an altar and fell asleep, and in a dream an old Dominican friar asked what he was thinking. The friar told him to preach on one verse only. It was: “You water the hills from your high rooms; the earth will be filled with the fruit of your works.”

Status: told by Saint Thomas himself, who said that he preached on that verse. Peter of Monte San Giovanni was there in 1274 and swore to it in 1319. Gerard de Frachet wrote before 1260 that Saint Thomas dreamed that someone gave him a book with the verse. The bull of 1323 also tells it: the verse was given to him by a revelation after he had prayed. Brother Peter of Caputio swore in 1319 that at Paris the friars read this story in a book called the Lives of the Brothers. The friars there held that the old friar who appeared to Saint Thomas was Saint Dominic.

A tooth falls out as Saint Thomas prays

Tocco swore in 1319 that he had often heard this story from Saint Thomas’s nephew Count Thomas of San Severino and from Ptolemy of Lucca. At Paris Saint Thomas woke to find that an extra tooth had grown in his mouth, and it hindered his speech. He had to decide a question the next morning, and he did not know what remedy to use. Saint Thomas went to his usual place of prayer, and while he prayed the tooth fell into his hand. Later he showed it to his companion and carried it with him for a long time.

Status: told under oath in 1319 by Tocco, who heard it from the count and from Ptolemy. Calo, Gui, and Tocco’s Life tell it too.

Source: Naples canonization process, 1319, no. 60, p. 347, in Laurent, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 4, https://archive.org/details/fontesvitaesthom00pr. Saint Thomas is seen raised in prayer

Tocco writes that at Salerno Brother James and Saint Thomas’s companion Reginald saw him raised two cubits above the ground in prayer. A cubit is about eighteen inches. It happened before the high altar, after the friars’ night prayer. At Naples the sacristan of the friary saw him raised about two cubits in the air in the chapel of Saint Nicholas. Tocco and Gui name the sacristan Dominic of Caserta, and Calo names him James of Caserta.

Status: told in the early Lives by Calo, Tocco, and Gui, written between about 1300 and 1323.

A crucifix speaks to Saint Thomas at Naples

The friars told that the sacristan of the Naples house heard a voice from the crucifix while Saint Thomas prayed in the chapel of Saint Nicholas. The voice said: “Thomas, you have written well of me. What reward will you take from me for your work?” Saint Thomas answered: “Lord, nothing but you.” Gui gives the answer as “I will take no other reward than yourself.” Tocco says Saint Thomas was then writing the third part of the Summa and wrote little after this.

Status: the friars’ tradition, first written down between about 1300 and 1323 by Calo, Tocco, and Gui.

Source: William of Tocco, Ystoria sancti Thome de Aquino, in Prümmer, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 1, p. 108, https://archive.org/details/prummerfontesvitaestho. Also Calo, p. 38, and Gui, p. 189.

Christ speaks over Saint Thomas’s notebook, Paris

The masters of Paris could not agree on a hard question about the Eucharist, and they chose to accept the answer of Saint Thomas. He wrote his answer and laid the notebook at the altar, and he prayed with his hands raised to the crucifix. His companion and other friars watched, and they saw Christ standing over the notebook. Christ said: “You have written well about the sacrament of my Body. You have settled the question well and truthfully, as far as a person can understand and define it in this life.” While Saint Thomas went on praying, he was seen raised about one cubit in the air.

Status: told by Tocco and by Gui.

Source: William of Tocco, Ystoria sancti Thome de Aquino, in Prümmer, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 1, pp. 125–126, https://archive.org/details/prummerfontesvitaestho. Also Gui, pp. 190–191.

Saint Thomas dictates to several scribes, even asleep

Bernard Gui writes what Saint Thomas’s companion Reginald, his students, and his scribes reported. Saint Thomas dictated on different subjects to three scribes at once, and sometimes to four. Gui says it seemed that God poured several truths into his mind at the same moment. Saint Thomas could dictate them to many scribes only by a special gift of grace, Gui writes.

A scribe named Evenus told one more thing. He was a Breton from the diocese of Tréguier. Saint Thomas had dictated to him and to two other scribes. Then Saint Thomas lay down to rest from the work of dictating. In his sleep he went on dictating, and Evenus wrote down what he heard from the sleeping man. It continued the subject that Saint Thomas had dictated while awake.

Status: told by Bernard Gui in his Life of Saint Thomas. He names Reginald, the students, and the scribe Evenus as his sources.

Source: Bernard Gui, Vita S. Thomae, ch. 32, pp. 198–199, in Prümmer, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 1, https://archive.org/details/prummerfontesvitaestho.

Saint Thomas feels no hot iron or candle flame

Bernard Gui writes that Saint Thomas’s mind was sometimes lifted so far from his senses that he did not feel an injury to his body. His doctors advised a cautery on his leg. A cautery is a treatment with a hot iron. He told his companion to warn him when the man with the fire came. When the moment came he stretched out his leg and was lifted in contemplation. He felt neither the heat nor the iron, and his leg stayed where he had put it.

Gui tells of another time. Saint Thomas was in his room, dictating on the treatise of Boethius on the Trinity. He held a lighted candle in his hand. His mind was so far away that the candle burned down to his fingers. He did not feel the flame. He held the candle without moving his fingers until it burned out. Saint Thomas had forbidden his scribe to call him, whatever the scribe saw.

Gui adds that the same was often seen at Paris. Saint Thomas lifted his mind from his senses before the man came to open a vein and let his blood. Then the man could open the vein safely. Gui says that Saint Thomas was very sensitive to pain by nature, and that God sometimes granted him not to feel what hurt his body.

Status: told by Bernard Gui in his Life of Saint Thomas.

Source: Bernard Gui, Vita S. Thomae, ch. 28, p. 194, in Prümmer, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 1, https://archive.org/details/prummerfontesvitaestho.

Saint Thomas settles an argument at Saint Louis’s table

Bernard Gui tells this story. Saint Louis was the king of France, and he invited Saint Thomas to dine at his table. Saint Thomas excused himself, because he was busy with his study and writing. The prior of Paris made him go, in the king’s name. Saint Thomas left his study with an argument in his mind against the Manichees. They were a sect that he was then answering in his writing.

He sat beside the king, and suddenly God gave him the truth. He struck the table with his hand and said: “The argument against the Manichees is now settled.” Then he called his companion by name and said: “Get up and write.” Saint Thomas spoke as if he were in his room.

The prior touched him and said: “Master, remember that you are at the table of the king of France, not in your cell.” Saint Thomas came to himself and blushed. He bowed to the king and said: “Forgive me, my lord king. I thought I was in my study, where I had begun to think against that heresy.” The king was amazed and moved to see a mind lifted so high that the body’s senses did not hinder it. He called a secretary and ordered him to write down at once what God had revealed to Saint Thomas.

Status: told by Bernard Gui in his Life of Saint Thomas.

Source: Bernard Gui, Vita S. Thomae, ch. 25, pp. 191–192, in Prümmer, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 1, https://archive.org/details/prummerfontesvitaestho.

Lost in thought, Saint Thomas keeps a cardinal waiting

Bernard Gui tells of a cardinal who came to Naples as the pope’s legate, his envoy to the kingdom of Sicily. Archbishop Peter of Capua had been a student of Saint Thomas, and he had told the legate wonders about his teacher. The legate asked him to arrange a friendly talk. Saint Thomas came down from his study and sat between the two men. His mind was so lifted that he said nothing to them.

They waited a long time. Then Saint Thomas said with a cheerful face: “Now I have what I was looking for.” The legate was amazed that Saint Thomas showed no sign of respect. In his heart the legate began to look down on him. The archbishop said: “Lord, do not wonder. He is often taken up like this, and then he speaks to no one.” The archbishop pulled Saint Thomas firmly by the cape and said: “Remember, Master, that the cardinal legate is here. He came to see you.”

Saint Thomas woke as from sleep and bowed to the cardinal. He asked pardon and said: “I thought I was in my study. A fine argument came to me for the work I am now writing, and it gave me great joy.” Gui says that he was then writing the Summa against the Gentiles. The cardinal was amazed and greatly comforted.

Status: told by Bernard Gui in his Life of Saint Thomas. He says that Archbishop Peter of Capua told this to many people.

Source: Bernard Gui, Vita S. Thomae, ch. 25, p. 192, in Prümmer, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 1, https://archive.org/details/prummerfontesvitaestho.

Saint Thomas is lifted in prayer at Mass

Bernard Gui writes that Saint Thomas once said Mass at the Naples friary on Passion Sunday, with many knights standing by. As the Mass went on, he seemed so taken up by the greatness of the sacrament that people thought he had been admitted to share in the divine mysteries. They thought that he felt the sufferings of Christ the man. His long withdrawal of mind and his flood of tears seemed to show it.

He stayed like this for a long time. The friars came and touched him to rouse him, so that he would go on with the Mass. After Mass some friars and some knights who were close to him asked him what had happened. They asked him to tell it for their good. He refused and kept it hidden.

Gui adds that Saint Thomas was often seen like this in Lent when the verse “Do not cast us off in the time of old age” was sung. He seemed lifted out of himself, and he shed many tears from the hidden depths of his mind.

Status: told by Bernard Gui in his Life of Saint Thomas.

Source: Bernard Gui, Vita S. Thomae, ch. 26, pp. 192–193, in Prümmer, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 1, https://archive.org/details/prummerfontesvitaestho.

Saint Thomas’s sister appears to him in visions

Tocco writes that a sister of Saint Thomas appeared to him in a vision while he was at Paris. She said she was in purgatory and asked for a number of Masses, and he asked his students to say Masses and pray for her. Later, in Rome, she appeared again and said that she was free and shared in glory. She told him that he was in a good state and would soon come to her, and that greater glory was ready for him. He asked about his brothers, and she said that Landulf was in purgatory and Rainald was in paradise.

Status: told by Tocco and by Gui, and one manuscript of Calo adds it. Tocco adds that an angel showed Saint Thomas a book with Rainald’s name in gold among the martyrs, because Rainald died for loyalty to the Church under Emperor Frederick.

Brother Romanus appears to Saint Thomas at Naples

Tocco writes that Brother Romanus came to Saint Thomas while he prayed in the church at Naples. Romanus was a master of theology whom Saint Thomas had left teaching at Paris. He said: “I have passed from this life, and I was allowed to appear to you because of your merit.” Saint Thomas asked in God’s name whether his own works pleased God, and Romanus said that they did. Romanus added that he was in eternal life after fifteen days in purgatory, because he had been slow to carry out a will that the bishop of Paris gave him.

Status: told by Calo, Tocco, and Gui. Tocco calls it a bodily vision and not a dream. He says that Saint Thomas asked about a question they had often debated, and that Romanus answered: “Brother Thomas, I see God. Do not ask me more about this question.”

A demon dances before a friar’s pastry

Tocco writes that at Naples the porter called a friar out of choir during the Mass of the community. The friar was given a pastry at the gate. He put it away in his cell and went back to choir. There he began to think about how he could eat it alone and not share it. He could not stay still in choir, because the temptation pressed on him. Saint Thomas saw this in spirit and called the friar over. He said: “Brother, look at what you are thinking, and do not do what tempts you.” The friar said he was thinking only of the Mass. Saint Thomas said: “Tell me why you were called out of choir.” The friar told him everything, including the temptation. Then Saint Thomas said: “I saw a demon dancing in front of you and showing you your pastry. Take care not to eat it alone. Share it with the other friars in charity.” The friar marveled and thanked God and Saint Thomas, who had freed him from the danger of the temptation.

Status: told by Calo and Tocco. Tocco calls it a vision of the imagination and of prophecy.

Source: William of Tocco, Ystoria sancti Thome de Aquino, ch. 46, pp. 119–120, in Prümmer, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 1, https://archive.org/details/prummerfontesvitaestho. Also Calo, p. 43.

A prayer about his brother Rainald is answered

Saint Thomas prayed that he would never love earthly things and never change his state. He also asked God to show him what had become of his brother Rainald. Emperor Frederick had killed Rainald, and Saint Thomas believed it was unjust. God answered him that he would never change his state or love earthly things, and that Rainald’s name was written in the book of life.

Status: told under oath in 1319 by Bartholomew of Capua, who heard it from John of Caiazzo, a close friend of Saint Thomas. Near the end of his life Saint Thomas told Reginald that God had given him these gifts. Tocco and Gui tell that scene.

Saints Peter and Paul explain Isaiah

Saint Thomas had prayed and fasted for many days over a hard passage in Isaiah. One night Reginald heard him talking with someone in his room. Saint Thomas then had Reginald light a candle, and he dictated as if he were reading from a book. Reginald fell at his feet and begged him with tears to say who had been with him. Saint Thomas answered that God had sent the apostles Saint Peter and Saint Paul, who taught him the whole passage. On God’s behalf he ordered Reginald to tell no one as long as he himself lived.

Status: Tocco swore to this at Naples in 1319. He had often heard it from Francis of Alatri, the vicar of the bishop of Nola. Francis had often heard it from Reginald himself. Calo, Tocco, and Gui also tell it in their Lives.

The Mother of God appears to Saint Thomas

Calo and Tocco write that near the end of his life Saint Thomas told Reginald that the Mother of God had appeared to him. She assured him about his life and his learning, and she said that he had received whatever he had asked of God through her. She also gave him what he had often asked in his prayers: he would not change his state in his Order. Tocco adds that it is piously believed that she won for him from her Son the special learning he had asked for, together with the lily of purity.

Status: told by Reginald, Saint Thomas’s companion, and written down by Calo and Tocco.

Saint Thomas runs at a demon at Naples

A judge of Naples named John Blasi swore in 1319 that he saw this himself. Saint Thomas went out of his cell onto an open terrace, and a demon appeared in the shape of a black man dressed in black. Saint Thomas saw that it was a demon and ran at it with his fist raised. He cried: “Why have you come here to tempt me?” As he came close, the demon vanished.

Status: told by a witness who swore an oath, 1319. Blasi said he knew the figure was a demon because he had once seen it in a crystal, in a ritual to find a stolen book. Tocco and Calo tell that Saint Thomas drove the demon away with the sign of the cross and a shout. Tocco names Blasi as the witness.

Saint Agnes’s relics heal a friar at Molara

Ptolemy of Lucca was traveling with Saint Thomas from Rome when they stopped at Molara, a castle of Cardinal Richard. Saint Thomas fell ill there with a fever that came every second day. His companion had a severe fever that never left him, and the cardinal’s doctors held out little hope for him. Saint Thomas sent him the relics of Saint Agnes that he had brought from Rome. He told him to lay them on himself and trust them fully. The friar recovered beyond all hope of the doctors. Saint Thomas decided to keep a feast of Saint Agnes each year with a good meal for the friars, and he kept it that year at Naples.

Status: told by an eyewitness, Ptolemy of Lucca. He says Saint Thomas died the next year. Calo, Tocco, and Gui tell it differently. In their Lives Saint Thomas goes to the sick friar and lays the relics on his chest, and Gui calls the friar Reginald. Ptolemy names the sick friar Raymund. Tocco swore in 1319 that he heard the story from Ptolemy.

A woman is healed by touching his cloak

Saint Thomas preached at Saint Peter’s in Rome during Holy Week and again on Easter Sunday. After the sermon a woman who had suffered from a flow of blood for a long time touched the hem of his cloak. She believed that she could be healed through his merits, and she was cured at once. She followed him to the Dominican house at Santa Sabina and told his companion Reginald. Reginald told the story to many people later.

Status: told by Calo, Tocco, and Gui. Leonard of Gaeta swore in 1319 that he heard it from Reginald. He places it on Easter Sunday at Saint Mary Major.

A bright star stands over Saint Thomas’s bed

A notary of Naples named John Coppa swore in 1319 that he saw this himself in 1274. Coppa visited Saint Thomas with his brother Bonfilius, who was a Dominican friar. Saint Thomas lay ill in his cell at Naples. A very bright star came in through a window of the cell and stood over the bed for about as long as it takes to say a Hail Mary carefully. It was white and almost silver, and then it vanished.

Status: told by an eyewitness who swore an oath, 1319. Coppa said that his brother Bonfilius also saw it, though Bonfilius had died by 1319. Gui tells it the same way. Calo and Tocco say that a young brother of the friar Bonfilius was alone in the room, and that the star rested above Saint Thomas’s head.

After Mass at Naples, Saint Thomas stops writing

Saint Thomas said Mass in the chapel of Saint Nicholas at Naples in 1273 and was moved by a wonderful change. After that Mass he never wrote or dictated again, and he left the third part of the Summa unfinished in the treatise on Penance. Reginald asked him why he left so great a work, and Saint Thomas answered: “Reginald, I cannot.” When Reginald pressed him, Saint Thomas made him swear to tell no one as long as he himself lived. Then he said: “Everything I have written seems to me like straw, compared with what I have seen and what has been shown to me.”

Status: told under oath in 1319 by Bartholomew of Capua. He heard it from an old friar named John del Giudice. Near his own death, Reginald had confessed it to this friar. In this account Saint Thomas later went to his sister, the Countess of San Severino, and hardly spoke to her. Tocco and Gui place the scene at San Severino, where Saint Thomas stayed in a trance for a long time. They give his words as: “The end of my writing has come. Such things have been shown to me that what I wrote and taught seems small to me.” They do not use the word “straw.” These accounts do not say what he saw.

Fresh herring appear at Maenza, 1274

Saint Thomas fell ill at his niece Francesca’s castle at Maenza on his way to the Council of Lyon, and he lost his appetite. He wished for the fresh herring he had eaten in France, and none had ever been seen in that region. A man came from Terracina with a load of sardines, and one box held fresh herring. Tocco says that the doctor John of Piperno found the box and sent the fish to Saint Thomas, who refused to eat them. Peter of Monte San Giovanni swore that he was there and that Saint Thomas ate them with his companions.

Tocco and Gui say that many others ate the fish.

At his death and burial, 1274

Stars are seen when Saint Thomas dies

Tocco writes that a monk of Fossanova fell asleep while he prayed in the church at the hour of Saint Thomas’s death. In a dream he saw a star of wonderful brightness fall on the monastery. Two other stars came down to it, and then the first star rose to heaven. When he woke, he heard the board struck as the signal of a death. Tocco adds that a star like a comet was seen over the monastery for three days before Saint Thomas died, and it vanished when he died.

Status: told in the early Lives by Calo, Tocco, and Gui. In 1319 Leonard of Gaeta swore that he had heard a version from Thomas of Fosco, a student of Saint Thomas. A hermit of Campania had told Thomas of Fosco that he saw a great star fall on Fossanova at the time of the death. Two other stars lifted it to heaven.

Saint Paul leads Saint Thomas from the school

Brother Paul of Aquila taught in the Naples house and had a vision as Saint Thomas died at Fossanova. In it Saint Thomas was lecturing to many students at Naples when Saint Paul came into the school with a company of saints. Saint Thomas asked Saint Paul whether he had understood his letters correctly, and Saint Paul said he had, as well as a man in this life can. He said he would lead Saint Thomas to a place of clearer understanding, and he seemed to take him by the cape and lead him out of the school. The friar cried out, “Help, brothers, for Brother Thomas is being taken from us,” and the friars later found that the hour matched Saint Thomas’s death.

Status: vision told by Brother Paul and written down by Calo, Tocco, and Gui.

Saint Albert weeps in Cologne as Saint Thomas dies

Saint Thomas died in Lent. On that day Saint Albert was sitting at table at Cologne with the prior and some friars. Suddenly he began to weep. The prior asked him why he wept. Saint Albert answered: “I bring grave news. Brother Thomas of Aquino has died. He was my son in Christ and the light of the Church. God has revealed it to me.”

The prior wrote down the day. Later it was found that Saint Thomas had died at that same time.

Status: told by Bernard Gui, and sworn to in 1319 by Brother Antony of Brescia. Antony swore that he heard it from Brother Albert of Brescia. In Gui’s telling Saint Albert says only that Saint Thomas of Aquino, his son in Christ and the light of the Church, has died.

A friar sees Saint Thomas say Mass

On the third day after Saint Thomas died, Brother Rainerius of Pisa wept at Anagni because he thought that so much learning was lost. He fell asleep and saw Saint Thomas come out of the sacristy in priest’s robes and say Mass. After the Gospel Saint Thomas preached to the people. When Saint Thomas came back, Rainerius saw that his right eye was much larger than his left and far brighter. Saint Thomas said that this was the difference between the knowledge he now had in heaven and the knowledge he had while he lived on earth.

Status: vision told by Brother Rainerius and written down by Calo and Tocco.

A blind monk sees at Saint Thomas’s body

Peter of Monte San Giovanni swore in 1319 that he saw this himself. John of Ferentino was the subprior of Fossanova, and for about twenty days he had not been able to see. He could not recognize people or read. A monk advised him to put his eyes against the eyes of the dead Saint Thomas, and he did so on the day Saint Thomas died. Tocco and Calo say that he knelt at the feet of the body with reverence and laid his face on the face of Saint Thomas. He prayed to God to give him back his sight through the saint’s merits. At once he could see, and he cried out: “Blessed be God, for by the merits of this saint my sight is perfectly restored.”

Status: told by an eyewitness who swore an oath, 1319. It is not among the ten miracles in the bull. Peter said that he saw John healthy for thirty years afterward. Calo and Tocco tell it too. Brother Octavian of Babuco swore in 1319 that John told him himself how he was healed. In Octavian’s account John prayed at the foot of the tomb. His sickness was a cataract. John later became bishop of Umbriatico.

Saint Thomas’s mule dies at his bier

Tocco writes that Saint Thomas’s niece Francesca did not dare to enter the monastery. She asked the abbot as a favor to have the body carried to the monastery door. At the door she and two other women mourned over it with loud cries. While they cried, the mule that Saint Thomas had ridden broke its rope in the stable and came to the bier with no one leading it. It fell dead there “with no other illness.”

Status: told in the early Lives by Calo, Tocco, and Gui.

Earth from Saint Thomas’s grave smells sweet

Tocco writes that the monks carried the body back to the church. While the monks were at dinner, a mason named Jacobus stayed to guard it. He opened the lid of the chest and took out earth that had fallen near the breast of the body. The earth gave off a strong sweet smell that filled the choir. A nobleman who had come for relics took some of it for his own shrine.

Saint Thomas appears to a leper

A young man of Piperno named Thomasius had leprosy. He came to the tomb of Saint Thomas with devotion, trusting in the saint’s merits, and the monks let him spend the night there. While Thomasius slept, Saint Thomas appeared to him and asked: “What do you ask of me?” The man answered that he asked God to free him through Saint Thomas’s merits. Saint Thomas said: “Get up and be healed.” The man woke with joy and found himself healed of all his sickness. He gave thanks to God and praised the merits of Saint Thomas with all devotion.

Status: told in Tocco’s list of miracles gathered for the canonization (no. 7). It is not among the ten in the bull. Gui tells it too (no. 76).

A man paralyzed by a demon is healed

Peter of Piperno was an oblate of Fossanova, a man who had given himself to serve the monastery. As he lay in bed, an evil spirit appeared to him in the shape of an ape and mocked him. He could not move his limbs or make the sign of the cross, and everyone thought he was possessed. Two monks and another oblate carried him with devotion to the tomb of Saint Thomas. There on their knees they begged God to heal him through the saint’s merits, and at once he was healed. He left the world and became a lay brother of the monastery.

Status: told in Tocco’s list (no. 3). Gui tells it too (no. 4). Peter himself swore in 1319 to what he saw. A hairy man with a dog’s body and a man’s face stood by his bed. The man brought him water in his hands and told him to drink, and a horrible smell came from him. Peter hid his face and refused. Fear took away his strength and his speech until the next day. His hands and fingers were drawn up, and his feet were heavy. He was carried to his mother’s house at Piperno. After eight days she had him brought to the tomb, and she waited outside the monastery gate, because no woman could enter. When she heard that he was well, she gave thanks to God. After that she fasted for three days every year in that season. Brother Gregory of Saint Stephen and a servant named Perrotto carried him to the tomb, and each of them said three Our Fathers and three Hail Marys for him. Gregory swore that Peter did not feel it when fire was put near his limbs. This is the same healing as the bull’s third miracle above. The bull gives the same signs but no name.

A poor man lame from birth stands up

A poor man had weak shins and legs from birth and could not walk. He dragged his legs behind him like pieces of flesh. Tocco writes that he was poor in goods but rich in devotion. He heard that God worked many miracles at Saint Thomas’s tomb through the saint’s merits, and he had himself carried there. He prayed a long time and begged God for the favor. Suddenly his legs grew strong, and he stood up and began to walk like a healthy man. He gave thanks to God, who heals the brokenhearted through the merits of his saints.

Storms stop when monks walk to the tomb

Bartholomew of Ferentino was a lay brother of Fossanova. He had an abscess that the doctors gave up on. His abbot urged him to turn to Saint Thomas and to commend himself to God through the saint’s merits. As soon as he made the vow, the abscess vanished. Bartholomew added one more thing. Whenever a bad storm frightened the monks, they called on the name of God and on the merits of Saint Thomas. Then they walked in procession to his tomb, and the storm stopped at once.

Status: told in Tocco’s list (no. 8). It is not among the ten in the bull.

A monk’s prayer stops a hailstorm near Sonnino

James of Piperno was a monk of Fossanova. Tocco’s list calls him the cellarer, the monk who looked after its food and supplies. He was going to the convent of Saint Mary of Cannis at Sonnino for the funeral of a nun. On the road a violent storm of hail and thunder broke over him, and he feared that the large hailstones would hurt him. He called on God through the merits of Saint Thomas. At once the whole storm stopped and calm followed.

Status: told in Tocco’s list (no. 20). It is not among the ten in the bull. Three monks swore to this event in 1321. James of Piperno was walking with Brother Nicholas of Frusinone and Brother John of Terracina to the funeral of a nun at Saint Mary of Cannis. Hailstones as big as nuts struck him on the head and hands, and the sky was black. He cried out: “Saint Thomas of Aquino, help me!” At once the hail stopped and the air cleared. Nicholas remembered rain and hail together, and John remembered rain. John says that James then said: “Blessed be God and blessed Thomas of Aquino, to whom I commended myself, for he made this storm cease.”

A drowning youth is saved at the mill

A young man named John of Theodino was filling a water jar in the river by the monastery of Fossanova. The current swept him and the jar into the channel of the mill. He struck the machinery of the mill and could not help himself. He cried out: “Saint Thomas of Aquino, help me!” At once he felt a man take him by the head and lift him out of the water.

Status: told in Tocco’s list (no. 36). It is not among the ten in the bull. Tocco says that a lay brother of the abbey named Brother Manuel came up as it happened. Gui tells it too (no. 80).

A swollen throat heals on the road home

Margaret of Piperno had a swollen throat from childhood. She was promised to a man named Nicholas, and he refused to marry her because of it. With her mother and her brother Peter she went to Fossanova with great devotion, but women could not enter the monastery. At the gate she and her mother begged a monk for God’s sake to bring out a relic of Saint Thomas. They hoped that through his merits she would be healed. The sacristan carried out the head of Saint Thomas and placed it against her throat. She prayed and begged the help of Saint Thomas, and on the way home she vomited a lump of matted flesh. Her throat was whole. Her brother found her well three days later, and in time she married Nicholas and had children.

Status: told under oath in 1319 by her brother Peter of Piperno, a monk of Fossanova. He swore that he saw the head laid on her throat. Gui tells it too (no. 15). A doubting blacksmith’s arm is healed

Brother Leonard of Piperno was a lay brother and a blacksmith at Fossanova. Gui writes that two Dominican friars had come to inquire into the miracles of Saint Thomas. Leonard was asked to shoe their mules, and he refused. He thought: “How these friars trouble us because of Brother Thomas! If he is the saint they say he is, let him work a great miracle so that these friars go away.” At once a pain struck his right arm. He could not move it or lift it to his mouth or do any work with it. The next day he remembered his thought and repented. He went to the tomb of Saint Thomas and rested his arm on it. He prayed there for an hour, and the arm was healed. Then he shod the mules with great joy.

Status: told in Gui’s list (no. 7) and Tocco’s list (no. 22). Tocco tells it differently. In his account Leonard doubted the miracles because he had seen none. He woke with his right arm paralyzed and went to the tomb. He was healed fully only when he confessed his unbelief and promised never to doubt again. Then he gave thanks to God, who had freed his soul from the sin of unbelief through the saint’s merits. Leonard himself swore in 1319 that the pain struck him on a Saturday in June, between the ninth hour and Vespers. On Sunday morning after Mass he rested his arm on the tomb and prayed for an hour, and he was healed. On Monday he went back to his trade. His apprentices James of Sonnino and Leo of Piperno saw him sick and then well.

A wounded arm is healed at Piperno

Nicholas of Maximo lived at Piperno. His enemies wounded him badly in the right arm. Gui writes that the bone had come apart from the shoulder. The doctors healed the flesh over it, but the bone stayed apart and the arm hung useless. One evening Nicholas made a vow to Saint Thomas. He promised to visit his tomb and to offer an arm made of wax if he was healed. In the morning he woke with his arm whole. He could move it and work with it as before. The monks examined the arm closely and felt it with their hands, and Nicholas kept his promise.

Status: told in Gui’s list (no. 9) and Tocco’s list (no. 25). Tocco adds that Nicholas’s wife urged him to turn to Saint Thomas. Nicholas also promised to forgive his enemies and make peace with a kiss the next day, and he rose in the morning giving thanks to God. Peter of Piperno swore in 1319 that he had heard of it at the abbey.

A paralyzed girl sees a friar beside her

Gui writes that a man of Saint Lawrence of the Valley, near Fossanova, had a daughter struck by madness. She lay still like a stone and could not eat or speak or breathe easily. No doctor could help her. Her father heard of the miracles of Saint Thomas and vowed her to him. He prayed that through the merits of Saint Thomas she would either be taken from this life or be healed by God’s mercy. She was carried half alive to Fossanova, and the abbot let her into the church. They laid her on the tomb of Saint Thomas until the monks came back from their meal. Her father wanted to lift her from the tomb out of respect for the community. She said: “Father, do not touch me! A great Dominican friar who heals me stands before me. He protected me from a black man who held me bound.” The abbot and the monks came and prayed that God would hear the father’s prayer through the merits of Saint Thomas. Then the girl saw the friar draw both his hands down her body from head to feet. He said: “Girl, get up, for you are cured.” At that word she stood up in good health.

Status: told in Gui’s list (no. 98). The girl herself told what she saw.

Two animals survive falls in the Alps

Messengers carrying the record of the inquiry to the pope came to the lake of Lausanne in the Alps. A pack animal with a Roman’s baggage rolled down a very high cliff and landed on sharp rocks by the lake. Canon Matthew called on Saint Thomas, and the animal stood up unhurt with its load. Then Matthew’s own horse slipped on the cliff edge, and he gave it up for lost. He called on Saint Thomas again, and the horse leaped back onto the road.

Status: told in Tocco’s list (nos. 56 and 57). Gui tells both events in his no. 88. In Gui’s account the pack animal belonged to Canon Cincius, who prayed for it, and Gui calls Matthew “of Viterbo.” Neither is among the ten in the bull.

Wind saves the records off Monte Argentario

Friars carrying the record of Saint Thomas’s miracles sailed from Naples toward the pope’s court. One night, in rain and strong wind, the ship was driven toward the rocks of Monte Argentario. The friars prayed to God with loud weeping. They called on the Virgin Mary, Star of the Sea, and on Saint Dominic and Saint Peter Martyr, and last of all on Saint Thomas. They begged him not to let the record of the miracles God had worked through him be lost in the sea. A new wind blew from the side of the mountain and turned the ship away, and everyone on board shouted that God had worked a miracle.

Status: told by Tocco in the third person, and by Gui. Gui and one manuscript of Tocco name the friars as William of Tocco and Robert of Benevento.

A monk sees Saint Thomas before the canonization

Tocco writes that Bartholomew of Sulmona was a monk of Fossanova known for his devotion, and his abbot vouched for him. While the inquiry into the miracles was under way, he saw Saint Thomas in a vision by night. Saint Thomas came into the monastery choir with many friars of his Order. The monk asked why he had come to the monastery again. Saint Thomas answered: “The pope is having inquiries made into my life, and I have come about this.” The monk answered: “It is true that you should be canonized. But Brother Peter of Morrone was canonized only after he died, and you are still alive.” Saint Thomas said: “Son, no one is canonized unless he is alive. Brother Peter of Morrone lives, and so he is canonized.” Then Saint Thomas bowed low and showed how Saint Peter used to pray in his cell. He said: “Brother Peter was a canonized saint because he was very devoted in prayer. About me you will sing this invitatory.” In the dream Saint Thomas spoke the invitatory to him with great earnestness. Then it seemed to the monk that Saint Thomas was putting on his vestments for Mass. The monk woke up rejoicing during the Mass. God had shown him wonders about his saint. Afterward he could not remember the invitatory.

Status: told in Tocco’s list of miracles gathered for the canonization (no. 14). Tocco says the monk’s abbot vouched for him.

Two judges of the 1319 inquiry are healed

Tocco writes that Archbishop Humbert of Naples had long suffered from a swollen leg with an ulcer. He lay in bed and could not walk. He asked God humbly and devoutly to heal him through the merits of Saint Thomas. His wish was to finish the inquiry for the glory of God and of the saint. As soon as he had said this with devotion, he rose from bed healed and walked around the room. The other judge was Bishop Angelo of Viterbo, and a high fever seized him one evening. He asked Saint Thomas for the same help and slept, and in the morning he got up cured but weak.

Status: told in Tocco’s list (nos. 53 and 54). Tocco says the two judges wrote to the pope about both cures. Gui (no. 87) gives both cures to Archbishop Humbert.

Lady Mary is healed of dropsy at Avignon

Gui writes that Lady Mary had dropsy at Avignon and that her doctors had given up on her. Her confessor told her to vow herself to Saint Thomas, whose canonization was then being decided. That night Saint Thomas appeared by her bed in the robe of a Preaching Friar. He spoke to her with sweet words and a gentle voice, and he asked: “Do you want to be cured?” He told her that she would be healed if she kept her vow. She and her mother vowed to send a candle every Christmas to the nearest Dominican house and to clothe a friar in need. By morning her swelling had gone down. She had not yet kept her vow, so Saint Thomas came again that night and scolded her. Then she kept it, and she was fully healed.

Status: told in Gui’s list (no. 89). Gui says that Mary was the wife of Arnald, the pope’s nephew.

A stillborn baby is seen alive for baptism

Gui writes that Lady Mary later could not give birth, because the child lay half born. She called on Saint Thomas, and at once she gave birth easily. Her mother thought the child was alive and baptized him on the spot. Then Lady Mary’s mother learned that he had been born dead. She prayed devoutly with tears that Saint Thomas, who had freed her daughter twice, would bring the child back. She asked that he be reborn in baptism and live for ever, and not be lost. After her prayer the child was seen alive again. He was reborn in baptism, and soon he went to live for ever.

Status: told in Gui’s list (no. 89).

A doctor is rebuked in a vision

A man with a fever that kept coming back asked Saint Thomas for help. The fever left him at once. A doctor said that nature could have done this. Saint Thomas appeared to the doctor and rebuked him, because he had given honor to nature and not to God. He added that soon a Mass in his own honor would be sung in the Church.

Status: told in Gui’s list (no. 90).

A man’s lifelong dislike of fish ends

Master Matthew of Viterbo was a chaplain of the archbishop of Naples. He was one of the two men who carried the record of the inquiry to the pope at Avignon. Gui writes that Matthew was past fifty and had never been able to eat fish. The smell and taste made him sick, and when others pressed him to eat it, he vomited or ran a fever. At the start of Lent he asked Saint Thomas to take away his disgust so that he could eat with his companions. That night he dreamed that a Dominican friar served him a fish. The friar said: “Master Matthew, eat. Brother Thomas sends you this fish. As this one tastes good to you, so will others from now on.” In the dream he ate it with relish. At the noon meal fish was brought, and he ate it gladly.

Status: told in Gui’s list (no. 28). Gui says that Matthew swore to it before the cardinals at the papal court. People who had known him for many years confirmed it.

A dead mother sits up, Tagulano, after 1323

He asked them to pray that God would raise his mother through Saint Thomas’s merits. They all knelt and begged God with tears, and the woman suddenly sat up alive. Their weeping turned to laughter and their grief to joy.

A Franciscan sees Saint Francis and Saint Thomas

Gui writes about a Franciscan named Brother Leutherius. A question puzzled him, and he had neglected to look for the answer in the books of Saint Thomas. A friar smells the hand’s scent, Salerno

Count Thomas of San Severino gave Saint Thomas’s right hand to the Dominican house at Salerno. He wanted the saint’s own order to keep it with more respect. A very devout friar went to honor it and hoped to smell the sweet scent he had heard about. He smelled nothing, so he prepared himself and approached again with more devotion. A knight’s useless arm is healed at the tomb

Peter Grassus was a knight of Naples in the king’s service. For about ten months his right arm had lost its strength. He could not comb his hair or tie his cap under his chin without help.

He was traveling to Rome as a pilgrim with two other knights of Naples. It was a Thursday toward evening in Lent. Near Terracina he thought of the body of Saint Thomas at Fossanova. He hoped that the saint’s merits could help his arm, and he decided in his heart to go there. At the monastery a monk showed him the tomb on the left side of the high altar. Peter had a cloth taken off it and knelt before it.

He prayed: “Lord God, you are wonderful in your saints. Through the merits of this saint, give me back the health that my arm has lost.” Then he stretched his whole body over the tomb and felt his strength come back. Only a numbness around the joints stayed, and it left that evening. Peter turned to his companions and said: “Hear a great miracle! I had great pain in my arm. I prayed on this tomb, and now I am healed.” After that day his arm was as sound as if it had never been sick.

Status: told under oath in 1319 by Peter Grassus himself. The knights Nicholas Filimarino and Henry Caracchulus swore that they were with him. Filimarino swore that he heard Peter say the words above as he rose from prayer. Tocco and Gui tell it too.

A knight’s sore eyes are healed at the tomb

James Capuanus was a knight of Naples. His eyes were full of a bloody fluid and gave him great pain. On a Thursday in Lent he was traveling toward Rome as a pilgrim with Peter Grassus and other men of Naples. At the tomb he knelt and bowed his head, and he touched the tomb with his eyes. He prayed to God that Saint Thomas’s merits would free him from the sickness of his eyes, and he said an Our Father. Then he stood up. Before he left the church he was healed.

Saint Augustine shows Brother Albert Saint Thomas’s glory

Brother Albert of Brescia was a devout, truthful, and learned Dominican. He often said: “I know, brothers, that Brother Thomas, the great teacher, is a saint in heaven.” Two friars pressed him to say how he could be so sure. They begged him in the name of God. Albert did not want to seem to scorn that name, so he told them.

He said that he had followed the teaching of Saint Thomas in all his reading, teaching, and writing. He had often wondered how Saint Thomas could reach such holiness and learning in so short a life. So he had prayed many times to God, to the Blessed Virgin, and to Saint Augustine to show him the glory of Saint Thomas. One day he prayed for a long time with tears before the altar of the Virgin Mary.

While he was awake in prayer, two men of great dignity appeared in wonderful splendor. One wore a bishop’s robes and a mitre. The other wore the robe of a Dominican, with a golden crown set with precious stones. Around his neck were two collars, one of silver and one of gold. On his chest was a great precious stone that sent out rays and lit the whole church. His cape was covered with precious stones, and his tunic and scapular shone white as snow. Albert threw himself at their feet and begged them to say who they were.

The one with the mitre answered: “Brother Albert, why do you wonder so? Your prayers are heard. I am Augustine, doctor of the Church. I was sent to show you the teaching and the glory of Brother Thomas of Aquino, who is here with me. He is my son. He followed the teaching of the apostles and my teaching in all things, and he lit the Church with his own. The precious stones show this. The great stone on his chest shows the right purpose that he had in defending and explaining the faith. Other stones stand for the many books that he wrote. In glory Thomas is equal to me. His crown of virginity is above mine, but I am above him in the rank of a bishop.”

After this Albert forbade the two friars to tell anyone while he lived, unless an inquiry into the miracles of Saint Thomas and his canonization took place.

A boy is healed without surgery after a prayer

John Garini of Piperno was a boy of nearly eight. He was born with the right testicle larger than the left, and it grew day by day to the size of a hen’s egg. It was as hard as wood, and the intestine slipped down into the pouch. He wore a truss. The doctors were Master Gregory and Master Leonard of Julgiano. They said that it could not be healed without cutting. They added that if they cut him, they would expect him to die. His grandfather John grieved and would not give him to the doctors.

His grandmother Maria Simeonis heard of the fame of Saint Thomas of Aquino. She believed that the boy could be healed without cutting, and that a child’s prayer might be heard sooner. She taught him to say: “Dear blessed Thomas of Aquino, if you will heal me of my sickness without any cutting, I will come to your tomb. I will bring a silver offering in the shape of my own figure, when you are canonized.” As he said these words in her presence, he began to improve. Soon he was fully healed.

About eight months after his healing, his family brought him before the inquisitors. The inquisitors and notaries saw him healed.

Status: told under oath in 1321 by the grandmother Maria Simeonis, the grandfather John Garini, the father Peter, and the doctors Gregory and Leonard of Julgiano. Gui tells it too.

Two women of Piperno are freed from arm pain

Schimana was a woman of Piperno, over fifty years old. For about four years she had pain in her right shoulder, arm, and finger. She could not spin. On the feast of Saint Martin in 1321 she came to Fossanova. That was eight days before she gave her oath. She devoutly vowed herself to Saint Thomas of Aquino. She promised a silver offering worth twelve pence if he freed her. From then on she felt no pain.

Lady Lea Pennazola came with her. Lea had the same pain in her right shoulder and arm, and Schimana swore that she was freed too. Lea told a longer story herself. For a year and a half she could not use that arm. One day she heard that relics of Saint Thomas had been brought to the church of Saint Mary at Piperno. She went there and saw the relics on the high altar. She commended herself to Saint Thomas and laid her arm on the case that was said to hold them. At once she was healed, and she used the arm as before. This was on a Sunday in May 1320.

Status: told under oath in 1321 by Schimana and by Lea herself. Lea’s daughter-in-law Romica swore that she saw Lea sick and then well. Gui tells both accounts.

A man’s fever and vomiting stop at once

Peter Ectari of Piperno was at Mass in the church of Saint Mary on a Sunday in October 1321, the Sunday before the feast of Saint Luke. A strong fever seized him. He went home, and the fever and vomiting afflicted him so badly that he feared he would die. His wife Gayta urged him to commend himself to Saint Thomas of Aquino. He did so that same day, and she heard him. His mother and a friend were also in the room. At once the fever and the vomiting stopped.

He was so well that on the Tuesday he set out on a trip. He says that he went to Calabria. His wife and his aunt say that he went to a fair in the kingdom within three days.

Status: told under oath in 1321 by Peter himself, by his wife Gayta, and by his aunt Maria Pepe. Gui tells it too.

A lay brother in Sicily regains his speech

Brother Peter Crescentii of Piperno was a lay brother of Fossanova. About three years before his oath he was in Sicily, at the monastery’s farm of Saint Christopher. One morning a swelling came on his face and throat. He could not speak. He could not open his mouth, even when a man tried to open it with a knife.

A man of Piperno named Nicholas of Monte San Giovanni was there. He advised Peter to commend himself to Saint Thomas of Aquino. Peter could not speak, so he commended himself humbly in his heart. Nicholas saw him join his hands. At about the ninth hour Peter fell asleep. At the hour of Vespers he woke healthy and able to speak, and the swelling had mostly gone down. Nicholas heard him begin to speak and confess his sins to Brother Gregory of Cisterna, a monk who was there. The swelling later left him completely.

Status: told under oath in 1321 by Peter himself and by Nicholas of Monte San Giovanni, who was with him. Gui tells it too.

A deaf man hears even low voices

Nicholas Picardi of Terracina had a ringing in his ears and was nearly deaf for fifteen days. He could hardly hear people who spoke to him, even in a loud voice. He came to Piperno and thought about the miracles that God worked through Saint Thomas of Aquino. At the midday meal on the day before his oath he commended himself to Saint Thomas. His friends Nicholas Taurellus and James Cafassus were with him.

The next morning he got up and heard well. The inquisitors and notaries saw that he heard well, even words spoken in a low voice. His friend James Cafassus swore that during the fifteen days Nicholas had said to him and to others: “I do not hear you.” Both friends swore that they saw him hear well the next morning.

Status: told under oath in 1321 by Nicholas Picardi and by his two friends. Gui tells it too.

A wife who lost her speech is healed

Jacoba was the wife of Nicholas Petri Nicholai of Piperno. In November 1321 she had a continuous fever for five days. It made her so weak that she lost her speech. On the fifth day people thought that she was dying. Her mother-in-law Herminia Luce, her husband, and others stood around her and wept.

Her husband commended her to Saint Thomas of Aquino. He promised to bring a lamp, a candle, and money for a Mass to the tomb if Saint Thomas healed her. At once she began to vomit. Her speech came back, the fever left her, and she was healed. Her husband kept his vow.

Status: told under oath in 1321 by her husband Nicholas. Gui tells it too.

A man crying that he is dying is freed

On Sunday 8 November 1321 after Vespers a great pain struck Peter Carolus of Terracina in his head and his whole body. He cried out until bedtime, and he believed that he would soon die. The women in the room believed it too. His wife and his daughter called Lady Gemma Ronilgione and Mabilia, the sister of Raynald Bulizola, to come.

Both women said to him: “Peter, commend yourself to blessed Thomas of Aquino.” He commended himself at once with devotion. While the women still stood there, he vomited heavily and was freed from all the pain. Mabilia says that he said to her: “Dear Mabilia, blessed be God and blessed Thomas of Aquino, through whose merits I am freed.”

A boy named Leonard Bulizola swore that he came to the house and heard Peter cry out: “I am dying, I am dying! Let the priest come!” Leonard was about thirteen. He saw Peter freed.

Status: told under oath in 1321, eight days later. Peter himself swore to it, and so did Gemma, Mabilia, and the boy Leonard.

A child with a flow of blood is healed

Julianessa of Sonnino had a little grandson named Nicholutius, the son of her son Peter. For almost a year he had a flow of blood, and she firmly believed that he would die. When Brother William of Tocco came to Sonnino, she said to him: “Brother William, if blessed Thomas gave us this boy back, it would be a great miracle.”

Tocco said: “Bring him to me at the church of Saint Angelo in this town, and we will commend him to blessed Thomas of Aquino.” She carried the boy to the church. There she and Tocco commended him to Saint Thomas. At once the boy began to improve. Within a few days he was fully well, and the flow of blood stopped. As far as she knows, he has had no illness since.

Status: told under oath in 1321 by Julianessa. A year had passed since the cure, and the boy was then six. She names her husband Bertraymus Varolus, her son Peter, her daughter-in-law Alcruda, and Tocco as people who knew of it.

A young man carries a lance to the tomb

Nicolucius Affinnati of Piperno was about twenty. For three months he had a pain in his right shoulder and arm, and he could not lift the arm to his mouth. On a Saturday in September 1320 he commended himself to Saint Thomas of Aquino and asked to be freed. His wife Leonarda was with him. On Sunday morning he got up and found himself healed. That same morning he came to the tomb and offered a candle, as he had vowed.

His brothers Frederick and Barbatus walked with him. Barbatus was sixteen. He swore that Nicolucius carried a lance in the hand of his bad arm. At first he did not seem to carry it freely. After they had visited the tomb, Barbatus saw him carry the lance well. From then on he did everything as a healthy man does.

Status: told under oath in 1321 by Nicolucius and by his brother Barbatus. Gui tells it too.

A cleric’s bloody cough ends at the tomb

Master Marcus was a cleric of the church of Saint Lucy at Piperno. Sixteen years before his oath he began to have a cough that lasted three years. Each winter, from its start until about May, he coughed hard and spat thick phlegm. Blood poured from his mouth and nose, two or three times a day.

One day in March he came to Fossanova. He humbly commended himself to Saint Thomas of Aquino and laid himself on the tomb. Lady Guerra Gulferama of Piperno and Dom Peter Andreae, a monk, were present. Marcus went back to Piperno. That night he vomited a large amount of blood, and from then on he was well. This was thirteen years before his oath, in the week of the Annunciation.

Status: told under oath in 1321 by Master Marcus himself. Gui tells it too. Tocco’s list tells a similar account of a man named Marcus Brachialis of Piperno.

A woman’s quartan fever ends after she prays twice

In October 1321 Lady Gemma Ronilgione of Terracina had six attacks of quartan fever. Fourteen years before she had had it for a year and a half, and it had made her so weak that she thought she would die. She feared that this fever would last as long. Humbly and devoutly she commended herself to Saint Thomas of Aquino and asked him to take the fever away. She had one more attack after that prayer. Then she commended herself again and said that she would tell everyone if he freed her. After that she had no more attacks.

She says that she has told it to more than a thousand people. Her son Ceccus swore that he heard her pray at the hour of the attack: “Blessed Thomas, you allow me to have the quartan fever again. Free me through the mercy of God.” She was also the woman who told Peter Carolus to commend himself to Saint Thomas, “who healed me before.”

Status: told under oath in 1321 by Gemma and by her son Ceccus Ronilgione.

Source: Fossanova canonization process, 1321, nos. 50–51, pp. 454–456; no. 105, p. 491, in Laurent, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 4, https://archive.org/details/fontesvitaesthom00pr.

A monk leads his sick kinsman to the tomb

Peter Nicholai of Sonnino had weak and swollen legs and feet for a year. He could hardly walk. One day he came to Fossanova in great pain to see his kinsman Brother Leo of Sonnino. Leo was a monk there. Peter complained of his weakness. Leo said: “Let us go to the tomb of blessed Thomas of Aquino, and commend yourself to him.”

They went. Peter knelt before the tomb and commended himself to Saint Thomas. He asked the saint to free him from the sickness. Then he lay down near the tomb. In that hour he felt better, and within four days he was completely healed. The swelling left his feet and legs, and he was as strong as before. This was about fourteen months before his oath.

Status: told under oath in 1321 by Peter himself and by his neighbor Bartholomew Mezettus. Brother Leo led him to the tomb. When Peter swore his oath, he said that Leo was in Sicily.

Source: Fossanova canonization process, 1321, nos. 47–48, pp. 452–453, in Laurent, Fontes vitae S. Thomae Aquinatis, fasc. 4, https://archive.org/details/fontesvitaesthom00pr.

A note on the sources. The bull and the sworn witnesses of 1319 and 1321 are the Church’s formal record. Most of the healings on this page come from those witnesses. Most of the visions and wonders of Saint Thomas’s own life come from the early Lives, written between about 1300 and 1323. Some stories are told in more than one way, and this page gives each telling with the name of its teller. The early Lives and the canonization documents read for this page do not give the saying about miracles and articles.
