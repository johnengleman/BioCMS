## SC-06 — A cardinal's castle at Molara
- Status: full (the year is an inference, see below)
- When and where: Molara, a castle of Cardinal Richard, "near Rome" [W-S1 Tocco p.96]. No source gives a day. Ptolemy says Thomas then kept a feast of St Agnes "that year at Naples", and "the next year he went to God" [W-S1 Calo p.45 n.5, quoting W-S3 XXIII.10]. This points to autumn 1272 on the road from Rome to Naples, or to Jan 1273 for the feast. It does not point to the 1260s as the outline says. Mark this as inference.
- Who was there: Ptolemy of Lucca (the narrator, who "came with him from Rome"); Thomas; Thomas's companion; Cardinal Richard's doctors.
- What happened:
  1. Thomas and Ptolemy came from Rome; Thomas turned aside to the castle [W-S3 XXIII.10].
  2. Thomas fell ill with a tertian fever (a fever that returns every second day) [same].
  3. His companion had a severe continuous fever. No signs of a turn showed, and the cardinal's doctors judged his case badly [same].
  4. Thomas took the relics of St Agnes that he carried from Rome, and sent them to the companion, with word to lay them on himself and trust fully [same].
  5. The companion did so and recovered, "beyond the hope of all the doctors" [same].
  6. Thomas then resolved to keep a solemn feast of Agnes each year with a good meal for the friars [same].
- Details: The companion is "Frater Raymundus" in Ptolemy, but "frater Raynaldus socius" in Gui [W-S1 Gui p.185-186]. The sources disagree; Gui probably means Reginald of Piperno. Tocco and Calo do not name the place or the companion. In their version Thomas goes to the sick man, urges patience, and himself lays the relics "on his chest", and the man sits up well and cheerful [W-S1 Tocco p.123-124; Calo p.45-46]. Ptolemy says Thomas sent them.
- Words spoken: none quoted. Tocco: Thomas told him to take special devotion to Agnes, "from whom he would surely hope for the gift of health" (paraphrase) [W-S1 Tocco p.124].
- Testimony (Ptolemy of Lucca, eyewitness, c.1317; Latin as quoted by Prümmer; public-domain):
  "Caeterum quod vidi de ipso ad judicium sanctitatis ejus tacere non debeo. Cum enim ego cum ipso venirem de Roma, declinavit Molariam ad castrum Domini Richardi cardinalis, ibique praefatus Doctor infirmatus est de tertiana. Socius vero suus Frater Raymundus de continua gravi (febri). Et cum non apparerent in eo signa critica, medici Domini Richardi male judicabant de ipso. Tunc venerabilis Doctor recepit reliquias B. Agnetis, quas ex devotione secum ferebat de Urbe et misit dicto Fratri, quod super se poneret et haberet fidem plenam in eis. Quod et fecit et curatus est ultra spem omnium medicorum; propter quam causam disposuerat singulis annis facere festum solemne de ipsa cum bona refectione fratrum, quod et illo anno fecit Neapoli. Sequenti vero transiit ad Deum."
  plain: "I must not keep silent about something I saw myself, which bears on his holiness. I was travelling with him from Rome. He turned aside to Molara, to the castle of Lord Richard the cardinal. There the Doctor fell ill with a tertian fever. His companion, Brother Raymund, had a severe continuous fever. No signs of a turning point showed in him, and Lord Richard's doctors gave a bad judgment on him. Then the Doctor took the relics of blessed Agnes, which he carried from Rome out of devotion, and sent them to the friar, to lay on himself and to trust fully in them. He did so and was cured beyond the hope of all the doctors. For that reason Thomas had resolved to keep a solemn feast of Agnes every year with a good meal for the friars, and that year he did so at Naples. The next year he went to God."
- What changed: a sick friend is cured, and Thomas begins a yearly feast of Agnes for his friars.
- Not in the sources: the season, weather, the room, the fever's length, any prayer words, Thomas's own feelings. Ptolemy gives no year. The outline's "1260s" has no support here. The "sending" (Ptolemy) and the visit (Tocco, Calo, Gui) are two versions; do not blend them without saying so.
- Sources: W-S3 XXIII.10 (through W-S1 Calo p.45 n.5); W-S1 Calo p.45-46; Tocco p.123-124; Gui p.185-186; Tocco p.96 (Molara "prope Romam", a different story).

## SC-07 — King Louis's table, Paris
- Status: full. Date: no source names a year; best inference 1269 to early 1270 (see "When").
- When and where: Paris, the king's table. Calo and Tocco say Thomas excused himself "because of the work of the Summa, which he was dictating at that time" [W-S1 Calo p.42; Tocco p.116]. The Summa of theology was begun at Santa Sabina in Rome and dated 1265-1274 [N-1]. Thomas taught again in Paris from the second regency, 1269-1272 [N-1]. King Louis died at Tunis on 25 Aug 1270 [N-2]. So the dinner falls in the second Paris stay, before Louis left in 1270. It does not fall in 1256-59. Gui gives a different reason: Thomas was then refuting the Manichaean heresy [W-S1 Gui p.191]. A later English life puts it "whilst engaged upon the Summa" [N-3, II p.424]. Inference only. Result for the chapter order: keep it in ch. 7 (second Paris). The outline's "move to ch. 5" condition is not met. Laurent's note dates Peckham's quarrel to 1270-72 [W-S2 p.374 n.b], which fits the same stay.
- Who was there: Louis IX; Thomas; the Dominican prior of Paris; the king's scribe; (Gui) Thomas's own companion.
- What happened:
  1. The king invited Thomas to table. Thomas excused himself for his work. The king pressed and the prior commanded [Calo p.42; Gui p.191].
  2. Thomas came "with the thought he had formed in his cell" [Calo p.42].
  3. Sitting beside the king, he suddenly struck the table and spoke [all three].
  4. The prior touched him and pulled hard at his cape to wake him [all three].
  5. Thomas came to himself, bowed, and asked pardon [all three].
  6. The king was moved, called his scribe, and had the thought written down before him [Calo p.42-43; Tocco p.117; Gui p.192].
- Details: the cape (cappa). Gui alone: Thomas called his companion by name, "Surge, scribe" ("Get up, scribe"), as if in his cell [Gui p.191].
- Words spoken: Thomas: "Modo conclusum est contra haeresim Manichaeorum." (Tocco p.116) = "Now the argument is settled against the Manichaean heresy." Prior: "Advertatis Magister, quia nunc estis in mensa Regis Franciae." = "Take note, Master: you are now at the table of the king of France." Thomas to the king (Gui p.192): "Parcatis mihi domine mi rex, quia credebam esse in studio" = "Forgive me, my lord king, for I thought I was in my study."
- Testimony (Calo, c.1300-1317; earliest of the three; public-domain):
  "Nam cum sanctus lodovicus rex francie semel invitasset eum ad mensam suam, et ipse se humiliter excusasset propter opus summe, quod tunc dictabat, instante rege et mandante priore parisiensi cum illa ymaginatione, quam manens in cella conceperat, accessit ad regem, iuxta quem existens in mensa, subito mensam percutit dicens : modo conclusum est contra heresim manichaei. Quem prior tetigit et dixit : Advertatis magister, quia nunc estis in mensa regis francie, et traxit eum per cappam fortiter, ut abstractum a sensibus excitaret. Qui ad se rediens, inclinans se ad sanctum regem, rogavit eum ut sibi parceret. De quo rex est plurimum admiratus et edificatus, et vocato notario suo voluit rex, quod coram se redigeretur in scripto."
  plain: "Once Saint Louis, king of France, invited him to his table. Thomas politely excused himself because of the Summa he was dictating then. The king insisted and the prior of Paris ordered it. Thomas came to the king with the thought he had formed in his cell. Sitting at table beside him, he suddenly struck the table and said: Now the argument is settled against the heresy of Mani. The prior touched him and said: Take note, Master, you are now at the table of the king of France. He pulled him hard by the cape to wake him from his trance. Thomas came to himself, bowed to the holy king and asked pardon. The king was greatly moved and built up by it. He called his scribe, and wanted the thought put into writing before him."
- What changed: a king sees his guest's mind at work and keeps what it found.
- Not in the sources: the year; the dishes, the room, the guests, the king's words; what the "argument" was; any laughter. All three Lives say "it is said" ("dicitur", "fertur"); no witness is named.
- Sources: W-S1 Calo p.42-43; Tocco p.116-117; Gui p.191-192; N-1; N-2; N-3.

## SC-08 — The crucifix, St Nicholas chapel, Naples
- Status: full as tradition. It is late and only in the three Lives.
- When and where: the chapel of St Nicholas in the Dominican house at Naples. No date is given. All three place it while he wrote the third part of the Summa on Christ's passion and resurrection (Gui adds "incarnation, nativity") [Calo p.38; Tocco p.108; Gui p.189]. That is Naples 1272-73 by the outline's dates; the sources give no more.
- Who was there: the sacristan, who watched; Thomas. The sacristan is Brother Dominic of Caserta in Tocco and in Gui ("a convert, an old man of holy life"), and Brother James of Caserta in Calo.
- What happened:
  1. The sacristan saw that Thomas always came down from his study to the church before Matins, and went back quickly after the bell so that others would not see him [Calo p.38; Tocco p.108].
  2. Once he watched more closely, went in behind into the chapel of St Nicholas, and found Thomas fixed in prayer [same].
  3. He saw him raised about two cubits in the air [same].
  4. He heard a voice come from the image of the crucified, toward which Thomas turned in tears [same].
  5. Thomas answered.
  6. After this he wrote little (Calo, Gui). Tocco and Gui read it as a sign that his work was ending, since the Lord had asked him about his reward [Tocco p.108; Gui p.189].
- Details: Tocco and Gui say the sacristan was "devout in prayer, careful in action, proved in virtue" [Tocco p.108]. Gui says the voice came "more clearly".
- Words spoken: Voice: "Thoma bene scripsisti de me. Quam ergo recipies a me mercedem de tuo labore?" = "Thomas, you have written well of me. What reward will you take from me for your work?" Thomas: "Domine, non nisi te." = "Lord, nothing but you." (Gui: "Domine non aliam mercedem recipiam, nisi teipsum" = "Lord, I will take no other reward than yourself.") [Calo p.38; Tocco p.108; Gui p.189].
- Testimony (Calo, c.1300-1317; public-domain):
  "Frater iacobus de caserta sacrista, vir oracione devotus, actione sollicitus et virtute probatus, qui alias habuit vigilans mirabiles visiones, advertens beatum thomam semper de camera sui studii ad ecclesiam ante matutinas descendere, et pulsato ad matutinas, ne videretur ab aliis festinato reddiit, semel ipsum curiosius observavit et accedens retro in capella sancti nicholai, ubi fixus in oracione manebat, vidit ipsum quasi duobus cubitis elevatum in aere. De quo diu admirans subito audivit de loco, ad quem predictus doctor conversus erat cum lacrimis, huiuscemodi vocem de ymagine crucifixi : Thoma bene scripsisti de me. Quam ergo recipies a me mercedem de tuo labore? Qui respondit : Domine, nonnisi te."
  plain: "Brother James of Caserta, the sacristan, a man devoted in prayer, careful in his work and proved in virtue, who at other times had wonderful visions while awake, saw that blessed Thomas always came down from his study to the church before Matins, and went back quickly after the bell, so that others would not see him. Once he watched him more closely. He went in behind, into the chapel of Saint Nicholas, where Thomas stayed fixed in prayer, and he saw him raised about two cubits in the air. He wondered at this for a long time. Then he suddenly heard, from the place toward which Thomas had turned in tears, a voice from the image of the crucified: Thomas, you have written well of me. What reward will you take from me for your work? Thomas answered: Lord, nothing but you."
- What changed: Thomas names his only wish; the Lives read it as the start of the end of his writing.
- Not in the sources: the date; the hour; Thomas's age or health; who else heard; how long it lasted; what the image looked like. No 1319 witness tells it [W §3]. The sacristan's name differs (James or Dominic). Tell it as tradition.
- Sources: W-S1 Calo p.38; Tocco p.108; Gui p.189.

## SC-09 — After Mass, St Nicholas chapel, Naples, December 1273
- Status: full
- When and where: Naples, the chapel of St Nicholas. "About the feast of Blessed Nicholas" is Reginald's own phrase (6 Dec is our reading): "A festo beati Nicolai circa fuit in isto statu" = "From about the feast of Blessed Nicholas he has been in this state" [W-S2 p.377]. It was before the journey to Lyon in early 1274.
- Who was there: Thomas; his companion Reginald of Piperno; at the sister's house, the Countess of San Severino.
- What happened:
  1. Thomas said Mass and was "moved by a wonderful change" [W-S2 p.376].
  2. After that Mass he never wrote or dictated again. He "hung up the tools of writing" in Part III, in the treatise on Penance [same]. (Laurent's note: Part III runs to question 90, article 4 [W-S2 p.376 n.a], French, facts only.)
  3. Reginald asked why he left so great a work. Thomas said "I cannot", and then the straw sentence [p.376-377].
  4. Fearing too much study had unsettled him, Reginald kept pressing [p.377].
  5. Thomas rode to his sister, the Countess of San Severino, "hardly speaking" to her; she asked Reginald why [p.377].
  6. Pressed again, he made Reginald swear by the living God to tell no one in his lifetime, then said the straw sentence in full [p.377].
  7. He went back to Naples, then set out for the council "writing nothing at all" [p.377].
- Words spoken: Thomas: "Omnia que scripsi videntur michi palee respectu eorum que vidi et revelata sunt michi." = "All that I have written seems to me like straw (chaff) beside what I have seen and what has been shown to me." [W-S2 p.377]. Tocco and Gui put the scene at San Severino, with a trance of nearly three days; there he says: "Venit finis scripture mee, quia talia sunt mihi revelata, quod ea quae scripsi et docui, modica mihi videntur" = "The end of my writing has come, because such things have been shown to me that what I wrote and taught seems small to me" [Tocco p.120; Gui p.194]. These two versions differ; Tocco and Gui do not use the word "straw".
- Inner life: only that he swore Reginald to silence and saw things "revealed" to him. No source says what he saw.
- Chain of the story (Bartholomew's hearsay): Reginald told it in his last illness to old Brother John del Giudice of Anagni; John told Bartholomew at Anagni just before Boniface VIII was seized in Sept 1303; Bartholomew told Tocco and others, then Pope Benedict XI [W-S2 p.377-378, with Laurent's notes, French]. The manuscript has a gap ("stupefactus quod ...") at one point [p.377].
- Testimony (Bartholomew of Capua, Naples process 1319, art. LXXIX, W-S2 p.376-377; public-domain; the full article is longer, copied here to the first refusal):
  "Item dixit idem testis quod, cum dictus frater Thomas celebraret missam in dicta capella sancti Nicolai Neapoli, fuit mira mutatione commotus, et post ipsam missam nunquam scripsit neque dictavit aliquid, immo suspendit organa scriptionis in tertia parte Summe, in tractatu de Penitentia. Et dum idem frater Raynaldus videret quod ipse frater Thomas cessaverat scribere dixit ei : « Pater, quomodo dimisistis opus tam grande quod ad laudem Dei et illuminationem mundi cepisti ? » Cui respondit dictus frater Thomas : « Raynalde, non possum. » ... et similiter ipse frater Thomas respondit : « Raynalde, non possum quia omnia que scripsi videntur mihi palee »."
  plain: "The same witness said that when Brother Thomas said Mass in the said chapel of St Nicholas at Naples, he was moved by a wonderful change. After that Mass he never wrote or dictated anything. He hung up the tools of writing in the third part of the Summa, in the treatise on Penance. When Brother Reginald saw that Thomas had stopped writing, he said: Father, how can you leave so great a work, which you began for the praise of God and the enlightenment of the world? Brother Thomas answered: Reginald, I cannot. And again he answered: Reginald, I cannot, because everything I have written seems to me like straw."
- What changed: he never writes again; the work stops at the treatise on Penance.
- Not in the sources: what the "change" was; what he saw; any date; Thomas's face, voice, or tears; the day of the week; the weather. "Palee" can mean straw or chaff. The word is only in Bartholomew's hearsay.
- Sources: W-S2 n.79 p.376-378; W-S1 Tocco p.120; Gui p.194.

## SC-10 — The road near Teano, early 1274
- Status: full
- When and where: on the road from Naples toward the council at Lyon, "going down from the city of Teano by the Borgonuovo road" [W-S2 p.375]. Gregory X summoned him; he carried his treatise against the Greeks [W-S1 Tocco p.129]. He died at Fossanova on 7 March 1274 [Tocco p.138]. No exact day is given.
- Who was there: Thomas; Reginald of Piperno ("companion, constant, familiar"); Lord William, then dean of Teano (later bishop); Abbot Roffrid, William's nephew (later dean). Roffrid later told Bartholomew what "he saw and heard" [p.376].
- What happened:
  1. Thomas hit his head on a tree that had fallen across the road. He was almost stunned [p.375].
  2. Reginald, William, and Roffrid ran to him. Reginald asked if he was hurt. "A little" [p.375].
  3. To lift his mind, Reginald said much good would come from the council for the Church, the order, and the Kingdom of Sicily [p.375].
  4. He added that Thomas and Brother Bonaventure would be cardinals and would raise up their orders [p.375].
  5. Thomas answered that in no state could he serve the order better than in his present one. Reginald said he spoke for the common good, not for Thomas. Thomas cut in and said he would never change his state [p.375-376].
- Details: before this, says Bartholomew from John of Caiazzo (a close friend), Thomas had prayed that he would never love earthly things and never change his state. The answer was that he never would [p.374-375].
- Words spoken (plain English; Latin in the Testimony): Reginald: "Sir, you go to the council, and many good things will happen there for the whole Church, our order and the kingdom of Sicily." Thomas: "God grant good things happen there." Reginald: "You and Brother Bonaventure will be cardinals and raise up your orders." Thomas: "Reginald, in no state can I be as useful to our order as in this one." Reginald: "Father, I do not say this for you, but for the common good." Thomas: "Reginald, be sure that I will never change my state."
- Testimony (Bartholomew of Capua, 1319, art. LXXVIII, W-S2 p.375-376; public-domain; gap marked):
  "descendens de civitate Theani per viam Burgi Novi percussit caput in quadam arbore que ceciderat per transversum ita quod fere stupefactus quodammodo fuit circa casum ; cui statim occurrit frater Raynaldus de Piperno, socius et continuus et familiaris ... Tunc idem frater Raynaldus processit ultra et dixit : « Vos et frater Bonaventura eritis cardinales et exaltabitis ordines vestros ». Et tunc respondit idem frater Thomas : « Raynalde, in nullo statu possum esse ita utilis ordini nostro, sicut in isto ». Tunc replicavit idem frater Raynaldus : « Pater, non dico hoc pro vobis, sed pro bono communi ». Statim idem frater Thomas intercidit verbum et respondit : « Raynalde, sis securus quod ego nunquam in perpetuum mutabo statum »."
  plain: "Going down from the city of Teano by the Borgonuovo road, he struck his head on a tree that had fallen across the road, and was almost stunned by the fall. Brother Reginald of Piperno, his companion, constant and familiar, ran to him at once ... Then Reginald went further and said: You and Brother Bonaventure will be cardinals and will raise up your orders. And Thomas answered: Reginald, in no state can I be as useful to our order as in this one. Reginald replied: Father, I do not say this for you, but for the common good. Thomas at once cut him off and answered: Reginald, be sure that I will never change my state."
- What changed: his last refusal of rank. He goes on and falls ill at Maenza, the castle of his niece Francesca [Tocco p.129; W-S2 n.8 p.276].
- Not in the sources: that he rode (it says only that he hit his head going down the road; mount or foot is not given); the hour; the weather; how hard the blow was beyond "a little"; whether the injury lasted; what Reginald or Thomas felt. "Cardinals" is Reginald's word; no source says an offer was made.
- Sources: W-S2 n.78 p.374-376; W-S1 Tocco p.129, 138; Calo p.47.
