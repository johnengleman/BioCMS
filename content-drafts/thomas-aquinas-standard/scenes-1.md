# Thomas Aquinas — scene cards, group 1 (SC-01 to SC-05)

Method. Latin was read in this session in the Fontes OCR (archive.org: prummerfontesvitaestho = fasc. 1, Prümmer, Toulouse 1911; fontesvitaesthom00pr = fasc. 4, Laurent). Latin quotations are exact copies except that line-break hyphens are joined and obvious OCR letter errors are fixed. Where the printed text looks wrong, a note says so. Plain English lines are our own translation from the Latin. All Latin quoted is public-domain. Page numbers are the printed Fontes pages. Keys follow outline.md (W-S1 Prümmer fasc. 1: Calo, Tocco, Gui; W-S2 Laurent fasc. 4: Naples process 1319).

## SC-01 — A storm over the family castle, c.1225 (Roccasecca)
- Status: full (no words of Theodora exist; see below)
- When and where: undated, "at the start of his life" (in suae vitae principio). The castle at Roccasecca (Tocco: "in dicto castro"). Tower, stable, and the bed of the nurse and child.
- Who was there: Thomas as a baby; his nurse (nutrix); his mother Theodora; his baby sister, asleep in the tower. Count Landulf is not named in the scene.
- What happened, in order:
  1. A sudden dreadful storm rose in the sky. [Tocco p.67]
  2. Lightning struck the tower. It killed the boy's sister, who slept in the tower, and the horses in the stable. [Tocco p.67]
  3. His mother, who worried about the boy more than about the daughter, came trembling to the bed where the nurse slept with him. [Tocco p.67]
  4. She found him unhurt with the nurse and gave thanks to God. [Tocco p.67]
- Details: only what step 1 to 4 give. Tocco's frame: God "protected his bodily life" so that the child could be kept for the mind (Latin: "corporalis vitae custodia"). Other infant tales (Ave Maria paper, with the hermit's prophecy) are left out by the outline.
- Inner life: none in the passage. Tocco's moral is his own: "Deo gratias reddidit" is the mother's act, not the child's.
- Testimony (Tocco, Vita S. Thomae, c.1318-23, p.67; public-domain):
  "Nam in dicto castro, cum subito horrenda tempestas fuisset exorta in aére, fulgur turrim exterruit, et sororem dicti pueri, quae dormiebat in turri, et equos, qui erant in stabulo, interemit. Mater vero, quae de puero magis quam de filia erat sollicita, ad lectum, in quo nutrix cum puero dormiebat, tremebunda pervenit, quae sanum ipsum cum nutrice inveniens, Deo gratias reddidit."
  plain: In that castle a terrible storm suddenly rose. Lightning struck the tower and killed the boy's sister, who was sleeping in the tower, and the horses in the stable. His mother was more anxious about the boy than about her daughter. She came trembling to the bed where the nurse slept with him, found him unhurt with the nurse, and gave thanks to God.
  (The printed text has "exterruit (exc'ruit)": the editor's note gives the manuscript reading as a doubtful verb. "Struck" is the sense. Say "struck" and nothing more exact.)
- Source of the story: Tocco says of the years up to age five that all of it "ex fideli narratione matris eius est habitum" (was had from his mother's faithful telling), p.69. So the mother is Tocco's source for the childhood, but Tocco writes it down about 90 years after the storm. [Tocco p.69]
- What changed: the child's life is spared; Tocco then sends him to Monte Cassino at five.
- Bridge to the family's plans (chapter's end): at five his parents sent him to Monte Cassino "ut sanctis instrueretur moribus"; Tocco calls him "non verbis garrulus ... tacitus" and says he often kept away from the noble boys, holding a reading sheet [Tocco p.69]. Plain: not talkative, he kept to himself and prayed. The abbot then advised Landulf to send him to Naples to study; under masters Martin (grammar, logic) and Peter of Ireland (natural science) [Tocco p.69-70]. Then he took the habit through John of San Giuliano [Tocco p.71].
- Father's plan: Bartholomew of Capua, sworn 1319, hearsay ("ex quodam communi preeloquio", a common saying): Thomas's father, "origine nobilis atque potens, monachavit dictum fratrem Thomam puerum disponens eum abbatie preficere Cassinensi." Plain: his father, noble and powerful by birth, made the boy a monk, meaning to put him over the abbey of Monte Cassino. [W-S2 n.76 p.371]. Prümmer's note says Tocco's own words ("per transitum ad aliquem Ordinem") show Thomas was not yet a monk, so "monachavit" is contested [Tocco p.71 n.1].
- Not in the sources: any words of Theodora in the storm; the weather's hour or season; the sister's name or age beyond "infant"; Thomas's own reaction; Landulf in the room.
- Sources: W-S1 Tocco p.67-71 (read in full); W-S2 n.76 p.371.

## SC-02 — By a spring on the road, c.1243 (Tuscany)
- Status: full
- When and where: the year is not settled. Prümmer notes the emperor's army was in Tuscany in 1240 and in 1243 and does not choose [Gui p.171 n.4]. The friars complained to Pope Innocent IV, who was elected in June 1243, so 1243 or after fits [Calo p.22 n.1]. Place: by a spring (Tocco and Gui "fons") or a well (Calo "puteum") on a road in Tuscany, after Rome. The brothers' camp was at Acquapendente (Tocco p.72; Gui p.172).
- Who was there: Thomas; four friars of his order; his brothers (soldiers of Emperor Frederick II; Tocco does not name them); the mother's messenger. In Ptolemy (notes only) it is Rainald, with Peter della Vigna [W-S3 XXII.20, notes].
- What happened, in order:
  1. Friars heard that Theodora was coming to Naples; they moved Thomas via Terracina and Anagni to Rome (Santa Sabina) so she would not find him; they planned to send him on to Paris. [Tocco p.72]
  2. Theodora, who could not see him, sent a messenger to her sons with the emperor at Acquapendente, "under the cover of a mother's blessing". [Tocco p.72-73; Gui p.172]
  3. The sons told the emperor and got leave; they posted guards on the roads. [Tocco p.73]
  4. They found Thomas resting by a spring with four friars. [Tocco p.73]
  5. They fell on him "not as brothers but as enemies". They could not pull off the habit he held tightly. [Tocco p.73]
  6. They let the friars go and sent him to his mother. She had him kept at Monte San Giovanni and Roccasecca until her sons came back. [Tocco p.73]
- Details: Gui adds: he rested "propter laborem itineris" (from the toil of the road); they tried to strip him "per violentiam" but gave up, "ne puer ... in corpore lederetur" (lest the boy be hurt as he fought) [Gui p.172]. Calo: "lassum ex itinere quiescentem" (tired from the road) and "vociferans et lacrimas uberrimas fundens vestem illam innocencie conservabat" (shouting and weeping heavily, he held on to that garment of innocence) [Calo p.21-22].
- Words spoken: only reported. Calo gives the mother's message as speech (he wrote c.1300, no witness): "Succurrite filii, filium meum rapite matri" = "Help, sons: take my son and bring him to his mother." [Calo p.21]. Tocco gives no speech.
- Testimony (Tocco p.73; public-domain): "Et contigit eos praedictum fratrem suum juxta cujusdam fontis irrigua quiescentem cum quatuor Fratribus dicti Ordinis invenire : in quem cum non ut fratres, sed ut hostes irruerent, non valentes ipsum exuere praedicti Ordinis habitum, quem fortiter tenebat assumptum, ... ipsum sic indutum matri suae sub bona custodia transmiserunt."
  plain: They happened to find their brother resting beside a flowing spring with four friars of his order. They rushed at him not as brothers but as enemies. They could not strip off the habit of his order, which he held firmly, so they sent him, still dressed in it, to their mother under guard.
- What changed: he goes from the road into his family's custody; he still wears the habit.
- Not in the sources: the season, the hour, a weapon, Thomas's age on the road, a fight with the four friars, the brothers' names or ranks, words of Thomas.
- Sources: W-S1 Tocco p.71-73; Gui p.171-172 and notes; Calo p.21-22 and notes. Ptolemy only via the notes.

## SC-03 — The locked room, c.1244-45 (Monte San Giovanni)
- Status: full (length of captivity is disputed; see below)
- When and where: "in camera", the room in which he slept under guard, in the castle of Monte San Giovanni (a later move to Roccasecca is Tocco's). Length: Tocco "fere per duos annos" (almost two years) [Tocco p.76-77]; Cantimpré "two or three years"; Calo's editor argues Innocent IV's election (June 1243) means the captivity was already long when the friars complained [Calo p.22 n.1].
- Who was there: Thomas alone; the brothers who sent the girl; two angels (in the vision); later his companion (socius), who first heard the story. Tocco also says the parents sent one of his sisters to talk him round; she was won over instead [Tocco p.74].
- What happened, in order:
  1. The brothers tore his habit into rags so that shame would make him put on another. He bore it and wrapped himself in the shreds. [Tocco p.74]
  2. They sent into his room "puellam pulcherrimam" dressed as a prostitute. [Tocco p.75]
  3. He felt desire rise, took a burning stick from the fire, and drove her out. [Tocco p.75; W-S2 n.61 p.349]
  4. He burned a cross on the wall with the end of the stick, fell to the floor, wept, and prayed God for the "belt" of lasting chastity. [Tocco p.75]
  5. He fell asleep. Two angels came, bound him tightly at the waist, and said they girded him on God's behalf. [Tocco p.75]
  6. In the pain of the girding he woke with a cry. Asked why, he would not say; he told his companion near the end of his life. [Tocco p.75-76; W-S2 n.61]
- Details: In the same captivity Tocco says he read the whole Bible, learned the Sentences, compiled (it is said) a treatise on Aristotle's fallacies, and taught his sisters; one sister he led to religious life, later abbess of Santa Maria in Capua [Tocco p.74]. Calo's earlier version has no stick or angels in the passage I read; it has the brothers sending sisters ("miserunt sorores") who he converted, and the Bible read in the cell [Calo p.22-23]. Cantimpré (c.1260) has the women shut in his cell for a time, and Thomas "fortius quam prius, spretis illecebris" (stronger than before, scorning the lures) enduring "two or three years"; he mentions neither stick nor angels [as quoted by Prümmer, Calo p.22 n.1].
- Words spoken: the angels, Latin and plain English. Tocco p.75: "Ecce ex parte Dei te cingimus, quod petivisti, cingulo castitatis, quod nulla possit de cetero impugnatione dissolvi" = "See, on God's behalf we gird you with the belt of chastity you asked for. No attack can undo it from now on." The 1319 version (below) is shorter: "Ecce ex parte Dei cingimus te cingulo castitatis quod nulla possit temptatione dissolvi."
- Inner life: Tocco's frame: he prayed for the gift "cum lacrymis" [Tocco p.75]. His own words: none; the gift he told only his companion, who told others. Tocco says that from then on he always avoided talk with women [Tocco p.75].
- Testimony: William of Tocco, sworn in Naples, 4 August (year 1319 in the notes), p.349. Rights: public-domain Latin. Note: this is Tocco reporting what Robert of Sezze and his uncle Stephen told him at Anagni in December 1318, so it is hearsay at two removes, and Tocco's printed Latin spells "strinserunt" and "cingimus" (OCR or manuscript spelling, left as printed).
  "volentes ipsum omnino extrahere, miserunt ad ipsum iuvenculam pulcherrimam et ornatam que ipsum induceret ad peccandum : quam cum ipse frater Thomas vidisset et sensisset in se carnis stimulum excitari, assumpto de camino ardente titione, ipsam cum indignatione expulit, et accedens ad angulum camere signum crucis cum sumitate titionis in pariete impressit, et diu orans cum lacrimis petivit a Deo ut nunquam sentiret carnis stimulum, quod eius integritatem mentis et corporis violaret. Et sic orans obdormivit, et ecce ad ipsum in visione missi fuerunt duo angeli, qui ei revelaverunt a Deo esse exauditum, et de pugna, quam habuit, habuisse triumphum : et hinc inde strinserunt ipsum in renibus et dixerunt « Ecce ex parte Dei cingimus te cingulo castitatis quod nulla possit temptatione dissolvi ». Qui in illa strictura sensibilem sentiens dolorem clamavit et excitatus est, quod excitatus noluit revelare ad quid clamasset."
  plain: Wanting to drag him out of the order, they sent a very beautiful young woman, dressed up, to lead him into sin. When Brother Thomas saw her and felt the sting of the flesh rise in him, he took a burning stick from the fire and drove her out in anger. He went to a corner of the room and, with the end of the stick, burned the sign of the cross on the wall. For a long time he prayed with tears and asked God never to feel any urge of the flesh that would harm the purity of his mind and body. Praying like this, he fell asleep, and two angels were sent to him in a vision. They told him God had heard him and that he had won the fight. They tightened a cord around his waist on both sides and said: "On God's behalf we gird you with the belt of chastity, which no temptation can undo." In that tight grip he felt real pain and cried out as he woke. Awake, he would not say why he had cried out.
- What changed: the family cannot break him by force, shame, or temptation; the friars later receive him.
- Not in the sources: the girl's name or what she said; the room's size or furniture; whether it was night; what Thomas said aloud; who heard the cry (Tocco: "quaerentibus", people asked, no one named); the length of his stay in this room.
- Escape (ch. end): Theodora, "dissimulata prudenter custodia", allowed him to be lowered "de fenestra castri per funem" (from the castle window by a rope) to friars waiting below, who took him to Naples [Tocco p.77]. Tocco does not say that it was night; the outline's "night" is not in this passage. Later he is sent to Rome, then Paris and Cologne [Tocco p.77].
- Sources: W-S1 Tocco p.73-77, Calo p.22-23 and n.1; W-S2 n.61 p.349. Cantimpré only through Prümmer's quotation (Bonum universale de apibus, lib.1; my own look at the 1627 OCR was unreadable).

## SC-04 — Albert's classroom, Cologne, c.1248
- Status: full
- When and where: at Cologne in Albert the Great's school of the Dominicans, in "scholis" (classroom) and in Thomas's cell (the page of notes). The year 1248 is the outline's; the passages give no date.
- Who was there: Thomas; Albert (called Brother Albert, master of theology); a fellow student who offered help; "magister studentium" (master of students). Tocco says Thomas was silent and the friars called him the "dumb ox".
- What happened, in order (Tocco p.77-79; Calo p.25-27):
  1. Thomas arrived at Cologne, was glad to find a teacher "who could explain the depths of knowledge", and became silent, attentive, and prayerful. [Tocco p.77; Calo p.25]
  2. The friars named him "bovem mutum" (dumb ox). [Tocco p.78; Calo p.26]
  3. Albert began lecturing on Pseudo-Dionysius, Divine Names. A fellow student, thinking him slow, offered to go over the lecture with him. Thomas thanked him and accepted. [Tocco p.78]
  4. The student failed in the repeating; Thomas "as if he had now been given leave to speak" repeated the lecture clearly and added what Albert had not said. [Tocco p.78]
  5. The student told the master of students; Albert heard of it. In Tocco a notebook page is found before his cell; in Calo the page falls from him by the door. Albert reads it and sees a gift. [Tocco p.78; Calo p.26]
  6. Albert had him answer a hard question next day. Thomas prayed first, "se Deo humiliter recommendans", then answered with a distinction. [Tocco p.79]
  7. Albert tested him with four hard arguments and Thomas answered them. [Tocco p.79; Calo p.26-27]
- Words spoken (Tocco p.79; Calo p.26-27 nearly the same): Albert: "Frater Thoma, non videris tenere locum respondentis, sed determinantis" = "Brother Thomas, you do not seem to hold the place of one who answers, but of one who decides." Thomas: "Magister, non video quomodo possim ad quaestionem aliter respondere" = "Master, I do not see how I can answer the question any other way." Albert: "Modo respondeas ad questionem per tuam distinctionem" = "Now answer the question by your own distinction."
- Details: Calo says the school rule was five years' silence before students could argue knowledge (Pythagoras and Hippocrates, "ante quinquennium non auderent de scientia concertare"); that rule explains his silence in Calo's account [Calo p.26]. Tocco gives Thomas's reason for silence: so he could "gather learning faster, with no outside talk to hinder it" [Tocco p.78]. After the test he did not grow proud [Tocco p.79]. He later wrote up Albert's lectures on the Ethics [Tocco p.79].
- Inner life: only Tocco's frame: he prayed before the test, and he made his student promise to tell no one, so he could stay hidden in his simplicity [Tocco p.78].
- Testimony (Tocco p.79; public-domain; "fertur" = "it is said"; written c.1318): "Unde cum repetitis argumentis Magistri praemisisset quandam distinctionem per quam ad questionem et argumenta sufficientissime respondebat, praedictus Magister ei dixit: Frater Thoma, non videris tenere locum respondentis, sed determinantis. Cui cum omni reverentia respondit : Magister, non video quomodo possim ad quaestionem aliter respondere. Tunc Magister dixit : Modo respondeas ad questionem per tuam distinctionem; et fecit ei quatuor argumenta tam difficilia, quod omnino se ei crederet conclusisse. Ad quae cum Frater Thomas sufficientissime respondisset, fertur Magistrum Albertum dixisse per spiritum prophetiae : Nos vocamus istum bovem mutum; sed ipse adhuc talem dabit in doctrina mugitum, quod in toto mundo sonabit."
  plain: After he repeated the master's arguments, Thomas put forward a distinction that answered the question and the arguments fully. The master said to him: "Brother Thomas, you do not seem to be answering but deciding." He replied with great respect: "Master, I do not see how I can answer the question another way." Albert said: "Then answer the question by your own distinction." Albert put four arguments to him, so hard that he thought he had trapped him. Thomas answered them completely. It is said that Albert then said, speaking as if by the spirit of prophecy: "We call him the dumb ox, but he will one day bellow in his teaching so that it will sound through the whole world."
- What changed: the hidden gift is known; Albert gives him the hard disputations from then on [Tocco p.79; Calo p.27].
- Not in the sources: the year, the room's look, the number of students, who wrote the page's subject, Albert's tone, the students' laughter, a "bellow" meant as a joke. Both authors wrote about 70 years later and tell it as "it is said" (fertur).
- Sources: W-S1 Tocco p.77-79; Calo p.25-27.

## SC-05 — The night before the mastership, Paris, c.1256
- Status: full
- When and where: Paris, in the Dominican house; at "the place of prayer he used" (Tocco p.85) or "near a certain altar" (Peter, below). The passages give no year; Gui's date for the mastership is flagged as wrong by Prümmer in the notes.
- Who was there: Thomas; the Chancellor of Paris (gave the order); the prior of Paris (passed it on); an old friar (in a dream or vision). Thomas told the story in 1274 at Maenza to the prior of Fossanova, who asked it at Reginald's urging; Peter of Monte San Giovanni was there [W-S2 n.49 p.330-331].
- What happened, in order:
  1. Near the time when bachelors of theology were presented to the Chancellor, the Chancellor told the prior to order Thomas to prepare to take the mastership. This broke the usual order of seniority. [Tocco p.84-85]
  2. Thomas excused himself for lack of knowledge and age, but obedience forced him. [Tocco p.85]
  3. He went to his place of prayer and prayed with tears for knowledge and grace. He began Psalm 11 (Vulgate numbering): "Salvum me fac Deus, quoniam diminutae sunt veritates a filiis hominum". [Tocco p.85]
  4. He fell asleep. A venerable old friar of the order appeared and asked why he wept. [Tocco p.85]
  5. Thomas said the burden was too heavy and he had no idea what to preach. [Tocco p.85]
  6. The old man said he was heard, and told him to say only: "Rigans montes de superioribus suis: de fructu operum tuorum satiabitur terra" (Psalm 103:13: "You water the hills from your high rooms; the earth is filled with the fruit of your works"). [Tocco p.85]
  7. He woke and thanked God. [Tocco p.85]
- Words spoken (Tocco p.85): "Fr. Thoma, quid Deum oras cum lacrymis?" = "Brother Thomas, why do you weep as you pray to God?" "Quia impositum est mihi onus Magisterii, ad quod scientia mea non sufficit : et quid proponam pro meo principio, non occurrit." = "Because the burden of the mastership is put on me, and my knowledge is not enough for it, and I cannot think what to put forward for my opening lecture." "Ecce exauditus es, suscipe onus Magisterii, quia Deus tecum est" = "See, you are heard. Take up the burden of the mastership, because God is with you."
- Details: Gerard de Frachet, Vitae Fratrum IV, written before 1260 while Thomas lived, tells a plainer version (as quoted by Prümmer, Tocco p.85 n.1): the night before, Thomas sees in a dream someone give him a book and say the verse; he took it for his opening theme. Paraphrase: no old friar, no tears, no psalm. Peter's 1319 version has no tears or psalm either: he "gave himself to prayer at a certain altar", fell asleep, and saw "a Preaching friar, gray and old". Ptolemy's hearsay (n.60, p.348) says "a certain old friar of the hood" with the same verse. Tocco's printed text has "sleep" in some manuscripts and "he stayed praying" in the Florence manuscript (Tocco p.85 n.1).
- Inner life: from Peter's witness only: he felt unfit "propter iuventutem" (because of youth), was in perplexity "quid ageret" (what to do), and gave himself to prayer. Tocco's gloss: the verse was not only his theme but a sign of "totius sui studii sufficientiam" [Tocco p.85].
- Testimony: Peter of Monte San Giovanni, monk of Fossanova, sworn 1319, eyewitness to this telling at Maenza (the castle of Thomas's niece), early 1274. Process of Naples n.49, p.331; public-domain Latin. The printed text reads "recipiendum conventum" where "magisterium" would be expected; copied as printed.
  "ipse frater Thomas ad magnam instantiam dicti prioris narravit sibi quod cum iniunctum fuisset sibi per priorem suum Parisiensem de mandato cancellarii Parisiensis ut pararet se ad recipiendum magisterium in theologia, et ipse reputaret se inhabilem ad hoc propter iuventutem, et positus in perplexitate quid ageret, dedit se in oratione circa quoddam altare ubi sibi obdormienti apparuit in sompnis quidam frater Predicator, canus et antiquus, qui dixit sibi : Quid cogitas, frater Thoma ? Et ipse respondit : Mandatum est mihi ut preparem me ad recipiendum conventum et cogito quid debeam proponere. Et ille dixit ei : Hoc verbum proponas et non aliud : Rigans montes de superioribus suis de fructu operum tuorum satiabitur terra. Et hanc auctoritatem dixit se proposuisse in dicto suo conventu, et illam fuisse prosecutum."
  plain: At the prior's strong urging, Brother Thomas told him this. He had been ordered by his prior in Paris, on the Chancellor's command, to prepare to take the mastership in theology. He thought himself unfit because of his youth and was at a loss. He gave himself to prayer beside an altar, and as he fell asleep a Preaching friar appeared to him in a dream, gray and old. The friar said: "What are you thinking, Brother Thomas?" He answered: "I have been ordered to prepare to receive the mastership, and I am wondering what I should speak on." The old man said: "Speak this word and no other: 'You water the hills from your high rooms; the earth will be filled with the fruit of your works.'" Thomas said he spoke on that text in his lecture and followed it through.
- What changed: he accepts the mastership and starts to teach, with Psalm 103 as his text.
- Not in the sources: the old friar's name (Tocco and Peter give none); the year; the hour; the Chancellor's name; Thomas's age (only "youth"); what lecture hall he used; the old man's fate beyond "disappeared" (Calo p.20 "Quo dicto disparuit"). Outline note: the "prior" who heard it is James of Florence ("frater Jacobus de Florentino", as printed), prior of Fossanova; he is not a deponent in this passage. The sworn witness is Peter.
- Sources: W-S1 Tocco p.84-86, Calo p.19-20; W-S2 n.49 p.330-332 (read in full), n.60 p.348-349; Gerard through Prümmer's note (Tocco p.85 n.1).
