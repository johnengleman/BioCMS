## Quotations - VERIFIED against raw Leonine Latin and Dominican Fathers English (more appended below)
Q1. ST I q.1 a.1 co. "necessarium fuit ad humanam salutem, esse doctrinam quandam secundum revelationem divinam, praeter philosophicas disciplinas, quae ratione humana investigantur." Own English: It was necessary for human salvation that there be a teaching from divine revelation, besides the philosophical disciplines that reason investigates. [S1 sth1001; S2 1001 a.1] public-domain.
Q2. ST I q.1 a.5 ad 1 "minimum quod potest haberi de cognitione rerum altissimarum, desiderabilius est quam certissima cognitio quae habetur de minimis rebus" Own English: the least that can be known of the highest things is more to be desired than the most certain knowledge of the smallest things. (He cites Aristotle, De animalibus.) Same reply: doubt in matters of faith comes "propter debilitatem intellectus humani", as the owl's eye in the sun (Dominican: "owl is dazzled by the light of the sun"). [S1][S2 1001 a.5 ad 1]
Q3. ST I q.1 a.8 ad 2 "Cum enim gratia non tollat naturam, sed perficiat, oportet quod naturalis ratio subserviat fidei" Own English: Since grace does not destroy nature but perfects it, natural reason must serve faith. [S1][S2 1001 a.8 ad 2] (This is the real source of the popular "grace perfects nature". The short form "gratia non tollit naturam sed perficit" is a common condensation; he writes "non tollat ... sed perficiat".)
Q4. ST I q.1 a.5 ad 2 philosophy as servant: "utitur eis tanquam inferioribus et ancillis" (uses them as lower things and handmaids); "non propter defectum vel insufficientiam eius, sed propter defectum intellectus nostri". [S1 sth1001][S2 1001 a.5 ad 2]
Q5. ST I q.3 pr. "de Deo scire non possumus quid sit, sed quid non sit" Own English: about God we cannot know what he is, only what he is not. [S1 sth1003] (Latin confirmed in Leonine text. The newadvent page for Q.3 does not carry this prologue sentence, so no Dominican English was checked for it; use our own English.)
Q6. ST I q.5 a.4 ad 1 "pulchra enim dicuntur quae visa placent. ... pulchrum in debita proportione consistit" Own English: for we call beautiful those things that please when seen; beauty consists in due proportion. [S1 sth1003][S2 1005 a.4 ad 1]
Q7. ST I q.2 a.3 co. fifth way ends: "Ergo est aliquid intelligens, a quo omnes res naturales ordinantur ad finem, et hoc dicimus Deum." (the arrow and the archer: "sicut sagitta a sagittante"). First way opens: "Certum est enim, et sensu constat, aliqua moveri in hoc mundo." [S1 sth1002][S2 1002 a.3]
Q8. ST I q.20 a.2 co. "cum amare nil aliud sit quam velle bonum alicui, manifestum est quod Deus omnia quae sunt, amat." ... "Sed amor Dei est infundens et creans bonitatem in rebus." Own English: since to love is nothing other than to will good to someone, God loves all things that exist. God's love pours out and creates goodness in things. [S1 sth1015][S2 1020 a.2]
Q9. ST I q.21 a.3 co. "misericordia est Deo maxime attribuenda, tamen secundum effectum, non secundum passionis affectum" and "misericors dicitur aliquis quasi habens miserum cor" (one is called merciful as "having a miserable heart"). [S1 sth1015][S2 1021 a.3]
Q10. ST I-II q.26 a.4 co. "amare est velle alicui bonum" (he cites Aristotle, Rhetoric II). [S1 sth2026][S2 2026 a.4]
Q11. ST I-II q.94 a.2 co. "bonum est faciendum et prosequendum, et malum vitandum" (first precept of law); "bonum est quod omnia appetunt". [S1 sth2094][S2 2094 a.2]
Q12. ST I-II q.90 a.4 co. law is "quaedam rationis ordinatio ad bonum commune, ab eo qui curam communitatis habet, promulgata" Own English: an ordinance of reason for the common good, made by the one who has care of the community, and promulgated. [S1 sth2090][S2 2090 a.4]
Q13. ST II-II q.23 a.1 co. "caritas amicitia quaedam est hominis ad Deum" ; "ridiculum enim est dicere quod aliquis habeat amicitiam ad vinum vel ad equum". Own English: charity is a kind of friendship of man with God; it is silly to say one has friendship for wine or a horse. [S1 sth3023][S2 3023 a.1]
Q14. ST II-II q.30 a.4 co. "inter omnes virtutes quae ad proximum pertinent potissima est misericordia". Also: "Secundum se quidem misericordia maxima est"; but for man charity, which unites to God, is greater. Own English: among all virtues toward neighbor, mercy is the greatest. [S1 sth3027][S2 3030 a.4]
Q15. ST II-II q.58 a.1 co. justice: "iustitia est habitus secundum quem aliquis constanti et perpetua voluntate ius suum unicuique tribuit". [S1 sth3057][S2 3058 a.1]
Q16. ST II-II q.168 a.2 co. (rest and play) "Quies autem animae est delectatio" and the story of John the Evangelist and the bow (Cassian, Conferences XXIV, 21): "quod similiter animus hominis frangeretur, si nunquam a sua intentione relaxaretur." Own English: the mind would break if its tension were never relaxed. ad 3: use play "sicut somno et quietibus ceteris" (as with sleep and other rests) once serious duties are done (cites Cicero). Virtue about play: "eutrapelia". [S1 sth3155][S2 3168 a.2]
Q17. ST II-II q.188 a.6 co. "Sicut enim maius est illuminare quam lucere solum, ita maius est contemplata aliis tradere quam solum contemplari." Own English: As it is greater to illuminate than only to shine, so it is greater to hand on to others what one has contemplated than only to contemplate. (This is the real source of the popular "to give to others the fruits of contemplation".) Same in III q.40 a.1 ad 2: Christ chose "vitam ... praedicando et docendo contemplata aliis tradit". [S1 sth3183, sth4040][S2 3188 a.6, 4040 a.1]
Q18. ST II-II q.10 a.8 co. Jews and pagans "nullo modo sunt ad fidem compellendi, ut ipsi credant, quia credere voluntatis est." Own English: they must not be forced to believe in any way, because to believe is an act of the will. (Same article allows force against heretics and apostates, and limits to prevent obstruction.) [S1 sth3001][S2 3010 a.8]
Q19. Lauda Sion (sequence for Corpus Christi, Officium "Sacerdos", Mass). "Quantum potes, tantum aude, / quia maior omni laude, / nec laudare sufficis." Own English: Dare as much as you can, for he is greater than all praise, and you are not able to praise enough. Also "quod non capis, quod non vides, / animosa firmat fides / praeter rerum ordinem." [S3 pcx.html] Latin public-domain; edition (c) Fundacion.
Q20. Pange lingua (vespers hymn, Corpus Christi; Officium "Sacerdos") "Verbum caro panem verum / verbo carnem efficit ... et si sensus deficit, / ad firmandum cor sincerum / sola fides sufficit." and "Tantum ergo sacramentum / veneremur cernui ... praestet fides supplementum / sensuum defectui." [S3] same rights. Editor note: "Officium Sacerdos" "authenticitate probabile"; Ptolemy of Lucca c.1317 says Thomas wrote the Corpus Christi office at Urban IV's command; bull "Transiturus" 1264, Thomas then at the curia. [S5]
Q21. Adoro te devote "Visus, tactus, gustus in te fallitur, / set auditu solo tute creditur." "Credo quicquid dixit dei filius, / nichil ueritatis uerbo uerius." "Plagas sicut Thomas non intueor, / deum tamen meum te confiteor." "Pie pellicane, Ihesu domine, / me immundum munda tuo sanguine." "Ihesu, quem uelatum nunc aspicio, / quando fiet illud quod tam sicio?" [S4 pat.html: Wielockx ed.; marked "authenticitate probabilis"; manuscript spelling]. Authenticity is probable, not certain: say "attributed".
Q22. Sacris solemniis (matins hymn): "recedant vetera, nova sint omnia, / corda, voces et opera." [S3]

## More verified quotations
Q23. SCG I.1 n.1-2 (S7) Latin: "Veritatem meditabitur guttur meum, et labia mea detestabuntur impium" (Prov 8:7) opens the work. n.2: "sapientis est ordinare" (cites Aristotle). I.2 n.1: "Inter omnia vero hominum studia sapientiae studium est perfectius, sublimius, utilius et iucundius." English Rickaby 1905: "OF all human pursuits, the pursuit of wisdom is the more perfect, the more sublime, the more useful, and the more agreeable." Own English: Of all the studies of men, the study of wisdom is the more perfect, the more lofty, the more useful, the more joyful. Public-domain.
Q24. SCG I.2 n.1 continues: wisdom's company "non habet amaritudinem conversatio illius nec taedium convictus illius, sed laetitiam et gaudium" (Wisd 8:16). [S7]
Q25. SCG I.1 n.4 "Oportet igitur veritatem esse ultimum finem totius universi" Own English: truth must be the last end of the whole universe. [S7]
Q26. De ente et essentia, prologue: "Quia parvus error in principio magnus est in fine" Own English: because a small error at the start is a great one at the end (he cites Aristotle, De caelo I). [S8 oee.html] This is the work he wrote as a young man; date not checked in this session.
Q27. De veritate q.1 a.1 co.: "veritas est adaequatio rei et intellectus" Own English: truth is the conformity of thing and intellect. NOTE: here he quotes Isaac Israeli ("dicit Isaac"); Thomas uses it as one of three ways to define truth, and the same article cites Anselm ("veritas est rectitudo sola mente perceptibilis"). Do not credit it to him as his own coinage. [S8 qdv01]
Q28. Super Ioannem, prologue 1: "Vidi dominum sedentem super solium excelsum et elevatum" (Is 6:1) with: "ceteri Evangelistae informant nos ... quantum ad vitam activam; sed Ioannes in suo Evangelio informat nos etiam quantum ad vitam contemplativam." Own English: the other Gospels teach us about the active life, but John teaches us about the contemplative life as well. He calls John's contemplation "alta, ampla et perfecta" (high, wide, complete). [S8 cih00]
Q29. In Symbolum Apostolorum (Credo conferences, report of Reginald of Piperno), end of prologue: "nullus debet dubitare de fide, sed credere ea quae fidei sunt magis quam ea quae videt: quia visus hominis potest decipi, sed Dei scientia nunquam fallitur." Own English: no one should doubt faith; he should believe the things of faith more than what he sees, because human sight can be deceived, but God's knowledge never fails. Opening: "Primum quod est necessarium Christiano, est fides". [S8 csv] NOTE: this is a reportatio, words as taken down by a listener (Reginald), not text he wrote himself.
Q30. De decem praeceptis (reportatio of Peter of Andria), prologue: "Tria sunt homini necessaria ad salutem: scilicet scientia credendorum, scientia desiderandorum, et scientia operandorum." Own English: three things are necessary for man's salvation: knowledge of what to believe, of what to desire, and of what to do (creed, Our Father, law). Natural law there: "lumen intellectus insitum nobis a Deo". [S8 cac] Reportatio, not his own writing.
Q31. ST I q.1 a.9 co. "impossibile est nobis aliter lucere divinum radium, nisi varietate sacrorum velaminum circumvelatum" (quoting Dionysius) and "Est autem naturale homini ut per sensibilia ad intelligibilia veniat, quia omnis nostra cognitio a sensu initium habet." Own English: it is natural for man to come to understanding through things sensed, because all our knowledge begins in the senses. [S1 sth1001][S2 1001 a.9]
Q32. ST II-II q.1 a.5 ad 1 "per lumen fidei videntur esse credenda" - see the flag on the Werfel line below. [S1 sth3001][S2 3001 a.5 ad 1]
Q33. Tocco's report of the crucifix at Naples (chapel of St Nicholas): voice from the image: "Thoma bene scripsisti de me, quam recipies a me pro tuo labore mercedem?" Thomas: "Domine, non nisi te." [S9, Tocco, running head p.108; Gui has "Domine non aliam mercedem recipiam, nisi teipsum" (p.~189 running head)]. Rights: public-domain; reported speech, not his writing.
Q34. Tocco, Fossanova, viaticum (chap. LVIII, running head p.132): "Sumo te pretium redemptionis animae meae, sumo te viaticum peregrinationis meae, pro cuius amore studui, vigilavi, et laboravi, te praedicavi et docui, nihil contra te dixi unquam, sed si quid dixi, ignorans dixi nec sum pertinax in sensu meo sed si quid male dixi de hoc Sacramento et aliis, totum relinquo correctioni Sanctae Romanae Ecclesiae, in cuius oboedientia nunc transeo ex hac vita." Own English: I receive you, the price of my soul's redemption; I receive you, provision for my pilgrimage, for love of whom I have studied, kept watch, and worked ... if I said anything wrongly about this sacrament, I leave it all to the correction of the Roman Church, in whose obedience I now pass from this life. Also: before it he answers the abbot's profession of faith: "Si maior scientia, quam fides de hoc Sacramento in hac vita haberi potest, in illa respondeo, quod vere credo". [S9]
Q35. Tocco: when Reginald found him praying, Thomas told him at Fossanova: "Fili Raynalde, haec requies mea in saeculum saeculi; hic habitabo, quoniam elegi eam." (Ps 131:14) [S9 Tocco chap. LVII p.130-131]. And of the monks carrying wood on their shoulders: "Unde hoc mihi, ut servi Dei serviant homini" (the line is cut off in my OCR; read the next lines before using). [S9]
Q36. Albert: "Nos vocamus istum bovem mutum, sed ipse adhuc talem dabit in doctrina mugitum, quod in toto mundo sonabit." Own English: We call him the dumb ox, but he will yet give such a bellow in his teaching that it will sound through the whole world. [S9 Tocco p.79; Gui p.178 has "in toto orbe sonabit"]. "fertur" (it is reported): legend-level report, 40+ years after.

## STRAW - exact check (Naples process 1319)
Source: [S9] Process, art. LXXIX, witness Bartholomew of Capua (his deposition starts near Fontes line 17244, "dominus Bartholomeus de Capua, logotheta"). Printed p.376-377 (running heads checked: "376" at the head of the page that holds the oath and "377" at the page of "non possum"). Exact Latin, two lines of the article:
(1) After Thomas stops writing at St Nicholas's chapel: "Raynalde, non possum." Reginald fears he has gone mad with study and presses him.
(2) "Raynalde, non possum quia omnia que scripsi videntur mihi palee".
(3) Thomas then swears Reginald to silence: "Ego adiuro te per Deum vivum omnipotentem et per fidem quam tenetis ordini nostro et per caritatem quam michi stringeris quod ea que tibi dixero nulli reveles in vita mea", and adds: "Omnia que scripsi videntur michi palee respectu eorum que vidi et revelata sunt michi".
Bartholomew's source: Reginald of Piperno himself ("frater Raynaldus"), his own words in the process text: Reginald was Thomas's companion; Bartholomew heard it from him "after some years". Article LXXIX opens: "cum dictus frater Thomas celebraret missam in dicta capella sancti Nicolai Neapoli, fuit mira mutatione commotus, et post ipsam missam nunquam scripsit neque dictavit aliquid, immo suspendit organa scriptionis in tertia parte Summe, in tractatu de Penitentia" (editor's note: stopped at III q.90 a.4). Then he went to his sister the countess of San Severino, spoke hardly a word to her; Reginald told her: "A festo beati Nicolai circa fuit in isto statu et ex tunc nichil scripsit." Then the journey to the council; fell ill at the castle of "Magentia de Campania" and died.
Two forms of the word: "palee" in the process; Baring-Gould (S10 p.152) renders it "worthless", not "straw". The English "straw" is the usual translation of "palee" (chaff, straw); no Latin word "stramen" is used. Own English: Everything I have written seems to me like straw, compared with what I have seen and what has been revealed to me. The day: the feast of St Nicholas, 6 Dec 1273 (Reginald's phrase "A festo beati Nicolai"; the year is not stated in the Latin; Baring-Gould writes of the chapel but gives no date in my pages).
Another Process detail from the same witness, art. LXXVIII (p.376): the friars at Paris: "Pater, quam pulchra villa est ista Parisius" ... "Venderetis eam regi Francie et de pecunia edificaretis omnia loca fratrum Predicatorum." Thomas: "In veritate plus vellem Crisostomum super Matheum." Source named: Nicholas de Mala Sorte, councillor of the king of France, "vulgariter et quasi communiter notum Parisius". NEW to most English pages (check).
Also art. LXXVII-LXXVIII (p.375-376): on the road to the Council of Lyons he struck his head on a fallen tree near Borgo Nuovo; Reginald: "you and Bonaventure will be cardinals"; Thomas: "Raynalde, in nullo statu possum esse ita utilis ordini nostro, sicut in isto"; and again "Raynalde, sis securus quod ego nunquam in perpetuum mutabo statum." (He prayed that he would never change his state and never love earthly things: p.374-375.) [S9]

## Popular lines wrongly credited, or not exact (FLAGS)
F1. "Beware the man of one book" / "hominem unius libri timeo": no source in Thomas's works; the Latin is first credited to him by Jeremy Taylor in 1649 (Wikipedia Homo unius libri [S12], fetched). Taylor's own report: asked how to become learned he said "by reading one book" (Southey quoting Taylor). Do not quote as his.
F2. "To one who has faith, no explanation is necessary. To one without faith, no explanation is possible." Not in Thomas. Popular form from the 1943 film of Werfel's "Song of Bernadette"; an 1882 St Louis Post-Dispatch editorial is the earliest found (search summary only: metabunk thread; not read raw). Loosest relative: ST II-II q.1 a.5 ad 1 (Q32) says unbelievers do not know matters of faith to be credible, believers know them "per lumen fidei". Mark: do not quote.
F3. "The things that we love tell us what we are" (and similar "love takes up where knowledge leaves off", "Better to be a fool and know it"): no locator found; sources are quote sites. Not verified. Do not use unless a part, question, article is found.
F4. "Grace perfects nature" / "gratia non tollit naturam sed perficit": real, with the exact words "gratia non tollat naturam, sed perficiat" (I q.1 a.8 ad 2, Q3). Also found in I q.2 a.2 ad 1? (not checked). Safe to use with locator.
F5. "Letter to Brother John" / "De modo studendi" ("Be slow to speak ... do not heed from whom you hear, but commit to memory whatever good is said ... do not seek things too high for you"): corpusthomisticum lists the author as "Ignotus Auctor" (Taurini 1954 text). Unknown author; not Thomas as far as Corpus Thomisticum says. Latin: "non respicias a quo audias, sed quidquid boni dicatur, memoriae recommenda". Do not credit.
F6. "Creator ineffabilis" (prayer before study): widely attributed (Pius XI, Studiorum Ducem 1923, per Wikipedia); I did not find scholarly proof either way in this session (Wikipedia page has no authenticity discussion). Mark "traditionally attributed". Do not quote as proven.
F7. "Concede mihi, misericors Deus" ("Grant me, merciful God, to desire ardently what is pleasing to you..."): a secondary page (S13) says Tocco includes it and that Lydia Maidl questioned the authorship; I searched Tocco's Latin in S9 and did NOT find the words ("Concede m..." gave only two unrelated hits), so the Tocco claim is unverified. Mark "attributed". English text there: "May all passing things become worthless to me on your account" etc. (do not quote until the Latin source is found).
F8. "Adoro te devote": Corpus Thomisticum label "authenticitate probabilis" (Wielockx 2007 ed.); Pange lingua, Sacris solemniis, Lauda Sion in the office "Sacerdos": label "authenticitate probabile"; the earlier office "Sapientia": "authenticitate dubium" (S3, S4, S5). The Wikipedia Aquinas page says modern scholarship confirms the authorship of the Corpus Christi hymns (S11, not checked against Torrell). Safe wording: "ascribed to him; modern scholars consider it probable".
F9. "Panis angelicus" (Sacris solemniis verses "Panis angelicus fit panis hominum"): in the text I read, the Sacris solemniis hymn has the stanza beginning "Noctis recolitur cena novissima"; the "Panis angelicus" verse is a later stanza of that hymn. I did not locate the exact stanza in the raw text; check before quoting.
F10. Often repeated: "The dumb ox" as Thomas's nickname - the Albert bellow story is "fertur" (reported) in Tocco and Gui; Baring-Gould adds the flying-ox joke ("I did not believe an ox could fly, nor did I, till now, believe that a religious could tell a lie") [S10 p.128], a legend.
F11. "Thomas asked Bonaventure where he found his books; Bonaventure pointed to the crucifix" (Baring-Gould, S10): the usual form is the reverse (Thomas points to the crucifix). Not checked in Tocco or Gui in this session.

## Timeline (own-words focus, with sources)
- c.1225 (1227 in some sources): Born at the Aquino family castle, Roccasecca, or nearby; Wikipedia gives c.1225, Baring-Gould "most trustworthy authorities" 1227 [S11][S10 p.119]. Date disputed.
- c.1230: Given as a child oblate to Monte Cassino; legend that he kept asking his teachers "What is God?" (Baring-Gould, flagged "such is the legend"). [S10 p.119-120] NEW check.
- Naples studies, then the Dominican habit (1244 by Wikipedia); his brothers seize him on the road and keep him a prisoner at Monte San Giovanni and Roccasecca (nearly one year, Wikipedia [S11]); the tempter and the firebrand: Tocco: he drove the girl out with a burning stick, drew a cross on the wall, prayed and slept; two angels girded him: "Ecce ex parte Dei te cingimus ... cingulo castitatis" [S9 Tocco p.~84]. Baring-Gould tells it too [S10]. Tocco's text is the oldest.
- 1245-48: Studies under Albert at Paris, then Cologne; silence, the "dumb ox" tale. [S10 p.127-128][S9 Tocco p.79].
- 1256-59: Master in Paris; 1259-65: Summa contra Gentiles (Wikipedia gives 1259-1265 [S11]).
- 1261-65: Orvieto; Catena aurea; the Corpus Christi office for Urban IV; bull "Transiturus" 1264 [S5 pcxn]. Baring-Gould places Job and the Catena aurea at Rome (S10); Wikipedia places the Catena at Orvieto. Sources conflict; not resolved.

## Character (own-words focus)
- Silent, large, slow to speak; students thought him a dull ox; the same man dictated to several scribes at once (only Tocco is the source for the dictation claim; not read here).
- Loved argument to the last point: ST always starts with the best objections. (ST structure; I q.1 a.8 ad 2 shows him sorting authority by strength.)
- Humble toward correction: at death he leaves his statements on the sacrament to the correction of the Church [S9 Tocco p.132].
- Absent-minded in thought: the king's table at Paris, he struck it and said "Modo conclusum est contra haeresim Manichaeorum"; the prior pulled his cape [S9 Tocco p.116; Calo p.42 has it too].
- Wants no rank and no money: he would rather have Chrysostom on Matthew than Paris itself (Process art. LXXVIII); the only primary words I read on rank: "in nullo statu possum esse ita utilis ordini nostro, sicut in isto" and "nunquam in perpetuum mutabo statum" [S9 p.375-376]). Do not claim that he refused a hat or abbacy beyond what these words show.
- Believes in rest and play: ST II-II q.168 a.2 (bow story, eutrapelia) [S1][S2].
- Teaches that the mind tires: "fatigatio animalis" cured by "delectatio" - a man who knew exhaustion.
- Prays with tears: Tocco on the Isaiah text, the fasting, the voice heard at night by Reginald (p.105-106); on Reginald's pleading he finally told him that Peter and Paul had come (rest of passage not read). Check before use.
- Critic's view: ST I q.1 a.8 ad 2 says authority of the doctors of the Church is only "probabiliter" (probable); he himself defers to the Church.

## Scenes (8 usable)
1. The ox in the schoolroom: Albert's "bos mutus" and the bellow that fills the world [S9 p.79; S10 p.128].
2. The firebrand and the cross on the wall: the girl, the burning stick, the angels' girdle [S9 p.~84].
3. King Louis's table: he thumps the table and says the Manichees are finished; the prior tugs his cape [S9 p.116].
4. The Paris hill: friars look down on Paris; "I would rather have Chrysostom on Matthew" [S9, Process art. LXXVIII p.376].
5. Isaiah at night: Reginald hears him talking in the dark; Thomas has him light a candle and dictates "as if reading from a book" [S9 Tocco p.105-106; the rest of the scene not read].
6. St Nicholas's chapel, Naples: the voice from the crucifix "Thoma, bene scripsisti de me"; his answer "non nisi te" [S9 Tocco p.108; Gui p.189 variant].
7. 6 Dec 1273: after Mass, silence; Reginald presses; "non possum"; the oath; "palee" [S9 Process art. LXXIX p.376-377].
8. Fossanova: the monks carry wood on their backs, he says "Unde hoc mihi"; the viaticum, "Sumo te pretium"; the Song of Songs dictated; death 7 March 1274 [S9 Tocco p.130-132; S10 p.153-154].
Extra: the tree on the road to Lyons; Reginald: "cardinal"; Thomas: "never change my state" [S9 p.375-376].
