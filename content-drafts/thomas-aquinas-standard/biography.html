Saint Thomas Aquinas was coming back to Paris from Saint-Denis with a group of friars. Near the city they stopped to rest a little, and they looked at Paris. “Father, what a beautiful city Paris is!” one of them said. “It certainly is,” Saint Thomas answered. “I wish it were yours,” said the friar. “What would I do with it?” Saint Thomas asked. “You could sell it to the king of France and build every house of the Preaching Friars with the money.” Saint Thomas said: “In truth, I would rather have Chrysostom on Matthew.” He meant a book: the sermons of Saint John Chrysostom, a Father of the early Church, on the Gospel of Matthew.1

He was a big, quiet man who would rather have a book about God than a city. What he wanted was God himself: to know him as far as a human mind can, and to hand on to others what he knew. His family once tried to take that from him by force. Later a pope tried to draw him away from it with honors.2

The Church remembers him as its great teacher. Pope John XXII declared him a saint in 1323. In 1567 Pope Saint Pius V named him a Doctor of the Church, a title the Church gives to very few saints, whose teaching it wants everyone to learn from. In 1879 Pope Leo XIII asked the bishops of the world to bring his thought back into their schools. The next year he made Saint Thomas the patron of every Catholic school. In his work faith and reason walk together. “Grace does not destroy nature but perfects it,” he wrote. So reason can serve faith. And he believed that what a person sees of God is meant to be given away.3

Saint Thomas Goes to the Monks at Five

Saint Thomas was born about the year 1225 at Roccasecca, a castle of the counts of Aquino in the hills between Rome and Naples. His father Landulf was the count, and his mother was named Theodora.4

While he was still a baby, a terrible storm broke suddenly over the castle. Lightning struck the tower where his sister lay asleep, and it killed her. It killed the horses in the stable too. Theodora was more afraid for her son than for her daughter. She came trembling to the bed where the nurse slept with him, and she found him unhurt. She gave thanks to God.5

At five he went to the great abbey of Monte Cassino. His parents sent him there so that the monks could train him in good ways. His father had a plan for him. Landulf was noble and powerful, and people said he meant one day to make his son the lord abbot of Monte Cassino.6

The boy was not talkative. He often kept away from the other noble boys and stayed by himself with a reading sheet in his hand, and he prayed. An old story says that he kept asking his teachers one question: what is God?7

The abbot saw what was in him. He sent word to Count Landulf that such a boy should not stay hidden in the cloister. The boy should go to Naples, where learning flourished, so that one day he could speak the words of saving wisdom. So his father sent him to Naples. There a master named Martin taught him grammar and logic, and Peter of Ireland taught him the study of nature. When he went over his lessons for the other students, he made them deeper and clearer than his teachers had.8

In Naples he also came to know the Preaching Friars, the new order of Saint Dominic. It was an order of poor men who lived to preach. He went to their house to hear them preach, and something in those sermons drew him in.9

Saint Thomas Joins the Friars and Is Seized

His name was spreading through the schools of Naples, and he began to think hard about his gift. God had given him a quick mind and had made it grow by grace. To hide it in an easy, careless life would make him like the servant in the Gospel who wrapped his master's money in a cloth and buried it. He could make it grow in a religious order. He was “thirsting for the fountain of wisdom,” and the Preaching Friars were what his heart had long desired. A friar named John of San Giuliano talked with him and urged him on. Saint Thomas was still in his teens. Against his father's wishes, he put on the robe of the Preaching Friars.10

He was a nobleman's son, and he could have had riches. He did not want them. As Solomon once did, he asked God for nothing but wisdom. It was the wisdom of God that he loved. Years later he put into words why it was so beautiful to him. “Of all the studies of men,” he wrote, “the study of wisdom is the most perfect, the highest, the most useful, and the most joyful.” He took Scripture's words about wisdom as his own: “Her company has no bitterness, and living with her brings no weariness, but gladness and joy.” He also wrote about the different kinds of religious life. There he put the heart of his calling into one sentence. “As it is greater to give light than only to shine, so it is greater to hand on to others what one has contemplated than only to contemplate.”11

The nobles of Naples were amazed that a young man of such birth would leave his parents' house and hide himself under the robe of a poor order. The friars were glad, and they hoped he would one day reach the heights of learning. When the family's tenants at Roccasecca heard the news, they went to his mother in tears. Theodora grieved, because she was losing the comfort of having her son near her. But she was glad too. Before he was born, a hermit from the mountain near Roccasecca had told her that her son would one day join the Preaching Friars. Now she saw it coming true. Theodora set out for Naples with a fitting retinue, to see her son and strengthen him in his choice. She longed to bear him a second time in the spirit, as she had once borne him in the flesh.12

The friars did not know her heart. They feared she had come to take him away. So they sent him out of the city by way of Terracina and Anagni to their house of Santa Sabina in Rome. From there they meant to send him on to Paris. Theodora followed him to Rome, longing all the more to see him. At Santa Sabina she begged the friars to show her the son she loved, and she promised not to lead him out of the order. They would not. When she learned they were sending him to Paris, she was shaken to the heart. She could not see her son, and the friars would not believe her. Her love as a mother overcame her faith.13

Her other sons were soldiers in the army of the Emperor Frederick II, camped at Acquapendente in Tuscany. She sent them messengers, and she commanded them by her blessing as their mother to take her son and send him to her under guard. Her message was a mother's cry: “Your brother Thomas has been carried off by the Preaching Friars, and they are taking him to Paris. Alas, I will never see him again! The friars would not show him to me even for a moment, the joy of my heart. Help, sons. Take my son and bring him to his mother.”14

The brothers told the emperor and got his leave. Then they posted watchers on the roads. They found Saint Thomas resting beside a spring with four friars of his order, tired from the road. His brothers rushed at him “not as brothers but as enemies.” They tried to strip the robe off him, but he held it fast, shouting and weeping. In the end they gave up, for fear of hurting him. They let the four friars go. Saint Thomas they sent to his mother under guard, and he was still wearing the robe.15

One of those brothers was Rainald. Years later the same emperor would have him killed.16

Saint Thomas's Family Locks Him in a Castle

When Theodora saw her son, she wept for joy. He would not be comforted. He wept without stopping, and “his tears were his bread day and night.” She was afraid of losing him again, after she had searched for him with so much toil and anguish. So she had him kept under guard in the family castles of Monte San Giovanni and Roccasecca until his brothers came home. She could not persuade him to take off the robe. Then she sent one person after another to talk to him, to test the promise made before his birth: if it came from God, no human pressure would change it. He would not give way.17

The friars took their complaint to Pope Innocent IV, and the pope ordered the emperor to punish the brothers. Frederick had them arrested. But the friars let the case drop for fear of scandal. They had heard that the young man was holding on to his robe even in prison.18

The captivity lasted almost two years, and Saint Thomas did not waste it. He read the whole Bible. He learned the Sentences, the textbook that every student of theology had to master. And he taught his sisters. One of them had been sent to talk him out of the order, and instead he turned her toward God. She later became abbess of the convent of Santa Maria at Capua. Brother John of San Giuliano came to see him and secretly left him tunics, so that he would not go without. As the friars later put it, “light came to him in the dark, and in chains he found more freedom.” When he read, God spoke with him, and when he prayed, he talked sweetly with God.19

His brothers came back and grew harsher. They tormented him with coaxing and with threats. They tore his robe to rags, so that shame would make him put on other clothes. He bore it patiently and wrapped himself in the shreds, “as if he had put on Christ” with that robe.20

Then they tried something worse. They sent into his room a very beautiful young woman, dressed as a prostitute, to lead him into sin. Saint Thomas was alone. He looked at her and felt desire rise in him, and it grieved him. He snatched a burning stick from the fire and drove her out in anger, and he shut the door behind her. Then he went to a corner of the room and burned the sign of the cross into the wall with the end of the stick. He threw himself on the floor in tears and prayed:21

“Lord, I cannot be pure unless you, God, give it to me. I beg your mercy, which no words can tell, to grant me purity of mind and body, so that I may serve you with a clean heart and a clean body.”

— Saint Thomas's prayer in the locked room, as Peter Calo gives it22

Praying like this, he fell asleep, and two angels were sent to him in a vision. They told him that God had heard him and that he had won the fight. They gripped him tight around the waist from both sides and said: “On God's behalf we gird you with the belt of chastity, which no temptation can undo.” The grip hurt, and he woke with a cry. People came running to ask why he had cried out. He would not tell them. He kept that gift a secret until he was dying. Then he told it only to his companion Reginald, and he added that he had never again felt the least stirring of the flesh.23

Theodora saw that nothing would move him. She was afraid to fight against the will of God, and she let the friars visit him more freely. After almost two years she decided to let him go, for she saw that what the hermit had foretold had to come true in her son. She wisely relaxed the guard and let Saint Thomas be lowered by a rope from a window of the castle. His friars were waiting below.24

They received him with joy and took him to Naples. They found that he had learned as much in prison as if he had spent those years in a school. Soon the order sent him to Rome. From there John the Teuton, the Master of the Order, took him north across the Alps to the school of a great teacher.25

Saint Thomas Studies Under Saint Albert

The teacher was Saint Albert, whom the Church now calls Saint Albert the Great. He taught at the Dominican school in Cologne, and men said he knew every field of learning. When Saint Thomas heard him, he was glad. He had quickly found what he was looking for: a master “from whom he could eagerly drink what he thirsted for.” And the young friar fell silent. He studied hard, he prayed, and he stored up in his memory all that he heard. His friars later explained the silence: with no outside talk to distract him, he could gather learning faster.26

His fellow students saw a tall, heavy young man who hardly said a word. They gave him a name. They called him the dumb ox.27

Saint Albert once began to lecture on the Divine Names, a hard book by an ancient writer called Dionysius. A fellow student thought Saint Thomas was slow, and he offered to go over the lecture with him. Saint Thomas thanked him and accepted. But the student lost his way when he tried to repeat the lesson. Then Saint Thomas took it up, as if he had just been given leave to speak. He went through the lecture clearly, and he added things the master had not said. Afterward he begged the student to tell no one. He wanted to stay hidden.28

The student told the master of students, and soon Saint Albert heard of it. A page of Saint Thomas's notes was found outside his cell, and Saint Albert read it. The next day he gave Saint Thomas a hard question to answer in front of the class. Saint Thomas prayed first and put himself in God's hands. Then he answered with a distinction, a way of dividing the question so that each part could be answered on its own.29

“Brother Thomas,” said Saint Albert, “you do not seem to be answering. You seem to be deciding the question.” Saint Thomas answered with great respect. “Master, I do not see how I can answer the question any other way.” “Then answer it by your own distinction,” said Saint Albert, and he put four arguments to him. They were so hard that the master thought he had him trapped. Saint Thomas answered all four. Then Saint Albert spoke as if by the spirit of prophecy. “We call him the dumb ox, but he will one day give such a bellow in his teaching that it will sound through the whole world.”30

Saint Thomas did not grow proud. From then on Saint Albert gave him the hardest debates. Saint Thomas also wrote up his master's lectures on Aristotle's Ethics. About 1252 Saint Albert wrote to the Master of the Order and asked that Saint Thomas be sent to Paris to teach. The Master hesitated, until a cardinal named Hugh pressed him to agree. Saint Thomas himself would have refused the honor, but he had to obey. The dumb ox was going to Paris.31

Saint Thomas Becomes a Master at Paris

At Paris Saint Thomas began as a bachelor, a junior teacher who lectured under a master. When he lectured, all that he had stored up in silence poured out. He raised new questions and found new and clear ways to settle them, with new reasons. His students caught from him a love of learning.32

One of his sisters had died. While he prayed in Paris, she came to him in a vision. She told him she was in purgatory. She asked for Masses, because she hoped they would set her free. Saint Thomas called his students together and asked them to say Masses and to pray for her soul. He saw to it at once, and with care.33

About 1256 the Chancellor of Paris told the prior of the Dominicans to order Saint Thomas to prepare for the mastership. A master of theology could teach in his own name and settle disputed questions. Older men usually went first. Saint Thomas begged to be excused. He said that he did not know enough and that he was too young. The prior held him to the command, and he obeyed.34

He was troubled. He thought himself unfit because he was young, and he did not know what to do. So he went to pray beside an altar. Weeping, he began a psalm: “Save me, God, for truths have grown few among the children of men.” He prayed a long time, and at last he fell asleep.35

In his sleep an old friar of his order came to him, a man worthy of great respect, sent from heaven. “Brother Thomas,” the old man said, “why do you weep as you pray to God?” “Because the burden of the mastership has been laid on me,” Saint Thomas answered, “and my knowledge is not enough for it. And I cannot think what to put forward for my first lecture.” “See, you are heard,” said the old man. “Take up the burden of the mastership, because God is with you. For your first lecture, put forward nothing but this: ‘You water the hills from your high rooms; the earth will be filled with the fruit of your works.’” Saint Thomas woke and thanked God, who had heard him so quickly.36

The verse comes from a psalm that praises God for watering the hills. Saint Thomas took it as his task. The water would come from God; his work was to carry it down to others. He spoke on that verse at his first lecture as a master of theology, when he was barely past thirty. Soon so many students crowded into his school that the room could hardly hold them. He settled questions in a plain, fair way. He let the other side speak first, and almost every article in the Summa begins with the arguments against the answer he will give. Only then does he answer.37

Saint Thomas Teaches in Italy and Refuses Honors

Around 1259 the order called him home to Italy. In 1261 it sent him to teach the friars at Orvieto. In these years he finished a long book called the Summa against the Gentiles. He opened it with a verse from Scripture: “My mouth will ponder truth.” In 1264 Pope Urban IV gave the whole Church a new feast, Corpus Christi, the feast of the Body of Christ. At the pope's command Saint Thomas wrote its prayers and hymns. One hymn tells the singer: “Dare as much as you can, for he is greater than all praise, and you cannot praise him enough.”38

In 1265 the order sent him to Rome to run its school at Santa Sabina, where the friars had once hidden him from his mother. There he began the book that would fill the rest of his life. He called it the Summa of Theology, and he meant it to set out the whole of Christian teaching in order. On its first pages he wrote down his desire. “The least that can be known of the highest things is more to be desired than the most certain knowledge of the smallest things.” He wrote that if the things of faith seem unsure to us, the fault is in our weak minds. They are like the eye of an owl dazzled by the sun. A few pages later he set down the limit of every human mind: “about God we cannot know what he is, only what he is not.”39

He is remembered too for a hymn to Christ hidden in the Host, the Adoro te devote. It is a prayer of longing, and near its end it asks a question: “Jesus, whom I now see veiled, when will that come which I so thirst for?”40

In Rome his dead sister came to him again as he prayed, and she told him that she was free and in glory. He asked her about himself. “You, brother, are in a good state, and you will soon come to us,” she said. “But a greater glory is prepared for you than for us. Only hold on to what you have.” He asked about his two brothers who had died. She said that Landulf was in purgatory and that Rainald was in paradise.41

Rainald had died a cruel death. He had stayed loyal to the Church, and Emperor Frederick had him killed. Saint Thomas believed the killing was unjust, and he prayed to know where his brother's soul had gone. The answer came that Rainald's name was written in the book of life. Another time he was shown in prayer an open book written in gold and blue letters, like a litany. He found his brother's name among the gold letters, which were the names of the martyrs. Rainald had died for the Church, and he had been patient under torment.42

Rainald was not the only one who suffered. Under the persecution of the Emperor Frederick, all of Saint Thomas's relatives had become exiles in Campania for the Church's sake. Pope Clement IV held Saint Thomas very dear. He offered him high offices in the Church, with their incomes. Some of that money could have gone to his family, if the pope allowed it. His love for his own people could have moved him to accept. He refused the offices and the incomes alike. As he bore his own needs for the love of God, so he could bear theirs.43

Another time he received a papal bull from the same pope. The sealed letter named him archbishop of Naples, and it added to the office the income of the abbey of San Pietro ad Aram. His family had once hoped to make him a lord abbot. Here were an archbishop's chair and an abbey's income in one letter, from the pope himself. Saint Thomas would not take the office. He humbly begged the pope never to promote him to anything else. He had a reason, and he kept it to himself for years.44

In 1268 the order sent him back to Paris to teach a second time. He went, and he carried the unfinished Summa with him.45

Saint Thomas Dines With Saint Louis of France

Those who lived with him said he was often far away in thought, lifted to the things of heaven even at table or among great people. He seemed to live more where his mind was than where his body sat.46

The king of France was Saint Louis IX, and he asked Saint Thomas's advice in hard cases. Once the king invited him to dinner. Saint Thomas excused himself politely: he was dictating the Summa and had no time. The king insisted, and the prior of the Paris house ordered him to go. So he went, still holding the thought he had been working on in his cell.47

He sat beside the king at dinner. Suddenly he struck the table. “Now the argument is settled against the Manichaean heresy.” He had been writing against that old error. He even called out to his companion, as if they were back in his cell: “Get up, scribe.” The prior touched him and tugged hard at his cape. “Take note, Master. You are at the table of the king of France.” Saint Thomas came back to himself. He bowed to the king and asked his pardon. “Forgive me, my lord king. I thought I was in my study.” The king was amazed and moved. He called his scribe and had the thought written down in front of him. A king's invitation could have delighted a count's son and drawn his mind away from God. Saint Thomas had not even noticed where he was.48

At Paris he was once contradicted in public. A man was being examined by the chancellor for the mastership, in the debate that by custom came before it. On the questions put to him, he held the opposite of what Saint Thomas had taught in his own school. Saint Thomas let it pass. Calm in mind and in word, he went back to the friary with his brothers.49

His students could not bear it, and neither could his companion. “Master,” they said, “we feel this as a deep insult to you. That master should not have spoken against your opinion, and you should not have let the truth be wronged like this in front of all the masters of Paris.” Saint Thomas answered calmly. “Sons, I thought a new master should be spared at his first lecture, so that he would not be shamed in front of all the masters. I have no doubt about my teaching, whoever speaks against it. With God's help I have made it firm with the words of the saints and with true reasons. But if the brothers think otherwise, tomorrow I can make up for what I left out.”50

The next day they met again in the bishop's hall, before the same masters. The new master gave the same answers and corrected nothing. Saint Thomas spoke with great gentleness. “Master, this opinion of yours cannot be held without harm to the truth, because it goes against a council of the Church. You must say it another way, if you do not want to disagree with the council.” The man began to say it another way, but he did not change his meaning. Saint Thomas argued again and set the council before him, until the new master admitted his error and humbly asked to learn the truth more fully. “Now you speak well,” said Saint Thomas, and he taught him. The masters wondered at the calm in his mind and in his words. He argued with an opponent as if he were teaching a student.51

He was the same in every debate, however sharp it grew. His answers were full of sweetness and kindness, and he used no proud words. Friars who knew him said his face was always cheerful, gentle, and pleasant.52

Saint Louis died at Tunis in August 1270, on crusade. In 1272 the order's meeting at Florence gave Saint Thomas a new task: he was to set up the order's house of studies at Naples. He left Paris for the last time and went back to the city where he had first met the friars.53

Saint Thomas Teaches and Preaches in Naples

On the road south from Rome he turned aside to Molara, a castle of Cardinal Richard, and there he fell ill with a fever that came back every second day. His companion had a heavy fever that did not break, and the cardinal's doctors expected the worst. Saint Thomas sent him the relics of Saint Agnes, which he carried with him from Rome. The friar laid them on himself and was cured, beyond the hope of all the doctors. That year at Naples Saint Thomas kept a feast of Saint Agnes for the friars, with a good meal.54

In October 1272 King Charles of Anjou ordered that Saint Thomas be paid an ounce of gold a month to teach theology at Naples. Saint Thomas was a big man now, tall and bald at the front. In Naples he settled into the last routine of his life. At dawn he said Mass in the chapel of Saint Nicholas and heard a second Mass after it. Then he lectured. Then he wrote or dictated to several scribes until dinner. After dinner he went to his cell to pray, and after a rest he wrote again. A man who knew the house well could remember seeing him outside the cloister only twice. Hardly any of his time slipped by unused. At table a dish could be set before him and taken away several times without his noticing.55

At his side was Reginald of Piperno, the friar who was his companion in everything. Saint Thomas was so often lost in thought that Reginald had to look after him almost like a nurse. He saw to his master's meals and set before him what he should eat. He served him as a student serves his master and as a son serves his father. When the Host was lifted up at Mass, Saint Thomas used to pray with great devotion and tears: “You, Christ, are the King of glory. You are the everlasting Son of the Father.”56

When he preached, the city came. In Lent 1273 he preached every day on the Our Father, and almost the whole people of Naples came to hear him. He stood in the pulpit with his eyes closed and spoke plainly, in the language of his native land. Reginald wrote down some of his sermons as he gave them. In one he told the people to believe the things of faith even more than what they see, “because human sight can be deceived, but God's knowledge never fails.”57

Once Saint Thomas was writing on the prophet Isaiah, and he came to a passage that he could not understand. No meaning he found was enough for him. For many days he fasted and prayed, and he begged God to show him the secret of those words.58

One night after a day of fasting, Reginald heard him talking with someone in his room. He could hear the sound of the talk but not the words, and he could not tell who was there. When the talking stopped, Saint Thomas called him. “Reginald, son, get up. Light a candle and take the notebook where you were writing on Isaiah. Get ready to write again.” Reginald wrote for a long time. Saint Thomas dictated as easily as if he were reading from a book. After a while he said: “Go and rest, son. There is still a long time left to rest.”59

Reginald longed to know the secret. He fell on his knees at his master's feet, weeping. “I will not get up from this place until you tell me who you were talking with so long tonight.” He charged him by the name of the Lord God to answer. Saint Thomas refused again and again. “Son, you do not need to know this.” Reginald charged him again, more strictly still. Saint Thomas would not seem to despise the name of God, and he burst into tears:60

“Son, you have seen how I have suffered these days over my doubt about this text, which I have just explained. With many tears I asked God to let me understand it. Tonight God took pity on me. He sent me the blessed apostles Peter and Paul, whom I had asked to pray for me, and they taught me everything fully. But on God's behalf I command you: do not dare to tell this to anyone while I live.”

— Saint Thomas to Reginald of Piperno61

He wrote that about God we cannot know what he is. Over one passage of Scripture he felt that limit for many days, and what his mind could not reach, God gave him in prayer.62

The friars of Naples remembered something that happened in the chapel of Saint Nicholas. The sacristan who looked after the church was a man of prayer. He noticed that Saint Thomas always came down from his study to the church before Matins, the friars' prayers in the night. When the bell rang, Saint Thomas hurried back so that no one would see him. Once the sacristan followed him. He went into the chapel behind him and found Saint Thomas fixed in prayer. He was raised in the air about two cubits above the floor, nearly a yard. Saint Thomas was turned toward the crucifix, weeping. Then the sacristan heard a voice come from the image of Christ on the cross, and he heard Saint Thomas answer.63

“Thomas, you have written well of me. What reward will you take from me for your work?”

“Lord, nothing but you.”

— The friars of Naples, as told by Calo, Tocco, and Gui64

He was writing then about the passion and resurrection of Christ. The great book had already covered God and creation, the human soul, law and grace and the virtues, and the life of Christ. Now it had reached the sacraments. By December 1273 he was writing on Penance, the sacrament in which God forgives the sinner who confesses.65

Saint Thomas Stops Writing After a Mass

Every morning he said Mass in the chapel of Saint Nicholas, and at Mass he often wept. The feast of Saint Nicholas falls on 6 December. Around that feast he was saying Mass there when a wonderful change came over him.66

After that Mass he never wrote or dictated another word. He “hung up the tools of his writing.” The Summa stopped in its third part at the ninetieth question, in the pages on Penance. He dictated nothing to his scribes. He wrote nothing himself.67

Reginald saw that he had stopped. “Father,” he said, “how can you leave so great a work, which you began for the praise of God and the enlightenment of the world?” Saint Thomas answered: “Reginald, I cannot.”68

Reginald was frightened. He feared that too much study had harmed his master's mind, and he kept begging him to go on. Each time the answer was the same. Saint Thomas could not.69

Then Saint Thomas went to his sister, the Countess of San Severino, whom he loved dearly. He hurried there, though the road was hard for him. She came out to meet him, and he hardly said a word to her. In great fear she turned to Reginald. “What is this? Brother Thomas is all dazed, and he has hardly spoken to me.” “From about the feast of Blessed Nicholas he has been in this state,” Reginald told her, “and since then he has written nothing.”70

At her castle he was caught up out of himself for a long time, as if his senses had left him. “The master is often caught up in spirit when he is contemplating something,” Reginald said, “but I have never seen him out of his senses for so long.” At last Reginald went to him and pulled hard at his cape, as if to wake him from sleep. Saint Thomas sighed.71

Reginald would not let it rest. Again and again he asked why his master would not write, and what had happened to him. At last Saint Thomas answered him:72

“I charge you by the living, almighty God, by the faith you owe our order, and by the love that binds you to me. Tell no one in my lifetime what I am going to tell you. Everything I have written seems to me like straw, compared with what I have seen and what has been shown to me.”

— Saint Thomas Aquinas to Reginald of Piperno, December 127373

He said one thing more. “From this I hope in God that, as my teaching has come to an end, so the end of my life will come soon.”74

He did not say what he had seen. All his life he had asked God for wisdom, and he had written down what he was given. In the same chapel he had asked for no reward but the Lord himself. Now he had been given more than any book could hold.75

His sister was left in great sorrow. Saint Thomas went back to Naples. Soon Blessed Pope Gregory X summoned him. The pope was calling a great council at Lyon in France, and he wanted Saint Thomas there. He set out with Reginald, carrying his treatise against the errors of the Greeks. On the road he wrote nothing at all.76

Saint Thomas Falls Ill on the Road

Long before, Saint Thomas had asked God in prayer never to love earthly things and never to change his state in life. The answer had come that he never would. On the road to Lyon that answer was put to the test.77

The travelers came down from the city of Teano toward Borgonuovo. A tree had fallen across the road. Saint Thomas struck his head against it and was almost stunned. He had always felt pain more sharply than most men. Reginald ran to him, and so did the dean of Teano and the dean's nephew. “Are you hurt?” Reginald asked. “A little.”78

Reginald tried to lift his spirits. “Sir, you are going to the council, and many good things will come of it for the whole Church, for our order, and for the kingdom of Sicily.” “God grant that good things come of it,” Saint Thomas said. Then Reginald went further. “You and Brother Bonaventure will be made cardinals, and you will raise up your orders.” Saint Bonaventure was a famous Franciscan teacher.79

“Reginald,” Saint Thomas answered, “in no state can I be as useful to our order as in this one.” “Father, I do not say this for you, but for the common good.” Saint Thomas cut him off. “Reginald, be sure of this. I will never change my state.”80

They went on toward Rome. At Maenza, the castle of his niece Francesca and her husband, he fell ill and took to his bed. He lost all wish to eat, and he could not taste any food. The prior of the Cistercian abbey of Fossanova, about six miles away, came to visit him with three of his monks and stayed four or five days.81

He grew worse. “If the Lord wants to visit me,” he said, “it is better that I am found in a house of religious than in the houses of lay people.” There was no house of his own order nearby, so he asked to be carried to the monks of Fossanova.82

Saint Thomas Dies Among the Monks

It was Lent of 1274. Fossanova was an abbey of Cistercian monks, an order different from his own. The abbot and the monks had invited him, and he had hoped to rest there a few days and then go on. He came in weak and ill. First he went into the church and prayed before the altar. Then he held on to a doorpost and spoke the words of a psalm to Reginald and the monks: “This is my rest for ever. Here I will stay, for I have chosen it.” His friars heard in the words that he knew he was dying, and they began to weep.83

He lay ill there for many days, and he bore it patiently. They had put him in the abbot's own room. The monks would not let animals carry wood for such a man, so they went to the forest themselves and carried the logs to his room with their own hands. Every time Saint Thomas saw them come in with the wood, he rose with great reverence. “Why is it given to me that holy men should bring me wood?”84

Some of the monks asked him to leave them something to remember his learning by. Weak as he was, he explained the Song of Songs to them in a few words. So his long study of wisdom ended where it had always been going, in the song of love between the soul and God.85

Reginald wept, because he knew his master was dying. He told his master one cause of his sadness. He had hoped that the council would raise Saint Thomas to some great office, for the honor of the order and of the saint's family. Then Saint Thomas at last gave the reason he had kept to himself for years.86

“Do not be troubled about this, son. I asked it of God among my other desires, and, thanks be to God, I received it: that he would take me out of this life, unworthy as I am, in this low rank, and that no high office would ever change me. I could have gone further in knowledge and been of more use to others in my teaching. But by a revelation made to me, it pleased God to lay silence on my teaching, when, as you know, it pleased him to show me the secret of a higher knowledge. So God has given me more than he gave other teachers who stayed longer in this life: that I should leave this mortal life sooner than they, and enter eternal life comforted. So be comforted, son, for I am comforted in all things.”

— Saint Thomas Aquinas to Reginald at Fossanova, as recorded by William of Tocco87

He told Reginald that he had received everything he had asked of God. He had asked that no love of any earthly thing would stain the purity of his mind, and that no high office would lift him out of his low state. And he had asked to know where the soul of his brother Rainald had gone, after so hard and cruel a death. God had heard him in all of it.88

Then he grew very weak, and he asked for the Body of Christ as food for his road out of this life. The abbot and the monks brought it with reverence. Many Dominicans and Franciscans had gathered, and the whole community of the abbey was there. Saint Thomas threw himself to the ground, weak in body and strong in mind, and went to meet his Lord with tears. The priest asked him the question that every dying Christian was asked. Did he believe that this Host was the true Son of God, born of the Virgin, who hung on the cross, died for us, and rose on the third day? He answered in a clear voice and with tears: “If a greater knowledge than faith can be had of this sacrament in this life, I answer in that knowledge. I believe it in truth and I know it for certain: this is true God and true man.”89

Then he received the Body of Christ, and as he received it he spoke:90

“I receive you, the price of my soul's redemption. I receive you, the food for my journey. For love of you I studied, kept watch, and worked. I preached you and taught you. I never said anything against you. If I said anything wrong, I said it in ignorance, and I do not hold stubbornly to my own opinion. If I said anything wrong about this sacrament or about other things, I leave it all to the correction of the holy Roman Church, in whose obedience I now pass from this life.”

— Saint Thomas Aquinas at his last Holy Communion, Fossanova, March 1274, as recorded by William of Tocco91

He lived three days after that Holy Communion. Then he asked for the last anointing, which the Church gives to the dying. He answered every prayer of the rite himself. Early in the morning of 7 March 1274 he gave back his spirit to God, in his forty-ninth year. He went out of his body gladly.92

That same day Saint Albert was far to the north in Cologne. He was sitting at table with the prior and some of the friars. Suddenly he began to weep. The prior asked him why. “Brother Thomas of Aquino, my son in Christ, who was the light of the Church, has passed from this light.” The prior marked the day. Later they learned that Saint Thomas had died on that very day.93

The Franciscan bishop of Terracina came for the funeral with some of his friars. Many Dominicans came too, and nobles of the countryside. About a hundred monks and lay brothers of the abbey were there. His niece Francesca did not dare to enter the monastery, so she asked the abbot to have the bier carried to its gate. There she and two other women mourned over him with all the grief of family love.94

A Dominican who had long been his confessor preached to them. “I heard this holy man's general confession,” he said, “and I bear witness that I found him as pure as a child of five.”95

The Monks Find Saint Thomas's Body Whole

They buried him before the high altar of the abbey church. But the abbot was afraid. The body had been left with the monastery only in trust, and he feared that the Dominicans would come with an order from the pope and take it away. So he had it moved in secret to the chapel of Saint Stephen, near the cloister.96

Soon the monks were saying openly that Saint Thomas had come to the prior in a dream with a plain request: “Bring me back to my first place.”97

About seven months after his death the abbot took the keys of the chapel. He called two monks from another house and some lay helpers, and they opened the tomb with iron tools. With them was Peter, the monk who had served Saint Thomas in his last days.98

“When the tomb was opened such a sweet smell came out that it filled the whole chapel and the cloister. His clothes were still sound and whole, and the whole body was whole and unharmed, except that a small crack showed at the tip of the nose.”

— Peter of Monte San Giovanni, sworn testimony at the Naples inquiry, 131999

The smell was like many spices, and monks who had not been called came running. The body had been large and heavy, and the grave was wet and deep, yet it lay whole in its robe, cloak, and hood. They carried him back into the church. No one had planned what came next. The monk who led the singing began the chant for a saint. The community sang the Mass of a confessor, the Mass for a saint who was not a martyr, instead of the Mass for the dead. Then the same monk began the words: “The mouth of the just man will speak wisdom.” So the monks brought him back to his first place, near the high altar.100

Others wanted him too. In the spring of 1274 the masters of arts at the University of Paris wrote to the Dominicans gathered at Lyon. They called Saint Thomas “the morning star, outstanding in the world.” They begged for his bones as the greatest gift the order could give them. No city but Paris should keep them, they wrote. Paris had raised him and been fed by his teaching. They asked too for the books he had left unfinished. The monks of Fossanova kept him.101

Reginald went back to Naples, where he taught the friars. He took up his own lessons where he had left them. Then he spoke to the friars, weeping hard. “Brothers, my master forbade me in his lifetime to reveal the wonders I saw in him.” Now he told them. Saint Thomas had gained his learning not by human talent but by prayer. Before he studied, debated, lectured, wrote, or dictated, he went apart to pray. With many tears he prayed that God would show him his secrets in truth. He went to prayer in doubt, and he came back taught.102

The words about straw Reginald kept to himself. He had sworn an oath, and he kept it for years. He spoke those words only in his last illness, in his final confession to an old friar of Anagni.103

In the fourteenth year after his death, that same Peter was abbot of Fossanova. Saint Thomas's sister Theodora, the Countess of San Severino, asked him for her brother's right hand as a relic. He promised it. The tomb was opened again, and again a great fragrance came out. The hand was cut off and brought to her. She received it with devotion and tears, and she had it kept with great care.104

The Church Makes Saint Thomas Its Teacher

In 1303 Reginald's secret reached Bartholomew of Capua, a royal official of Naples. He told it to Blessed Pope Benedict XI, who heard it with great joy. About 1317 the Dominicans sent William of Tocco to the pope's court with the record of the miracles, to ask Rome to declare Saint Thomas a saint.105

Pope John XXII heard the friars at Avignon. “We believe that Brother Thomas is a glorious saint in heaven,” he told them, “because his life was holy and his teaching could not exist without a miracle.” After inquiries at Naples and Fossanova, he declared Saint Thomas a saint at Avignon on 18 July 1323. He set the feast on 7 March, the day of the saint's death.106

In 1368 Blessed Pope Urban V gave the saint's body to the Dominicans, and he ordered the University of Toulouse to follow Saint Thomas's teaching as true and Catholic. On 28 January 1369 the relics were carried into Toulouse, with Louis the Duke of Anjou at the head of the crowd. In 1567 Pope Saint Pius V named Saint Thomas a Doctor of the Church. In 1969 the Church moved his feast to 28 January, the day his relics came to Toulouse.107

In 1879 Pope Leo XIII looked back over six centuries. He wrote that in the great universities, from Paris and Salamanca to Naples and Coimbra, “Thomas reigned supreme.” The next year he made Saint Thomas the patron of every Catholic university, college, and school.108

Leo remembered one more honor. When the bishops of the Church met at the Council of Trent, they laid the Summa on the altar, beside the Scriptures and the decrees of the popes. It was the book Saint Thomas had left unfinished. It still stops in the pages on Penance.109

A Note on the Sources

The oldest Lives of Saint Thomas were written by Peter Calo, William of Tocco, and Bernard Gui between about 1300 and 1330. Beside them stands the sworn testimony of the Naples inquiry of 1319. They do not always agree. Tocco says his mother held him prisoner; Bartholomew of Capua swore that it was his father. Only Ptolemy of Lucca names Rainald among the brothers who seized him. Tocco gives the captivity as almost two years; Thomas of Cantimpré, writing in Saint Thomas's lifetime, says two or three, and Gerard de Frachet about one. Tocco says his mother allowed the escape; Ptolemy of Lucca says Saint Thomas arranged it himself. The sources disagree on the year he entered the order and on the friar who gave him the robe. The stories of the dumb ox and the talking crucifix appear only in the Lives, not in the sworn testimony. Saint Albert's words about the ox are given in the Lives with “it is said.” Tocco and Gui name the sacristan of the crucifix story Dominic of Caserta; Calo calls him James of Caserta. Ptolemy names the sick companion at Molara as Raymund, where Gui names Reginald. The vision of the dead sister is told by Tocco, who says “it is said,” and by Gui; Gerard de Frachet told a version before 1260. None of them names the sister. The year of Rainald's death is not given; Prümmer thinks it was probably 1248.

The saying about straw comes from Reginald through an old friar of Anagni and then Bartholomew of Capua. Tocco and Gui tell the day at San Severino without the word straw, and only Tocco gives the hope of an early death. Gui calls the Countess of San Severino Theodora, and Tocco names Theodora as the sister who received the hand; Laurent's notes name the countess Mary. Peter the monk says Saint Thomas lived three days after his last Communion; Tocco suggests a shorter time. Tocco names Reginald as the funeral preacher, where other witnesses say only a Dominican who had long been his confessor. Tocco says the dream about the first place came to the abbot; Abbot Nicholas heard that it came to the prior. Abbot Nicholas and Bartholomew say Saint Thomas asked to be carried to Fossanova; Tocco says the monks invited him; Peter says he rode there with the prior. Saint Albert's tears at Cologne are told by Gui. Tocco and Ptolemy say Saint Thomas wrote the office of Corpus Christi, and modern scholars think this is probable; they say the same of the Adoro te devote. The crowd at Toulouse is described by Conway and Butler, not by a writer of the time. The sources do not give the date of Saint Louis's dinner, of the night over Isaiah, or of the crucifix scene.

How This Life Was Researched

This life rests on twenty-six sources in four languages: Latin, English, French, and Italian. Its core is the Latin record. That record holds the sworn depositions of the Naples inquiry of 1319 and the three Lives by Calo, Tocco, and Gui. It also holds the Church History of Ptolemy of Lucca, who studied under Saint Thomas. Quotations from Saint Thomas's own works were checked against the Latin text at Corpus Thomisticum. Quotations from the witnesses and the Lives were checked against the printed Latin of the Fontes edition. For this telling, the Latin of the main scenes was read again. Eleven popular sayings credited to Saint Thomas were also checked. Those that could not be traced to him are left out.
