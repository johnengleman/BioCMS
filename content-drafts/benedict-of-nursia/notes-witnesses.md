## Identity facts from S1/S1n
- Gregory wrote the Dialogues in 593, "three years after his elevation to the papacy", at the request of monks of his household [S1n intro p.xx]. Gregory letter to Maximianus of Syracuse asks for miracle stories of the Italian Fathers [S1n].
- Gardner dates Monte Cassino founding c. 528/529 and Benedict's death 543 (21 March 543; Scholastica 10 Feb 543, "according to the tradition") [S1n intro p.xxi; notes ch.33]. NOTE: Gregory himself gives NO year for the death, the founding, or any date. Modern date 547 not in S1; check.
- Gardner: Book II is "the earliest and most authoritative account of St. Benedict that we possess" [S1n].

## The four witnesses (Gregory's own words, S1 prologue, 1608 English)
- "All the notable things and acts of his life I could not learn; but those few, which I mind now to report, I had by the relation of four of his disciples: to wit, of Constantinus, a most rare and reverent man, who was next Abbot after him; of Valentinianus, who many years had the charge of the Lateran Abbey; of Simplicius, who was the third General of his order; and lastly of Honoratus, who is now Abbot of that monastery in which he first began his holy life." [S1 p.51]
- Honoratus is the named source of the Rome-will-decay prophecy; Gregory adds he did not hear it from Benedict himself but from monks who did: "Honoratus, by whose relation I had this, saith not that he received it from his own mouth, but that he had it of other monks, which did hear it themselves." [S1 ch.15 p.75]
- Other named informants in Book II: Valentinian's brother (ch.13, a layman, who ate on the road); Anthony, "honourable man", whose father's boy was cured of leprosy (ch.26); Peregrinus, "one of his disciples" (money found, ch.27); Gregory says the first twenty-odd miracles come from the four, then "such things as I had from the mouth of his own scholars" [S1 ch.27 p.88]. Exhilaratus "our monk, a lay-brother, whom you know" (ch.18) = a person of Gregory's own circle.
- Lombard note: Monte Cassino destroyed by Lombards of Benevento in 589 per Gardner [S1n]; Gregory says "not long since" the monks were spoiled but none killed (ch.17) — this is how the witnesses' chain (Constantine, Simplicius, Valentinian, Honoratus) reached Rome: displaced Cassino monks, Lateran abbey [S1 p.79].

## Timeline (Gregory, Book II; chapter numbers as in the 1608/1911 text)
- Intro: born in the province of Nursia, "of honourable parentage", brought up at Rome in the study of "humanity" (letters). Saw many fall to dissolute life through learning, "drew back his foot", left book, father's house and wealth. Gregory: "instructed with learned ignorance, and furnished with unlearned wisdom." [S1 p.51]
- Ch.1: At "Enside" (Effide/Affile), church of St. Peter, with a group of devout men; his nurse (only she followed him) borrowed a sieve, it broke; Benedict prayed and found it whole. Townsmen hung it over the church door; "even to these very troubles of the Lombards" it hung there. [S1 p.52] NEW to many English pages (nurse still at his side). Marked: miracle report.
- Ch.1: Flees from the nurse to Sublacum (Subiaco), "distant almost forty miles from Rome"; a lake from a spring. Meets a monk Romanus on the way, who keeps the secret, gives habit, serves him. [S1 p.53]
- Ch.1: Three years in a "strait cave" unknown to all but Romanus (monk under Abbot Theodacus), who stole hours and bread, lowered it by a long rope with a little bell; "the old enemy" threw a stone and broke the bell. [S1 p.53-54]
- Ch.1: Easter. A priest far off, told by the Lord in a vision "Thou hast provided good cheer for thyself, and my servant in such a place is afflicted with hunger", finds him in the cave on Easter day. Benedict did not know it was Easter: "I know that it is Easter with me and a great feast, having found so much favour at God's hands as this day to enjoy your company." [S1 p.54] NEW? vivid.
- Ch.1: Shepherds see him in skins, "thought it had been some beast"; converted; his fame spreads. [S1 p.54]
- Ch.2: Temptation: a little black bird (merle/ousel) flies at his face; memory of a woman he had once seen; he strips and rolls in briers and nettles: "by the wounds of his body, he cured the wounds of his soul". After that "he never felt any such thing." Reported by Benedict himself to his disciples. [S1 p.55]
- Ch.3: Monks of a neighbouring monastery (abbot dead) beg him to rule them. He refuses: "their manners were divers from his". He gives in. He enforces regular life. Monks mix poison in his wine. He makes the sign of the cross over the glass; it breaks "as though the sign of the cross had been a stone thrown against it". His reply, "with a mild countenance and quiet mind": "Almighty God have mercy upon you, and forgive you: why have you used me in this manner? Did not I tell you before hand, that our manner of living could never agree together?" Returns to the wilderness. [S1 p.57] Gregory's gloss: he "dwelt with himself" (habitare secum). Gregory does not name the monastery (tradition says Vicovaro: not in S1; check).
- Ch.3 end: Gregory's reasoning that a saint may leave a hopeless community; cites Paul let down in a basket at Damascus [S1 p.59-60].
- Ch.3 end: Twelve monasteries at Subiaco, twelve monks each, "and a few he kept with himself". Roman nobles bring sons: Evitius gives Maurus, Tertullus the senator gives Placidus ("a boy of tender years") [S1 p.60-61].
- Ch.4: Monk who cannot stay at prayer, drawn out by the skirt by "a little black boy" only Benedict (then Maurus after two days) can see; Benedict strikes him with a wand; cured. Pompeianus is father of that abbey. [S1 p.61-62]
- Ch.5: Three mountain monasteries lack water; Benedict climbs at night with Placidus, prays, lays three stones; water springs from the rock. [S1 p.62-63]
- Ch.6: A poor-in-spirit Goth cleaning briers by the lake loses the iron head of a bill; Benedict puts the handle in the water and the iron rises: "Behold here is thy bill again, work on, and be sad no more." (Goth = Zalla, per Gardner note ch.31.) [S1 p.63-64] Marked: miracle.
- Ch.7: Placidus falls in the lake, carried "so far as one may shoot an arrow"; Benedict, in his cell, knows; sends Maurus, who runs on the water and drags the boy by the hair; Placidus says he saw the abbot's garment on his own head. Benedict credits Maurus's obedience, Maurus credits the command. [S1 p.64-65]
- Ch.8: Florentius, priest of a nearby church ("grandfather to Florentius our sub-deacon", i.e. a man Gregory knew), envies him, sends a poisoned loaf. Benedict commands the tame crow that took bread from his hand daily: "In the name of Jesus Christ our Lord, take up that loaf, and leave it in some such place where no man may find it." The crow hops about, protests, finally takes it; returns after three hours for his usual bread. Florentius then sends seven naked young women to dance in the monastery yard to tempt the young monks. Benedict, "fearing the danger", leaves with a few monks: "he gave place to envy". Florentius is killed when his chamber collapses; Maurus sends word, Benedict weeps; he gives Maurus a penance for rejoicing. Gregory's pupil Peter lists the Old Testament parallels (Moses, Elisha, Peter, Elijah, David). [S1 p.65-67] KEY SCENE (Subiaco to Cassino turn). Note Gregory's "ten miles" detail: Benedict was "scarce ten miles off".
- Ch.8 end: Moves to Cassino: "the town, which is called Cassino, standeth upon the side of an high mountain", an ancient chapel where country people "worshipped the god Apollo". He "beat in pieces the idol, overthrew the altar, set fire on the woods", built oratory of St. Martin in the temple and of St. John where the altar was; preached to the people. [S1 p.68] Gregory dates nothing. The devil appears visibly to Benedict: "Blessed Bennet", then "Cursed Bennet, and not blessed: what hast thou to do with me?" monks hear voice but do not see [S1 p.68-69].
- Ch.9: stone that cannot be moved (devil sits on it); Benedict blesses it [S1 p.69]. Ch.10: idol of brass dug up; phantom kitchen fire; "bless your eyes" [S1 p.69-70]. Ch.11: wall falls, kills a little monk, "son of a certain courtier"; brought in a sack; Benedict prays in his cell, boy restored and sent back to work [S1 p.70-71].
- Ch.12: Benedict knows monks ate out at a religious woman's house against the rule; his words: "Why do you tell an untruth? for did you not go into such a woman's house? and eat such and such kind of meat, and drink so many cups?" [S1 p.71]
- Ch.13: Valentinian's brother (a layman who fasted until he reached the abbey) eats on the road; Benedict names the lapse [S1 p.72-73] (read only the start).
- Ch.14: TOTILA. In the time of the Goths, Totila, hearing that Benedict had the spirit of prophecy, sent word ahead of his coming. He tested him: dressed his guardsman Riggo in the king's shoes and royal robes with three attendants (Vultericus, Rudericus, Blindinus). Benedict, seated a little way off, as Riggo approached: "Put off, my good son, put off that apparel, for that which thou hast on, is none of thine." Riggo and the escort fell flat, fled back to the king. [S1 p.73-74]
- Ch.15: Totila comes himself, lies on the ground; Benedict, asked three times, raises him with his own hands. Words: "Much wickedness do you daily commit, and many great sins have you done: now at length give over your sinful life. Into the city of Rome shall you enter, and over the sea shall you pass: nine years shall you reign, and in the tenth shall you leave this mortal life." Totila "was nothing so cruel as before"; Gregory adds the fulfilment: he went to Rome, sailed to Sicily, lost kingdom and life in his tenth year. [S1 p.74-75] (Gregory's "prophecy" is told after the event, c. 50 years later; Gregory's evidence is Honoratus and monks.)
- Ch.15: Bishop of Camisina (Gregory's text; Gardner normally "Canusium") says Totila will leave Rome uninhabited. Benedict: "Rome shall not be utterly destroyed by strangers: but shall be so shaken with tempests, lightnings, whirlwinds, and earthquakes, that it will fall to decay of itself." Gregory: "we see before our eyes in this very city" [S1 p.75]. 
- Ch.16: Priest of Aquinum possessed; martyrs' shrines did not heal him, "to the end that the world might know what great grace was in the servant of God, Bennet"; healed with a ban on meat and orders; he later took orders and died possessed [S1 p.75-76]. Bishop Constantius of Aquinum (commemorated 1 Sept per Gardner).
- Ch.17: Benedict found weeping bitterly to Theoprobus (convert, familiar of Benedict): "All this Abbey which I have built... are by the judgment of almighty God delivered to the gentiles, to be spoiled and overthrown: and scarce could I obtain of God to have their lives spared, that should then live in it." Gregory: "we see them to be proved most true": the Lombards sacked the abbey by night when monks slept, none captured [S1 p.78-79].
- Ch.18: Exhilaratus hid a flagon of wine in a bush; Benedict: "Take heed, my son, that thou drinkest not of that flagon which thou hast hidden in the bush: but first be careful to bow it down, and thou shalt find what is within it." A snake leaps out. [S1 p.79] HUMOR.
- Ch.19: monk takes handkerchiefs from nuns he preached to: "How cometh it to pass, brother, that sin is entered into your bosom?" [S1 p.79-80]
- Ch.20: proud thought of the candle-holding monk (son of a great man): "Sign your heart, brother, for what is it that you say? Sign your heart." [S1 p.80-81]
- Ch.21: Famine in Campania, five loaves left; Benedict: "Indeed, to-day some want there is, but to-morrow you shall have plenty"; 200 bushels of meal at the door, "by whom, or what means, that is unknown to this very day" [S1 p.81].
- Ch.22: Terracina (Tarracina) monastery site shown by vision in sleep to the abbot and prior; "Did not I appear to either of you in your sleep" [S1 p.82-83].
- Ch.26-29: leper boy healed (Anthony's father's boy); Peregrinus's twelve shillings, 13 found; poison-skin cured by touch; oil in glass thrown from window not broken (sub-deacon Agapitus), barrel of oil overflowing [S1 p.87-90].
- Ch.30: Devil as a physician on a mule with horn and mortar going to "give them a drench" [S1 p.90]. Ch.31: Zalla the Goth bound by a farmer, loosed by Benedict's sight [S1 p.91] (read only start). Ch.32: countryman brings dead son to gate, cries "Give me my son, give me my son!" Benedict: "Away, my good brethren, away: such miracles are not for us to work, but for the blessed Apostles: why will you lay such a burthen upon me, as my weakness cannot bear?" He stretches on the child and prays: "Behold not, O Lord, my sins, but the faith of this man..." Child revives, trembles [S1 p.92-93].
- Ch.33: SCHOLASTICA. Sister "dedicated from her infancy to our Lord", visits once a year; they meet "not far from the gate, to a place that did belong to the Abbey" with a few monks, spend the day "in the praises of God and spiritual talk". At night she begs him to stay; he refuses: "that he might not by any means tarry all night out of his Abbey". Sky clear. She joins hands on the table, bows head; tears; thunder and rain break the instant she lifts her head. Benedict: "God forgive you, what have you done?" She: "I desired you to stay, and you would not hear me, I have desired our good Lord, and he hath vouchsafed to grant my petition: wherefore if you can now depart, a God's name return to your monastery, and leave me here alone." They talk all night. Gregory: "she did more which loved more." [S1 p.93-95]
- Ch.34: three days later Benedict, standing in his cell, sees her soul "in the likeness of a dove" ascend; has her body brought to his own prepared grave: "as their souls were always one in God whiles they lived, so their bodies continued together after their death." [S1 p.96] Gregory gives no year and no 10 Feb date.
- Ch.35: VISION. Servandus (deacon-abbot of the monastery founded by Liberius in Campania) visits; they sleep in a tower with one stair; Benedict rises before matins, prays at the window; sees a light in the dead of night "far more clear than the light of the day". "The whole world, gathered as it were together under one beam of the sun, was presented before his eyes" and he saw the soul of Germanus, Bishop of Capua, in a fiery globe carried up by angels. He calls Servandus twice or thrice; Servandus sees only "a little remnant of the light". Theoprobus sends a messenger to Capua that night: Germanus died at that very moment. [S1 p.96-97] Gregory's explanation to Peter: "all creatures be as it were nothing to that soul which beholdeth the Creator" [S1 p.98]. Source of the report: "as himself did afterward report" (Benedict told it). Witness Servandus saw the remnant of light.
- Ch.36: Gregory on the Rule: "he wrote a rule for his monks, both excellent for discretion and also eloquent for the style." Only mention of the Rule in early sources checked so far. [S1 p.99]
- Ch.37: DEATH. "The same year in which he departed this life, he told the day of his holy death to his monks", told those present to keep it secret and gave absent ones a sign. "Six days before he left this world, he gave order to have his sepulchre opened", fell into a fever; on the sixth day carried to the oratory, received the body and blood of Christ, "having his weak body holden up betwixt the hands of his disciples, he stood with his own lifted up to heaven, and as he was in that manner praying, he gave up the ghost." Two monks, one in his cell and one far away, had the same vision: a road from his cell eastward to heaven hung with tapestry and lamps; a man: "This is the way by which the beloved servant of God, Bennet, is ascended up to heaven." Buried in the oratory of St. John Baptist; his cave (Subiaco) still works miracles. [S1 p.99-100] NOTE: Gregory does not name the year, the day, or the month.
- Ch.38: mad woman cured by sleeping in his cave "lately" [S1 p.100].

## Character (from S1)
- Old mind in young body; flees honours; prefers labour to praise (ch.1). Mild in anger (poison: "mild countenance and quiet mind"). Weeps over the death of his enemy and sets penance on the monk who rejoiced (ch.8). Sharp with hidden faults: wand, "Sign your heart", flagon. Humble: refuses the dead-child miracle. Strict about the rule: will not sleep a night outside the abbey. Gives away all stock in famine (oil). Grief in prophecy: weeps over the future sack of Cassino (ch.17).
- Gregory repeatedly says Benedict did some things by prayer and some by power (ch.30).

## What Gregory's sources can and cannot bear
- Date and purpose: written 593, finished late 593 or published 594 (Dudden argues from Gregory's letter to Maximianus of summer 593, and Dial. iv.47 "three years" after John's burial; a slip about the 589 flood "nearly five years" points to 594) [S4 vol1 p.321 n.]. Gregory wrote "at the request of certain monks of his household" [S1n]. Dudden's view of aim: edification and comfort in the Lombard years, plus a glorification of the Catholic faith [S4 vol1 p.323].
- Strengths: named witnesses, still living, within about 50 years of the events; Gregory tells what he thinks sound [S4 vol1 p.337-338]. Gardner: "the earliest and most authoritative account" [S1n]. CE: "the only authentic life" [S2].
- Limits: Gregory states he got only "those few" things from four disciples and could not learn "all the notable things" [S1 p.51]. Dudden: "a few years would be insufficient" argument reversed; sixth-century people were "predisposed in favour of the miraculous", so legend grew quickly; and "the legends of the Dialogues will scarcely pass as strict records of fact", but mirror customs, manners and beliefs of sixth-century Italians [S4 vol1 p.338, 344] (paraphrase; check wording before quoting).
- Dudden on the Cassino years: "doubtful and obscure, dimly discernible through a haze of legend" (about fifteen years) [S4 vol2 p.167]. (Quote copied from OCR; fits his words "The life of Benedict at Monte Cassino, covering a period of some fifteen years, is doubtful and obscure, dimly discernible through a haze of legend.")
- Gregory's text shows Gregory's own moral commentary threaded through (Peter's questions): e.g. "habitare secum" (dwelling with himself), power versus prayer in miracles, the vision of the world explained by "all creatures be as it were nothing to that soul which beholdeth the Creator". The interpretive layers belong to Gregory (593), not Benedict's disciples.
- Gregory gives no year for anything: no birth, Subiaco, Cassino, Totila visit, or death. All modern dates are inference. Britannica 1911: "the only fixed chronological point is a visit of the Gothic king Totila to him in 543, when Benedict was already established" [S3]; Dudden: probably during Totila's southern march, Naples fell 543 [S4 vol1 p.~230]. NOTE: Gregory's own text implies the visit came before Totila took Rome (Rome fell 17 Dec 546 per Dudden vol1 p.~245).
- Death date conflict: 21 March 543 (Gardner, CE, Guérin, Vatican DB per Wikipedia catalog "480-543"); c.544 (Britannica 1911); 547 (modern popular, Wikipedia, osb.org per search snippet; not verified in a source I read). Guérin's footnote: by the paschal cycle, in 543 Easter was 5 April, so the tradition "Holy Saturday, 21 March 543" does not fit; some authors say he died on the Saturday before Passion Sunday (21 March 543), others 24 April [S5 note on p.584]. Gregory's text has no day, no Holy Saturday. "Holy Saturday" and "Maur in Gaul" are late additions.
- Scholastica: Dudden "543 or 544", "a few months before" Benedict's death [S4 vol2 p.168]; Gregory only says Benedict saw her soul three days after the storm night. Gardner "tradition: 10 February 543". Twin-sister: CE says tradition accepted by Bede [S2]; Gregory only says "his sister". Convent at Plumbariola (Dudden: "it is supposed") [S4 vol2 p.168].
- Parents: tradition names Euproprius and Abundantia and an Anician family; Gregory says only "honourable parentage" (liberiori genere); Dudden finds no sound proof [S4 vol1 p.~25 and vol2 p.~165]. Marked late/legendary. 
- Age at leaving Rome: CE argues he was at least 19-20, not 14 [S2]. 480 birth is inference (Dudden "about the year 480").
- Founding Cassino: Gardner "528 or 529" [S1n]; Dudden "about the year 529" [S4 vol2 p.166]; Wikipedia 530. Dates inferred, not in Gregory.
- Vicovaro: Gregory does not name the monastery with the poisoners; tradition and Dudden/CE identify it with Vicovaro ("identified by some with Vicovaro" [S2]; Dudden calls it "bad communities, like that of Vicovaro" [S4 vol1 p.346]; Guérin text says "retour de Vicovaro à Sublac" [S5 p.592]).
- Lombard sack: Gardner dates Cassino's destruction 589 (Lombards of Benevento) [S1n]. Gregory says it was "not long since" and monks escaped [S1 ch.17]. The disciples reached Rome after: Valentinian ran the Lateran abbey; this is the route by which Gregory heard.
- Scholarly doubt on authorship: Huldreich Coccius (1551) first challenged it on style and seriousness [S6]. Francis Clark argued a forger of the 680s; Clark's JEH 1989 response to Paul Meyvaert. Meyvaert's "The Authentic Dialogues of Gregory the Great" (Sacris Erudiri 43, 2004), Adalbert de Vogüé, Moorhead 2003 and Matthew dal Santo defend Gregory; most scholars now accept Gregory as author (from search summaries; names cited in S6/S7; not read in full). 
- Silence of others: "Gregory of Tours says not a word either of Maurus or of the Rule of Benedict" [S4 vol2 p.~246]; Dudden: no sign of Benedict's Rule adopted in Gaul for nearly 100 years [S4]. Maurus in Gaul at Glanfeuil: "much obscurity, and the documents relating to it cannot be relied upon" [S4 vol2 p.~245 n.]. Legend of Maurus carrying Rule to Gaul "the year before Benedict's death" = late (Mabillon AA.SS.OSB saec.1).
- Marcus the monk's poem (Gardner/Dudden n.): legend of a journey to Cassino; Paul the Deacon HL i.26 mentions it [S4 vol2 p.167 n.]. Paul the Deacon not read.

## Later or legendary (mark in the entry)
- Parents' names, Anician descent, twin-hood (Bede), Holy Saturday, Maur in Gaul vision, burial under high altar "a little uncertain" (translated to France per Gardner) [S1n].
- In Guérin: Benedict "tall and well proportioned", dignity mixed with sweetness; Lenten bread and water twice a week; "he is hardly found to have left Monte Cassino twice" [S5 p.584]. Unsourced there. Not in Gregory. Treat as late.

## Scenes (max 10)
1. The sieve at Effide: the nurse weeps over the broken sieve; the boy gathers the pieces and prays [ch.1].
2. The cave and the rope with the bell; Romanus lowers bread; the devil's stone breaks the bell [ch.1].
3. Easter in the cave: the priest finds a man who has lost count of days; "Rise up, brother, and let us dine" [ch.1].

## Miracles (all reported only by Gregory c.593, from disciples; status: recorded in Gregory's text; none "approved" in a process)
- Effide, sieve mended (ch.1) [S1 p.52]; the sieve hung at the church door until the Lombard troubles.
- Subiaco: broken glass at poison (ch.3); water from the mountain (ch.5); iron head rises from lake (ch.6); Maurus walks on water (ch.7); crow carries poisoned loaf (ch.8); possessed monk freed by wand (ch.4).
- Cassino: idol/Apollo shrine cleared (ch.8, not a miracle); immovable stone (ch.9); phantom fire (ch.10); boy crushed by wall restored (ch.11); knows hidden meals (ch.12, 13); Totila's disguise and prophecy (ch.14-15); possessed clergyman of Aquinum (ch.16); foresees the sack (ch.17); snake in the flagon (ch.18); handkerchiefs (ch.19); proud thought (ch.20); 200 bushels of meal (ch.21); Terracina vision (ch.22); leper boy (ch.26); 13 shillings for a 12-shilling debt (ch.27, witness Peregrinus); poison-skin cured (ch.27); oil glass unbroken and barrel overflowing (ch.28-29); devil as doctor and the old monk (ch.30); Zalla the Goth loosed (ch.31); dead child raised (ch.32); Scholastica's storm (ch.33); her soul as a dove (ch.34); world in a ray and Germanus's soul (ch.35); foreknowledge of death date, two monks' vision (ch.37); mad woman cured in the cave (ch.38).
- Afterlife: Gregory: Benedict "in that cave in which he first dwelled, even to this very time, worketh miracles" [S1 p.100].

## Character (more)
- Cannot control the world: Gregory builds ch.33 on "he would have had that thing, which yet he could not". Scholastica wins the contest of wills. 
- Dry humor at others' lapses (snake in flagon; "bless your eyes"; devil with a mortar "to give them a drench").
- Fear for his monks' purity (Florentius's women); leaves rather than fight.
- Gregory on his gift: prophecy not constant: "The spirit of prophecy doth not always illuminate the minds of the prophets" [S1 p.81].
