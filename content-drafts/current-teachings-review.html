The current teachings pages for three saints, as they are in the Directus drafts today.

Francis of Assisi — current teachings page

Francis wrote little, and simply. His Rules, his Testament, a handful of letters, short sayings called Admonitions, and his songs of praise survive. The passages below are our own English translations: the Latin from the 1904 Quaracchi critical edition, and the Canticle from the thirteenth-century Assisi manuscript. The Testament, the letter to Leo, the Praises, and the last will for Clare were checked against page scans of that edition; the other Latin was read in a scanned text. The famous prayer that begins “Lord, make me an instrument of your peace” is not his; it was first printed, unsigned, in Paris in 1912.

What was bitter became sweet

For Francis, conversion began with the thing he could least bear. The Lord “led” him among the lepers, he wrote, and what had been bitter turned into sweetness. His own account puts that change at the root of his new life.

“When I was in sins, it seemed too bitter to me to see lepers. The Lord himself led me among them, and I showed them mercy. And when I left them, what had seemed bitter to me turned into sweetness of soul and body.”

— Francis of Assisi, Testament, 1226 (our translation from the Latin)

He left these words in his Testament, near the end of his life, and put them first, before anything else he wanted his brothers to remember. He lived them: he kissed a leper’s hand on the road, gave alms in the leper house, and near death still wanted to go back and serve the lepers.

The form of the Gospel

Francis believed God had shown him a way of life that was simply the Gospel, lived literally. His brothers were to own nothing, because Christ had made himself poor.

“The Most High himself showed me that I should live according to the form of the holy Gospel. And I had it written in a few words and simply, and the lord pope confirmed it for me.”

— Francis of Assisi, Testament, 1226 (our translation from the Latin)

In the Rule of 1223 he wrote: “The brothers shall make nothing their own, not house, nor place, nor anything. … For the Lord made himself poor for us in this world.” He asked that his words be understood “simply and purely,” as they were written. When he heard the Gospel of the sending of the Twelve, he threw away his shoes, staff, and belt, and he died in a habit that had been lent to him.

Brothers who mother each other

Francis asked his brothers to care for each other with a mother’s tenderness, and he used the same image for every Christian who carries Christ in the heart.

“For if a mother feeds and loves her son in the flesh, how much more carefully should one love and feed his spiritual brother?”

— Francis of Assisi, Rule of 1223, ch. 6 (our translation from the Latin)

In his Letter to All the Faithful he wrote: “We are his mothers when we carry him in heart and body through love and a pure and sincere conscience.” In a note to Brother Leo, written in his own hand, he spoke “as a mother would,” and ended: “if you need it for your soul, or for some other comfort, and you want, Leo, to come to me, come.”

Mercy without limit

Francis could be severe with brothers who refused the Rule, but he asked those in charge to be merciful without condition. He told one minister to take whatever stood in his way, even from the brothers, as grace.

“No brother, however much he has sinned, should leave your presence without mercy, if he asks mercy; if he does not ask, you ask him.”

— Francis of Assisi, Letter to a Minister (our translation from the Latin)

He wrote this to a minister whose name the letter does not keep. On his deathbed he forgave all his brothers, “as well absent as present,” as Celano records.

Patience shows what we are

Francis held that a person’s worth is what he is before God, and that patience is tested only when others oppose us.

“You cannot tell how much patience and humility a servant of God has while everything pleases him. But when the people who should satisfy him do the opposite, he has as much patience and humility as he shows then, and no more.”

— Francis of Assisi, Admonition XIII (our translation from the Latin)

In another Admonition he wrote, “As much as a person is before God, so much he is, and no more.” And in another: “Where there is love and wisdom, there is neither fear nor ignorance. Where there is patience and humility, neither anger nor disturbance. Where there is poverty with joy, neither greed nor avarice.” The true peacemakers, he wrote, are those who keep peace in mind and body, whatever they suffer, for the love of Christ.

Praise through the creatures

Francis saw every creature as a brother or sister, and the whole world as a reason to praise God. He preached to birds and spoke to fire as “brother fire.”

“Praised be my Lord for our sister, mother earth, who sustains and governs us and brings forth many fruits with colored flowers and grass.”

— Francis of Assisi, Canticle of Brother Sun, San Damiano, about 1225 (our translation from the Umbrian Italian)

He made the Canticle, ill and nearly blind, in a hut near San Damiano, in the Italian of Umbria rather than Latin. Its last line sums up what he asked of everyone: “Praise and bless my Lord, give him thanks, and serve him with great humility.” Even death was “our sister, bodily death, from whom no living person can escape.”

Work and simplicity

Francis wanted his brothers to work with their hands, and he told those who could not read not to trouble about learning letters but to long for the Spirit of the Lord.

“And I worked with my own hands, and I want to work.”

— Francis of Assisi, Testament, 1226 (our translation from the Latin)

In the Testament he remembered the first days: “we were uneducated and subject to everyone.” The Rule of 1223 asks that preaching be brief and that brothers who cannot read not trouble to learn. He himself carried stones to rebuild San Damiano and begged for what he ate.

Poverty to the end

Francis wanted to follow the poverty that Christ and his mother had lived, and he asked Clare and her sisters never to leave it, whatever anyone advised them.

“I, little Brother Francis, want to follow the life and poverty of our most high Lord Jesus Christ and of his most holy mother, and to hold to it to the end.”

— Francis of Assisi, last will for Clare and her sisters (our translation from the Latin)

He wrote it shortly before he died, and Clare kept it by copying it into her own Rule. In his Testament he wrote of going back to “the Lord’s table,” begging alms from door to door. On his deathbed he would accept his habit only as a loan.

A servant to everyone

Francis called himself the servant of his brothers and of everyone. He gave up the government of the Order in 1220, saying, “Henceforth I am dead to you,” and promised obedience to the man who took his place.

“I think myself the greatest of sinners, for if God had followed any other wicked man with such great mercy he would have been tenfold more spiritual than I.”

— Francis of Assisi, in Thomas of Celano’s Second Life, 1244–47 (tr. A. G. Ferrers Howell)

He signed his letter to all Christians “brother Francis, their servant and subject,” and his Testament “your little one and servant.” When a brother urged him in his illness to have the Prophets read to him, he answered, “I need no more, my son: I know Christ, the poor man crucified.”

Seraphim of Sarov — current teachings page

Seraphim is not known to have written a book. His teaching survives in the words that people who knew him remembered: the monks of Sarov who wrote the first Lives, the sisters of Diveyevo, and Nikolai Motovilov. A volume of Spiritual Instructions printed under his name in 1863 is mostly a collection of passages from the Church Fathers, so it is not used here. The seven themes below follow his remembered words, with the place and the reason for each. The translations from Russian are our own.

Acquiring the Holy Spirit

The aim of the Christian life, Seraphim taught, is to acquire the Holy Spirit of God. Prayer, fasting, vigils, and good works are good, but they are the means and not the goal. He explained the word “acquire” as a merchant would: worldly people work to make money, and a Christian works to gather a capital of grace that is never lost. Some works are beyond a person’s strength or means, so he urged people to put their effort where it pays best.

“Prayer, fasting, vigil, and every other Christian work, however good they are in themselves, are not the aim of our Christian life merely in the doing of them, though they serve as the necessary means for reaching it.”

— Seraphim to N. A. Motovilov, November 1831, in Motovilov’s account (published 1903)

He said it in a snowy forest clearing near Sarov in November 1831, to Motovilov, who had wanted to know the aim of the Christian life since he was twelve. The conversation survives only in Motovilov’s account, which was published in 1903, and its authenticity is debated. Seraphim’s own life fits it: he spent about thirty years in the forest and in his locked cell in prayer, and then he gave his last years to the people who came to his door.

Prayer anyone can keep

Seraphim held that prayer is open to everyone, “rich and poor, noble and simple, strong and weak, healthy and sick, righteous and sinner.” For lay people he set a short rule. On rising, a Christian should stand before the icons and say the Lord’s Prayer three times, “Rejoice, O Virgin Theotokos” three times, and the Creed once, and do the same at night. Through the day he or she should keep repeating the Jesus Prayer.

“Walking and sitting, working, and standing in church before the service begins, coming in and going out, keep this unceasingly on your lips and in your heart.”

— Seraphim on the Jesus Prayer, in the Life by Archimandrite Seraphim (Chichagov), 1903

The rule is recorded in Chichagov’s Life, written from the memories of the Diveyevo sisters and Motovilov’s notes. Its promise, he said, is peace, purity of spirit and body, and the dwelling of the Holy Spirit. His own rule in his enclosure was far heavier: a Gospel a day, a thousand prostrations, and the Jesus Prayer. In the forest he stood on a stone for a thousand days and nights repeating the publican’s prayer, “God, be merciful to me a sinner.”

Joy and the Easter greeting

Seraphim greeted visitors, in every season, with the Easter greeting, “Christ is risen!”, and called them “my joy.” The people who knew him said they never saw him sad or downcast. One saying remembered in his name joins inner peace to love for others.

“My joy, I beg you, acquire a peaceful spirit, and then thousands of souls around you will be saved.”

— Seraphim to a monk, as given in a later collection of memories of him; not found in the first Lives

The saying appears in a later collection of memories, not in the four early Lives, so its exact wording is less certain than the rest. He lived the joy to the end: a week before his death a visitor found him leaning on his coffin with a lit candle, extremely cheerful, and on his last evening a brother heard him singing the hymns of Easter.

Cutting off vanity

Seraphim treated vanity as the fault to catch first. He called himself “poor Seraphim,” turned praise back to God, and refused credit for healings.

“We must always, in every case, try to cut off the causes of vanity at the very start.”

— Seraphim, in the Life by Hieromonk Sergius (1841)

The Life of 1841, by a monk who knew him for fifteen years, records the saying. When praised, he answered with the psalm, “Not to us, O Lord, not to us, but to Your name give glory.” Around 1796 he begged his spiritual father to refuse for him the post of archimandrite at Alatyr, and he refused to let a portrait be taken: “Who am I, poor Seraphim, that I could allow a portrait to be taken of me?”

Forgiving those who harm us

The sources we read record no saying of his on forgiveness. What follows is the example of his life, not his own doctrine. When three men came to rob him in 1804, he was holding an axe and could have fought. Remembering that all who take the sword shall perish by the sword, he put it down.

“Do what you need to do.”

— Seraphim to the three robbers, 12 September 1804, in the Life by N. V. Elagin (1863)

They beat him nearly to death, and he walked bent for the rest of his life. He begged the abbot not to punish them and said he would rather leave the monastery himself, and later the men came to ask his forgiveness. In the same spirit, a Diveyevo sister remembered, he bore the hostility of some Sarov monks “good-naturedly, even laughed.”

Trust in God in sickness

Seraphim taught the sick to put their faith in God first, and he gave his own prayer along with it.

“I have given myself over to the True Physician of souls and bodies, our Lord Jesus Christ, and to His Most Pure Mother, the Most Blessed Virgin Mary.”

— Prokhor to Abbot Pachomius, 1780, in the Life by Hieromonk Sergius (1841)

He said it as a young novice swollen with dropsy, when his abbot offered a doctor and bleeding, and the Lives say he recovered after receiving Communion and a divine visit. Later, the sick who came to him were asked first whether they believed. To Mikhail Manturov he said that “to one who believes, everything is possible from God … and I, poor Seraphim, will pray,” and to Motovilov, “If you believe, then you are already well!”

The desert and resolve

Seraphim loved the hermit’s life and knew its cost. Monks in a community, he said, fight the enemy as if with doves, but in the desert as if with lions and leopards. When a brother asked why the monks of his day lived less strictly than the ancients, his answer was about the will.

“If we had the resolve, we would live as the fathers who shone in ancient times.”

— Seraphim to a brother of Sarov, in the Life by N. V. Elagin (1863)

His first answer to that brother was, “Because we have no resolve.” He lived in the forest from 1794 to 1810, kept three years of silence, and spent fifteen years in his cell, and in his old age he still carried a sack of stones on his back. When a Diveyevo sister wanted to leave her community, he told her that he had never once, even in thought, left the monastery.

Nicholas of Myra — current teachings page

Nicholas of Myra left no writings, and no sermon or letter of his survives. What follows is the spiritual example of his life as the oldest stories remember it, not his own doctrine. The only words of his that the sources record are the short sayings that his biographers put in his mouth, and those are quoted where they belong.

Giving in secret

In the oldest Life, Nicholas gives his inherited gold to a poor neighbor at night, through a window, and asks only that the father tell no one. The father calls him his savior, “after God,” in Michael’s words. The example is of a gift that seeks no thanks and points beyond the giver. It is why Nicholas became the saint of gifts.

Calling oneself a sinner

Three times in the stories, Nicholas gives his own name with the same word: at the church door when he was chosen bishop, and in the dreams of the emperor and the prefect. Michael records the first.

“Nicholas, a sinner, a servant of your Holiness, O master.”

— Nicholas at the church door, in the Life by Michael the Archimandrite, early ninth century

He said it as he was being made bishop of the chief city of Lycia, and again, in the Praxis, when he appeared to the emperor and ordered him to free three condemned men.

Defending the condemned

The oldest story of Nicholas is about justice. He ran to the place of execution, took the sword from the executioner, and told the governor to his face that bribe money, not the men he blamed, had condemned the innocent. Then, when the governor fell on his knees and the officers pleaded for him, he forgave him. The example joins courage before the powerful with mercy toward the one who did wrong.

Feeding the hungry

When famine came to Myra, Nicholas went to the grain ships at the harbor and asked each to unload a hundred measures for his city, promising to answer for the loss to the treasury at Constantinople. In a sermon attributed to Andrew of Crete he stood among the sailors as one of them and left three gold coins behind. His care took the form of practical help for a whole city.

Coming when called

Michael tells of sailors who called on Nicholas in a storm and found him among them.

“Behold, you have called me, and I am here to help you.”

— Nicholas to the sailors in the storm, in the Life by Michael the Archimandrite

When the same sailors found him later in his church, he told them that their greed and lust invited God’s correction. Rescue and correction went together: he helped them, and then he called them to change their lives.

Sources: Michael the Archimandrite, Life of Saint Nicholas; Praxis de stratelatis; Andrew of Crete, Encomium.
