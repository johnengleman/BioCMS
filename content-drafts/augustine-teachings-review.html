Saint Augustine: Teachings and Influence

On 18 May 2025 Leo XIV preached at the Mass that began his pontificate. He is the first pope from the Order of Saint Augustine. In the first lines of his homily he quoted Saint Augustine: you have made us for yourself, and our heart is restless until it rests in you. The man who wrote that line had been dead for 1,595 years.

Such praise had begun almost at once. In 431 Pope Saint Celestine I wrote to the bishops of Gaul, where some had started to attack the teaching of the bishop who had just died. The pope wrote that Rome had always counted Saint Augustine in its communion. His learning was so great that even earlier popes had ranked him “among the best teachers.”

Saint Augustine shaped how the Western Church speaks about grace. Grace is God’s free help. He held that it comes first, before even the first wish to believe. About a century after his death, popes and a council in Gaul made that teaching the Church’s own. The line runs on through the Council of Trent to the Catechism of today. In the Confessions he also showed the Church a way to look for God inside the self. Benedict XVI said that he left a very deep mark on the cultural life of the West. The Orthodox East came to know his works late, and the Orthodox Church keeps his feast on 15 June.

What Saint Augustine Left Us

Saint Possidius lived with Saint Augustine for about forty years. After Saint Augustine died, he made a list of his books. The list counts at least 1,030 writings that Saint Augustine himself had numbered, with more that he never numbered. Pope Benedict XVI gave that count in 2008 and added what survives today: more than 300 letters and almost 600 sermons. There were once far more sermons, perhaps three or four thousand. Saint Possidius wrote that many sermons were preached in church, then written down and corrected. The great Benedictine edition of 1679 to 1700 counts in another way: 270 letters, 53 of them from other writers, and 363 sermons it holds certainly his.

We can be sure of the main works, and Saint Augustine made that easy. From 426 to 428 he reread his own books in the order he had written them and published his corrections under the title Retractations. The Catholic Encyclopedia of 1907 calls that book a guide “of inestimable price” to how his thought grew. Benedict XVI called it a teaching of sincerity and intellectual humility.

Five works are a good place to start. The Confessions was written about the year 400. It tells his life to God as one long prayer in thirteen books. The City of God took him from 413 to 426. He began it after the Goths sacked Rome in 410, and in twenty-two books it asks what Christians may expect from God and from the state. The Enchiridion is a handbook on faith, hope, and love. He wrote it in 421 at the request of a Roman named Laurentius. Letter 130 is his answer to a widow named Proba. She had asked him to write about prayer. On Christian Doctrine is a treatise on reading Scripture. He began it in 397 and finished it in 426. The Catholic Encyclopedia calls it historically the first true treatise on explaining Scripture.

One great work is harder. On the Trinity runs to fifteen books, and he finished it about the year 420. Part of it was published without his knowledge. In a letter to his friend Saint Evodius he wrote that he was stopping the dictation of it for a time. He said that the books were too demanding and that few could understand them. He hoped for more texts that would help many. Benedict XVI pointed to that letter. He said that Saint Augustine cared more about taking the faith to simple people than about writing lofty works. Yet Saint Augustine finished the hard book as well.

Readers of English can read much of this for free. The old translations in Philip Schaff’s Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers stand on the New Advent website. Benedict XVI said that his sermons became models for other bishops and priests, and that his commentary on the Psalms was read widely in the Middle Ages. He added that copies of the works were made in very great numbers. Hippo was burned in the end. The books outlived the city.

What Saint Augustine Taught About God

God’s Help Comes Before Our First Good Wish

Grace is the Church’s word for God’s free help, given to people who have not earned it. Saint Augustine taught that this help comes first. We do not take the first step and then receive God’s aid. God moves us to take the first step. He prayed it in the Confessions in one line: “Give what you command, and command what you will.”

His reason was the state of the human will. He had lived it. For years he had known what he should do and had not been able to do it. In one of his last books he wrote about Christ’s prayer for Saint Peter.

“The will is prepared by the Lord, and so Christ’s prayer for Peter could not be in vain. When he prayed that Peter’s faith would not fail, what did he ask for? He asked that Peter would have, in his faith, a most free, strong, unconquerable, and lasting will. See how fully the freedom of the will is defended in agreement with the grace of God, and not against it. The human will does not win grace by freedom. It wins freedom by grace, and with it a delightful steadiness and an unbeatable strength to hold out to the end.”

— Saint Augustine, On the Gift of Perseverance, chapter 17, 429 (our translation)

His teaching on grace took shape in two fights. The first was with a teacher named Pelagius. He held that a person can live a good life by the natural power of free will, without the necessary help of grace. A council at Carthage condemned the Pelagians in 412. Pope Saint Innocent I confirmed the condemnation in 417, and Pope Saint Zosimus condemned the Pelagians in 418.

Then a quieter dispute began inside the monasteries. Monks at Hadrumetum in Africa wanted a middle way between Saint Augustine and Pelagius. So did a group at Marseilles led by Cassian. They held that a person’s own goodwill comes first, and that God then rewards it. Saint Prosper of Aquitaine told Saint Augustine about their views. He answered in On the Predestination of the Saints. There he taught that even the first wish for salvation is a gift of God’s grace. In 429 he came back to the question in On the Gift of Perseverance.

In the Retractations Saint Augustine looked back at his early books. He wrote that he had not yet understood this: the first good movement of the will is itself God’s gift. Was the teaching new? The Catholic Encyclopedia of 1907 speaks of the teachings on the Fall, grace, and predestination. In its words he was “the first to put them in a strong light.” It adds that he was the first to bring the Fall, grace, and free will into one account. The Catechism records his opinion on the saving of sinners: the justification of the wicked is a greater work than the creation of heaven and earth. Heaven and earth will pass away, and the salvation of the elect will not.

Every Person Shares the Wound of Adam

Original sin is the Church’s name for the wound that all people inherit from the first man. The Catechism says that Pelagius reduced the harm of Adam’s fault to a bad example. Saint Augustine taught that every person is born in need of the healing that Christ gives. The Catechism says the Church stated this teaching more exactly in the fifth century, under the impulse of his reflections against Pelagianism. The Orthodox writer John Sanidopoulos holds that it was Saint Augustine who formulated the doctrine.

Rome kept the heart of the teaching and gave some of his wording a clearer form. In 1546 the Council of Trent condemned anyone who says that Adam’s sin harmed Adam alone. That council also ruled on the pull toward sin that remains after baptism. In its words, this pull “is of sin, and inclines to sin.” It also said the Church has never called it sin in the strict sense in those who are baptized. The Catholic Encyclopedia says that wording which seemed to make original sin and this pull the same has given way to clearer wording. It adds that the Church did not adopt his severe view that unbaptized children suffer bodily pain. Popes have allowed a departure from him there, and the bull Auctorem fidei of 1794 is its example. The Church reads Saint Augustine with honor, and also with judgment.

A Sinful Priest Cannot Spoil Christ’s Gift

A sacrament is a sign through which Christ acts, such as baptism or the Eucharist. In Saint Augustine’s Africa a rival Church had broken away from the Catholics over this question. The Donatists held that a priest who had sinned gravely could not give true sacraments, and that a church with sinners in it had lost its holiness. Saint Augustine answered that the gift is Christ’s. In a homily on John he said that a proud minister is to be ranked with the devil. Yet Christ’s gift is not spoiled by him, and what flows through him keeps its purity. The Catechism uses his words when it teaches about the ordained minister.

The quarrel came to a head in June 411 at Carthage. A solemn conference met there, with 286 Catholic bishops and 279 Donatist bishops. Saint Augustine and Saint Aurelius of Carthage spoke for the Catholics. In the emperor’s name, a Roman official named Marcellinus decided for them. Saint Augustine did not build this teaching alone. The Catholic Encyclopedia says that Saint Optatus of Mileve had expressed its basis, and that Saint Augustine deepened and completed it. The same source says he corrected and perfected what Saint Cyprian of Carthage had written on the Church. For an ordinary Christian the teaching is a comfort. The baptism you received is Christ’s, whatever the man who poured the water was like.

Believe First, and Then You Will Understand

Saint Augustine held that faith and reason need each other. A person believes in order to understand, and understands in order to believe better. He preached it in one sentence that the Catechism still quotes: “I believe, in order to understand; and I understand, the better to believe.” The reason is the size of God. “If you understood him, it would not be God.” Reason can approach God. It cannot hold him.

His own search had started with a hard question. Where does evil come from? He wrote in the Confessions that he had looked for the source of evil and found no answer. For about nine years he followed the Manichees. Against them he came to teach that every work of God is good, and that moral evil has one source: the free choice of creatures. The Catholic Encyclopedia names his Letter 120 to a man named Consentius among his main works on faith and reason. It quotes the historian Harnack, who called him the first of the Fathers “who felt the need of forcing his faith to reason.”

One God in Three Persons, Not Three Gods

The Trinity is the Christian teaching that the one God is Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. Saint Augustine set down a rule for speaking about it. What the Persons are to each other tells them apart: Father, Son, and Gift. What each is in himself is said of all three together in the singular: God, good, almighty. Pius XI quoted the rule in 1930 and gave its last words: “and yet not three gods.” On the Holy Spirit he taught that the Spirit proceeds first from the Father. Through the Father’s eternal gift of this to the Son, the Spirit also proceeds from the communion of the Father and the Son. The Catechism gives that sentence from the fifteenth book of On the Trinity.

The last books of that work look for likenesses of the Trinity in the human soul. He himself called these comparisons far-fetched and very obscure, the Catholic Encyclopedia says. Orthodox writers say the Greek East knew little of his works until the thirteenth century. Some Orthodox theologians have since disputed his teaching on the Spirit. That story comes later in this essay.

Two Loves Made Two Cities

In 410 the Goths sacked Rome. Many pagans and many Christians said that the Christian God had failed to protect the city. Saint Augustine answered with the City of God. Benedict XVI said its plan is the struggle between two loves. Two loves made two cities. The earthly city is built by love of self to the point of contempt for God. The heavenly city is built by love of God to the point of contempt for self. Benedict XVI said the book answers two questions. What should we expect of God, and what should we not? And what is the relation between politics and the Church?

The book also defines peace. “The peace of all things is the calm of order. Order is the arrangement that gives each thing, equal or unequal, its own place.” Pius XI used the book to describe a Christian ruler. He quoted Saint Augustine’s remark that we do not call Christian emperors happy because their reign was long. The author of the Catholic Encyclopedia article wrote that in this book Saint Augustine, “in a burst of genius,” created the philosophy of history.

What Saint Augustine Taught About Everyday Life

How to Pray When You Do Not Know How

A widow named Proba asked the bishop of Hippo to write to her about prayer. His answer is Letter 130. He wrote that we do not know what to pray for as we ought, as Saint Paul says. He gave that not-knowing a name.

“So there is in us a certain learned ignorance, if I may call it that. It is an ignorance that we learn from the Spirit of God, who helps our weakness.”

— Saint Augustine, Letter 130 to Proba, 15.28 (our translation)

Letter 130 also says that anyone who prays rightly says nothing that the Lord’s Prayer does not already contain. The Catechism uses the same thought in its teaching on the Our Father. In his commentary on the Psalms he said that Christ prays for us as our priest. Christ prays in us as our head, and we pray to him as our God. By this teaching, even a short Our Father leaves out nothing that right prayer asks.

Let Love Be the Root of Every Act

In his homilies on the First Letter of John he gave a short rule for action: love, and do what you want. He meant no general permission. He was speaking about how to correct someone. His example was a father who punishes his son out of love. Set against him is a kidnapper who coaxes a child with caresses in order to sell him. Love may be stern, and a caress may be wicked. What matters is the root. If the root is love, nothing but good can grow from it.

He lived under the same rule at his own table. Two lines of verse were written on it against speaking ill of anyone who was absent. One day some of his closest fellow bishops forgot them. He lost his temper and told them to wipe the lines off the table, or he would get up and leave the meal. Saint Possidius was there. The man who wrote “love, and do what you want” would not let his guests gossip. The two facts belong together.

Sing, but Keep Walking

On the Alleluia he preached a sermon with a picture from the road. Travelers sing to lighten the work of walking. “Sing, but walk,” he said. Comfort your labor by singing, and do not love laziness. Sing and walk. “What does ‘walk’ mean? Make progress, and make progress in the good.” He knew what singing does to a person. In the days after his baptism in Milan he wept at the hymns of the church. He wrote that the truth flowed into his heart with the voices. He taught that joy in the faith is good as long as it keeps you moving.

Hold Money Loosely

Saint Possidius tells how Saint Augustine handled money, and the facts speak louder than a sermon. He never carried the key to the church treasury and never wore its ring. The accounts were read to him once a year. He would not buy a house or land for the church, and he refused gifts that would rob the giver’s heirs. Once a leading man of Hippo asked for the return of a deed of gift and sent a hundred gold coins for the poor to sweeten the request. Saint Augustine sent the deed back unasked, refused the coins, and rebuked him. Saint Possidius also reports what he told his people: he would rather live on their gifts than manage church estates. He offered to hand the estates back, and they never took them. Saint Possidius sat at his table for about forty years, and he gives these words as he remembered them.

A Bishop for You, a Christian with You

The Second Vatican Council quoted one line of his in 1964 to show how a bishop stands with his people. “For you I am a bishop; but with you I am a Christian.” In that sermon he said that what he was for his people frightened him. What he was with them comforted him. The first was a duty and a danger. The second was a grace and salvation. In 391 the people of Hippo seized him in church to make him a priest, and he wept. Saint Possidius says he told his friends that he wept because he saw many dangers ahead. He then asked his bishop for time until Easter to prepare himself “by praying, reading, and weeping.” The office is the danger. The faith he shared with his people is the safety.

Saint Augustine and the God Within

Saint Augustine’s way to God led inward. The Confessions is a prayer in thirteen books. In it he tells God his own life: the pears and the sect and the lie to his mother and the garden in Milan. The Latin word confessio means two things at once, and he meant both. It is the confession of sin and the praise of God. Benedict XVI said in 2008 that the Confessions turn attention to the mystery of the self and to the mystery of God hidden in the self. He called this attention “without precedent.”

The turn inward was how Saint Augustine found God. He wrote of the months in Milan before his baptism: “I entered into my inmost self, with you as my guide.” In Book 10 he looked back over all the years of searching.

“Late have I loved you, beauty so ancient and so new, late have I loved you. You were within, and I was outside, and there I looked for you. ... You were with me, and I was not with you.”

— Saint Augustine, Confessions X.27.38 (our translation)

The inward way did not make him a solitary. He called the Eucharist “O sign of unity! O bond of charity!” and the Catechism keeps those words. In the City of God he described the whole redeemed city, the society of the saints, as one sacrifice offered to God.

What Saint Augustine Changed in His Own Day

Saint Augustine changed the African Church first. On 8 October 393 the bishops of Africa met in a plenary council under Saint Aurelius of Carthage. Saint Augustine was still a priest, and the bishops asked him to speak. His address became the book On Faith and the Creed. The Catholic Encyclopedia says he left his mark on the councils of Carthage in 398, 401, 407, and 419, and on those of Mileve in 416 and 418. African councils wrote to Rome against Pelagius, and Rome agreed. Pope Saint Innocent I praised letters from Saint Augustine and four other bishops as “full of faith.” He also wrote to Saint Augustine as a friend very dear to him.

His house in Hippo became a school for bishops. He lived with his clergy under one roof with nothing owned by anyone. The Catholic Encyclopedia says that men from that house founded monasteries across Africa and became bishops of nearby cities. Saint Possidius counts ten of Saint Augustine’s friends and disciples who became bishops. Saint Alypius had stood beside him in the garden in Milan, and in 394 he became bishop of Thagaste. Saint Evodius had sung the psalm at Saint Monica’s deathbed, and he became bishop of Uzalis. Saint Possidius himself became bishop of Calama. Pius XI wrote that Saint Possidius tells of many religious men sent out from Hippo to found new monasteries, “as one fire kindles another.”

His opponents felt the change too. In 404 a Manichee named Felix debated him in public. Felix declared himself beaten, embraced the faith, and signed the record of the debate with Saint Augustine. A merchant named Firmus left the same sect after one of his sermons. The Catholic Encyclopedia says that Donatism died out little by little after the conference of 411 and disappeared with the coming of the Vandals.

The Donatist quarrel changed his own mind about one thing. At first he sought conferences and friendly debate. According to the Catholic Encyclopedia, the violence of the Circumcellions led him to approve strict laws against the Donatists. He gave this view in his Letter 93, from the year 408. It adds that he never wanted heresy punished by death, and in another letter he wrote: we ask you not to kill. It calls this a change of opinion.

In 426 he was seventy-one. He wanted to spare Hippo the turmoil of an election after his death. So he had the people and clergy accept the deacon Heraclius as his helper and successor. The emperor Theodosius II later ordered that Saint Augustine be summoned to the Council of Ephesus in 431, with every mark of respect. The summons came to a dead man.

How Saint Augustine’s Influence Grew After Death

His memory faced its first test within a year. In Gaul some churchmen had begun to attack his teaching on grace, and his memory with it. Pope Saint Celestine I wrote to the bishops of Gaul in 431.

“We have always held Augustine, a man of holy memory, in our communion for his life and his merits. Not even a rumor of evil suspicion ever touched him. We remember that his learning in his time was so great that even my predecessors always ranked him among the best teachers.”

— Pope Saint Celestine I, letter to the bishops of Gaul, 431 (our translation)

The popes kept to that line for a century. Pope Saint Gelasius I called Saint Jerome and Saint Augustine lights among the Church’s teachers. A bishop named Possessor asked what Rome held about free will and grace. In 520 Pope Saint Hormisdas answered that this could be learned from the many books of Saint Augustine, most of all those he wrote to Hilary and Saint Prosper. He added that the formal chapters were kept in the Church’s records. In 534 Pope John II wrote to certain senators and appealed to the works of Saint Augustine against heretics. He said that the Roman Church follows and keeps his teaching.

Then a council took up the same teaching. In 529 Saint Caesarius of Arles presided over a council in the town of Orange in southern Gaul. He had asked Pope Saint Felix IV for support against those who still held that the first step toward faith is our own. The pope sent him a set of chapters drawn almost entirely from the works of Saint Augustine and from the Sentences of Saint Prosper of Aquitaine. These became the basis of the council’s twenty-five canons. A canon is a short ruling of a council. Pius XI later said that the council used his very words.

The council’s conclusion sums up the teaching. The sin of the first man weakened free will. Since then no one can love God as they ought, or believe in him, or do good for God’s sake, unless the grace of God’s mercy has come first. With horror the same council rejected the idea that God predestines anyone to evil. On 25 January 531 Pope Boniface II confirmed the council. He wrote that many Fathers had treated the question fully, and he named above all “Bishop Augustine of blessed memory.” So no one should doubt any longer that faith itself comes to us from grace. The Catholic Encyclopedia says that a century of fierce contests, from 430 to 529, ended in “the triumph of moderate Augustinism.”

Pope Saint Gregory the Great also wrote to a Roman official named Innocentius, the prefect of Africa.

“If you want to be fed with rich food, read the little works of blessed Augustine, your countryman. Next to his fine wheat, do not look for our bran.”

— Pope Saint Gregory the Great, Register X.37, to Innocentius, prefect of Africa (our translation)

Saint Augustine’s books were known at the court of Charlemagne too. Einhard wrote a life of the king, and he knew the court. He wrote that at meals the king listened to reading or music. The readings were stories and deeds of olden time. “He was fond, too, of St. Augustine’s books, and especially of the one entitled ‘The City of God.’”

In 1298 Pope Boniface VIII ordered that the feasts of four great teachers be kept with special honor through the whole Church. They were Saint Ambrose, Saint Jerome, Saint Gregory the Great, and Saint Augustine. A Doctor of the Church is a saint whose teaching the Church holds up for everyone. The Catholic Encyclopedia calls these four the Western Church’s four Doctors. By then his name was also on a way of life. In Hippo he had lived with his clergy with everything in common, and in Letter 211 he had written to the nuns of Hippo about how to live together. The Church calls his instructions the Rule of Saint Augustine. Collections of monastic rules from the eighth and ninth centuries already used that name for them.

In 1059 a synod in Rome asked priests to share their income. In the twelfth century many groups of clergy took up what became known as the Rule of Saint Augustine. They were called canons regular. Two other orders, the Premonstratensians and the English Gilbertines, adopted it with changes of their own. In 1256 Pope Alexander IV gathered several groups of hermits into one order under it, the Hermits of Saint Augustine. Their first general meeting took place on 1 March of that year, and the papal bull of 4 May 1256 is the order’s founding charter. Pius XI wrote in 1930 that a good number of religious communities of men and women follow the rule that Saint Augustine gave to the women of Hippo. Some are called Augustinians. Others have founders who added their own constitutions to it.

The quarrels about grace never fully ended, and the way the Church handled them shows how it reads Saint Augustine. The Catechism draws one line from Orange in 529 to the Council of Trent in 1546 on original sin. Pius XI said that Trent and Orange both used his very words. By then the Reformation had divided the Western Church. Martin Luther had entered the Augustinian monastery at Erfurt on 17 July 1505. So he belonged to the order that bore Saint Augustine’s name.

A century later the quarrel took a new form. Cornelius Jansenius was the bishop of Ypres. He said that he had read all of Saint Augustine ten times and his writings against the Pelagians thirty times. His book Augustinus appeared in 1640 and claimed to set out Saint Augustine’s teaching. The Holy Office condemned it on 1 August 1641, and in 1653 Pope Innocent X condemned five propositions drawn from it. In 1690 Pope Alexander VIII went further. He condemned the claim that one may hold a teaching simply because it is “clearly based on Augustine,” with no regard to any papal ruling. In each case the Church kept Saint Augustine and read him through its own judgment. In 1748 Pope Benedict XIV wrote that the Apostolic See “favours the liberty of the schools.” The three schools of theology could all teach their own account of how grace and freedom fit together.

In the Greek East the story is slower. He wrote in Latin. Metropolitan Hierotheos of Nafpaktos writes that his works were largely unknown to the Orthodox Fathers until the thirteenth century. Maximos Planoudes translated On the Trinity before 1281. The Confessions and the City of God were first put into Greek in the twentieth century. Saint Nikodemos the Hagiorite wrote a Synaxarion for his feast. A Synaxarion is a short life read in church. He called Saint Augustine a great teacher and theologian of the Church of Christ. He also lamented that the books were only in Latin and that the Greeks were deprived of such wealth.

The East also kept its own view of some of his teaching. Gennadius Scholarius became the first Patriarch of Constantinople after 1453. He criticized Saint Augustine’s teaching that the Spirit proceeds from the Son. Father John Romanides reports this in an essay. Saint Nikodemos writes that people once prayed: “Deliver us O God from the dialectics of Augustine.” He names Gennadius as his witness. Saint Nektarios of Aegina admired Saint Augustine’s love for God. He loved a Greek book of prayers called the Kekragarion, which went under Saint Augustine’s name, and he put it into verse in 1910.

The Orthodox theologian Father John Romanides wrote an essay titled “Is Augustine of Hippo a Father of the Church?” He judged that Saint Augustine’s teaching was the source of errors that Orthodox councils condemned. That is one theologian’s opinion. The Orthodox Church keeps Saint Augustine’s feast on 15 June.

Saint Augustine in the Church Today

The Catholic Church calls him the Doctor of Grace. Pius XI used the title in 1930 in the encyclical Ad salutem humani, a letter to the whole Church for the fifteen hundredth anniversary of Saint Augustine’s death. A footnote in the Catechism says that the Roman Missal’s first Preface of the Saints cites him as “the Doctor of grace.” The same pope put readings from Saint Augustine into the liturgy of the feast of Christ the King. He did so because Saint Augustine had more than once brought out the kingship of Christ. The Ten Commandments carry his mark too. The Catechism says it follows the division of the commandments that Saint Augustine established. It is also the division of the Lutheran confessions. The Greek Fathers divided them a little differently, and the Orthodox Churches and the Reformed keep that division.

Today’s Catechism quotes him again and again. It quotes him on faith and understanding and on the Eucharist and on the Our Father and on the prayer of Christ in us. The Second Vatican Council quoted his “with you I am a Christian” in 1964 when it wrote about the people of God. Benedict XVI gave a series of public talks on him in early 2008 and called him the greatest Father of the Latin Church. On 18 May 2025 Leo XIV opened his homily with the restless heart. Later in the same homily he quoted a sermon of Saint Augustine’s on what the Church is. It is all those who live in harmony with their brothers and sisters and love their neighbor.

His feast in the Catholic Church is 28 August, the day of his death. The Orthodox Church keeps him on 15 June, and the calendar of the Orthodox Church in America lists him as “Blessed Augustine, Bishop of Hippo” with hymns for the day. The Orthodox view of his teaching has more than one voice, as shown above. To Saint Nikodemos he was a great teacher, and to Father John Romanides a source of error.

And the Rule still has people living under it. The Order of Saint Augustine has thousands of members around the world, and one of them became pope in 2025. Canons, Premonstratensians, and many congregations of men and women follow the Rule with their own constitutions added. Sixteen centuries ago a new priest in Hippo began to live with other servants of God who owned nothing of their own. That way of life is still kept.

A Note on the Sources

Saint Augustine’s own words here come from the Latin of the Confessions and from the old public-domain translations of his letters, sermons, and treatises, put into plain English. We give the letters of Pope Saint Celestine I, Pope Saint Hormisdas, Pope John II, and Pope Saint Gregory the Great from the Latin. Pius XI quoted them in 1930, and we did not read the original letters. Much of the story after the year 600 rests on the Catholic Encyclopedia of 1907, whose author Portalié also supplies the judgments we credit to him by name. The Council of Orange and Boniface II are read in modern translations. They are paraphrased, with one short quotation from Boniface II.

Scholars have raised several questions, and we record them here with respect for the tradition. The canons of Orange and the decree of Trent do not name Saint Augustine. Pius XI and the Catholic Encyclopedia say that those councils used his words. Scholars also differ on how new his part was in the teachings on grace and original sin. They debate who gave the Rule its final text, and we say only that it goes back to his house and his letters. Some works that circulate under his name come from later hands: a set of Meditations, a second set of Soliloquies, and the Athanasian Creed. The Kekragarion that Saint Nektarios loved is the Meditations. Writers still differ on what Luther took from Saint Augustine’s books, and on how to weigh his support of strict laws against the Donatists. The counts of his letters and sermons differ because the sources count different things. Where an Orthodox writer’s view differs from the Western record, we name the writer and give the view as his.
