On 15 August 1222, almost the whole city of Bologna crowded into the square in front of the town hall to hear a man preach. A student named Thomas stood in the crowd. He remembered the preacher without flattery. The man’s robe was dirty. He looked like someone to despise, and his face was plain. He began with three words: “angels, men, demons.” Then he talked less like a preacher than like a man at a town meeting, and every word he said aimed at one thing. He wanted the people of Bologna to end their old hatreds and make peace.1

In Bologna, noble clans had carried on bloody feuds for years. That day God gave the plain man’s words such power that many of those clans were brought to make peace. When he finished, men and women rushed at him in crowds. They pushed to touch the hem of his robe or to tear off a scrap of it and carry it home.2

A scrap of cloth was nearly all anyone could take from him. The preacher was Saint Francis of Assisi, a rich cloth merchant’s son who now owned nothing but his robe. He had founded the brothers called the Friars Minor, the Lesser Brothers.3

Not twenty years before Bologna, he had wanted something else entirely. He had wanted to be a knight and a great prince, and he had wanted the whole world to honor him. Then a love so great took hold of him that he could not keep quiet about it. It would ask him for everything he had, and he would give it gladly. This is the story of what that love cost him and what it gave him.4

A Rich Young Man in Prison

Saint Francis was born in Assisi in 1181 or 1182. His father was Pietro di Bernardone, a rich cloth merchant. His mother had him baptized John, and the name the world remembers came later. He grew up quick at trade and quicker at spending, and he was “by nature cheerful and merry.” The young men of the town made him king of their feasts because they knew he would pay.5

In 1202 Assisi went to war with the city of Perugia. Saint Francis was taken prisoner with many men of his town and held in Perugia for a year. Because he was “a noble in his manners,” he was put with the captured knights.6

The prisoners were downcast, but Saint Francis was not. He was cheerful, and he even seemed to be joking. One of the others rebuked him: only a madman would be glad in prison. Saint Francis answered in a loud voice. “What do you think of me? I will be worshipped by the whole world.” The others thought he had lost his mind, and he laughed at his chains.7

Among the prisoners was a knight whom all the rest shunned because he had injured one of them. He was “very proud and unbearable.” Saint Francis alone would not turn his back on him. He kept the man company and urged the others to do the same, and in the end he brought them to make peace with him. It was a small peace, made among prisoners. Twenty years later a whole city would watch him make a larger one.8

After a year the two cities came to terms, and the prisoners went home. Then Saint Francis fell ill, and the illness was long. When at last he could walk outdoors with a stick, he looked out over the fields and vineyards around Assisi. They gave him no delight. Even so, he was soon getting ready to ride to war again.9

Saint Francis Turns Back From War

This time he meant to ride south to Apulia with a nobleman of Assisi and win a knighthood there. He told his friends he was going to be “a great prince.” One night he dreamed of a house full of weapons. Before he set out, he gave his new clothes to a poor knight.10

He got as far as the town of Spoleto. There the Lord spoke to him in the night, in a vision. He asked Saint Francis, “Which can do more for you, the lord or the servant?” “The lord,” Saint Francis answered. Then the Lord asked, “Why, then, are you seeking the servant instead of the lord?” Saint Francis said, “Lord, what do you want me to do?” The Lord told him to go back to his own country, and he went.11

So the young man who had ridden out to become a knight came home without a battle. In Assisi his friends made him king of their feast once more. When they set out through the town afterward, Saint Francis walked behind them in silence with a staff in his hand. He fell farther and farther behind. A sweetness came over him so strong that he could neither speak nor move from the spot.12

He no longer talked of Apulia. He said he would do noble and great things in his own town, and people thought he meant to marry. His friends asked him whether he was thinking of taking a wife. “I will marry a nobler and more beautiful bride than you have ever seen,” he answered. “She will surpass all others in beauty and in wisdom.” He did not say who she was.13

Saint Francis Kisses a Leper

He began to slip away to pray. Outside the town there was a cave, and he went into it alone while a friend waited nearby. In prayer God answered him. He must now despise everything he had loved in a worldly way, and the things he had loathed would become great sweetness to him.14

Lepers filled him with disgust. People sick with leprosy lived apart in houses of their own. When Saint Francis saw one of those houses even two miles away, he would turn his face aside and hold his nose.15

One day he was riding near Assisi when a leper met him on the road and held out his hand for alms. Saint Francis had to force himself. He got down from his horse and put money into the man’s hand. Then he kissed the hand, and the leper gave him the kiss of peace. Saint Francis mounted again and rode on. A moment later he looked back across the open country, and the man was nowhere to be seen. He was filled with wonder and joy.16

A few days later he took a large sum of money to the lepers’ hospital. He gave something to each leper there, and he kissed each one’s hand and mouth. What had been bitter to him was now sweet. To Saint Francis, doing penance meant turning from sin to God. Near the end of his life he began his Testament with this moment:17

“The Lord gave me, Brother Francis, to begin to do penance in this way. When I was in sins, it seemed too bitter to me to see lepers. The Lord himself led me among them, and I showed them mercy. And when I left them, what had seemed bitter to me turned into sweetness of soul and body. And afterward I stayed a little while and left the world.”

Saint Francis of Assisi, Testament, 122618

In his own telling there is no horse and no road. There is only the Lord who led him, and the bitter that turned to sweet.19

Not long afterward he was walking past San Damiano, a little church outside Assisi. It was so old that it seemed about to fall. He felt led to go in and pray. He knelt before a painted image of Christ on the cross, and Christ spoke to him from it. The lips moved, and the voice called him by name. “Francis, do you not see that my house is being destroyed? Go, then, and repair it for me.” Saint Francis was trembling and astonished. “I will do it gladly, Lord,” he answered.20

He took the words at their plainest: the house, he thought, was the old church around him with its crumbling walls. He gave the priest money to keep a lamp burning before the cross. From then on he could hardly stop weeping. He told one close friend what had happened, and the friend wept with him.21

Then Saint Francis took scarlet cloth from his father’s house and rode to the town of Foligno. There he sold the cloth and his horse too. He walked back to San Damiano and kissed the hands of the poor priest. Then he offered him the money for the church. The priest was afraid of Saint Francis’s parents and thought the offer might be a trick, so he would not keep it. Saint Francis threw the money onto a windowsill, “caring as little for it as for dust.” His father was about to learn where his cloth had gone.22

Saint Francis Gives Everything Back to His Father

Saint Francis hid from his father in a cave for a month. When at last he came up into the town, people shouted that he was mad and threw mud and stones at him. His father dragged him home and chained him in a dark place for days. Then Pietro went away for a time. His wife was alone in the house, and she set her son free.23

Pietro came back and complained to the consuls who governed the town. They answered that his son was no longer their business: “From the time he entered the service of God, he has been outside our authority.” So Pietro took his complaint to the bishop of Assisi, and the bishop summoned his son. “I will come to the lord bishop,” Saint Francis said, “for he is the father and lord of souls.” The bishop received him with joy. He told him to give his father’s money back and to trust in the Lord.24

They met in the bishop’s house, and many people had come to watch. Saint Francis went into the bishop’s room and took off every piece of clothing he had. He came out and laid the clothes with the money before the bishop. Then he stood naked in front of them all, and he spoke:25

“From now on I can freely say ‘Our Father who art in heaven,’ and no longer ‘father Pietro di Bernardone.’ See, I not only give him back the money, but I give up all my clothes as well. So I will go naked to the Lord.”

Saint Francis, in the bishop’s house at Assisi26

Then the people saw that he had been wearing a hair shirt next to his skin, under the bright clothes of a merchant’s son. His father was full of grief and anger. He gathered up the money and the clothes and carried them home. The crowd turned against the father, and then what they had seen moved them to tears. Then the bishop, who would be his helper from that day on, put his arms around the naked young man and covered him with his own cloak.27

The break with his family stayed painful. Early one winter morning he rose to pray in poor, worn clothes. His own brother passed by and said mockingly to a neighbor, “Tell Francis to sell you at least a penny’s worth of his sweat.” Saint Francis heard it and was filled with joy. He answered in French: “I will sell that sweat to my Lord at a very high price.” His father cursed him whenever they met. So Saint Francis took a poor beggar with him, and each time his father cursed him he asked the beggar to bless him instead.28

Soon after, he was walking through a wood and singing God’s praises in French when robbers stopped him. They asked who he was. “I am a herald of the great King,” he said. “What is that to you?” They beat him and threw him into a pit full of snow. “Lie there,” they said, “you peasant herald of God!” Saint Francis shook off the snow and climbed out. He went on his way praising the Creator aloud.29

Then he went back to San Damiano to keep his promise. He set to work rebuilding the church, gathering stones and carrying them on his own shoulders. When he went begging for oil for the lamps, he came to a house where men were gambling. He was so ashamed that he turned away. Then he went back to the house, told the men aloud of his shame, and begged in French. In French too he told the people around him that women given to God would one day live at San Damiano.30

The priest of San Damiano fed him on syrups and sweets. Saint Francis argued with himself about it. “You will not find this priest everywhere,” he said. So he took a bowl and went from door to door, begging scraps. When he looked at the mixed leftovers in the bowl, he was filled with disgust. Then he ate them. No rich syrup had ever tasted so good.31

The First Brothers Join Saint Francis

Next he dressed as a hermit and repaired another abandoned church. It was a little chapel of the Virgin Mary called the Portiuncula, the Little Portion. One day at Mass he heard the Gospel in which Christ sends his apostles out to preach. It was about the year 1209. Afterward he asked the priest to explain it. Then he cried out, “This is what I want, this is what I am seeking, this is what I long to do from the very bottom of my heart.”32

He took off his shoes and put down his staff. He traded his leather belt for a cord, and he made himself a rough tunic in the shape of a cross. From then on he greeted everyone with words he said the Lord had shown him: “The Lord give you peace.”33

He was not alone for long. A rich man of Assisi named Bernard asked him to spend a night at his house. In the night Bernard asked what a man ought to do with goods his lord had given him. Saint Francis said he should give them back. Then Bernard said he would give away everything he had in whatever way Saint Francis thought best.34

At dawn the two of them went to the church of Saint Nicholas near the marketplace. A man named Peter who also wanted to join went with them. They prayed. None of them was learned, and they did not know where to find the right words in the Gospel book. So Saint Francis took the closed book and bowed before the altar. Then he opened the book three times in honor of the Trinity, the three Persons of the one God.35

The first time, the book opened at a saying of the Lord. “If you want to be perfect, go, sell all that you have, and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven.” Saint Francis rejoiced and gave thanks to God. The second time it opened at the words “Take nothing with you on your journey.” The third time it opened at “Whoever wants to come after me must deny himself.” Saint Francis turned to the other two. “My brothers, this is our life and Rule, and that of all who will want to join our company. Go, then, and do what you have heard.”36

Bernard sold everything he owned and gave the money to the poor of the city. Peter did the same as far as he could. Both put on the same rough robe as Saint Francis.37

Others came, until there were eight. On the roads people laughed at them and pulled at their hoods from behind. Saint Francis told the little band that many would follow them: “Frenchmen are coming, Spaniards hurrying, Germans and English running.” When there were twelve, he had their way of life written down “in a few words and simply,” as he said later. Then the twelve set out for Rome to ask the Pope to approve it.38

Pope Innocent III blessed them and sent them out to preach repentance. Yet he thought their life “too hard and rough” for those who would come after them. He told Saint Francis to pray and learn what God wanted. Saint Francis prayed, and the answer came to him as a story. A poor but very beautiful woman lived in a wilderness. A king loved her and she bore him sons, and in time the sons were sent to their father’s court. Saint Francis told the story to the Pope. “I, my lord, am that poor woman, whom God loved and in his mercy has adorned in this way.”39

Innocent was amazed, for he had seen a vision of his own. The great church of Saint John Lateran was about to fall, and a small and lowly man of religion was holding it up with his own back. “Surely this is the man,” the Pope said, “who will hold up the Church of Christ by what he does and teaches.” He embraced Saint Francis and approved the Rule by word of mouth.40

Back in Assisi, a young noblewoman wanted so much to hear him that she began slipping out of her father’s house to meet him in secret.41

Saint Clare Leaves Home to Follow Saint Francis

Saint Francis had heard of her too, and he was no less eager to see her and speak with her. She was Saint Clare, from a noble house of Assisi. She came to him with one trusted companion so that no one would know. “His words seemed to her like fire, and his deeds seemed more than human.” He urged her to give herself to Christ alone. She put herself wholly in his hands and took him as her guide, second only to God.42

When Palm Sunday drew near, she asked him what she should do. He told her to go to the blessing of the palms in her finest clothes and to leave the world on the night after the feast. That night the brothers kept watch at the Portiuncula with lighted torches. Saint Clare broke out of her father’s house by a door that had been blocked with beams and stones, and she came to them through the dark. There she laid aside her ornaments, and Saint Francis cut off her hair before the altar. The year was 1211 or 1212.43

Her family came after her with force and with promises. Saint Francis had taken her to a house of Benedictine nuns, and there she held fast to the altar cloths and showed her family her shorn head. Later she went to San Damiano on his advice. It was the church he had worked so hard to repair. Women given to God came to live there with her, as he had once said in French they would. Saint Clare stayed in that house for forty-two years. When she fasted too hard, Saint Francis and the bishop of Assisi ordered her to eat at least half a roll of bread a day.44

Saint Francis was once journeying through the valley of Spoleto when he came upon a great gathering of birds near the town of Bevagna. He preached to them too, and he told them to praise their Creator, who had given them feathers for clothing and wings for flight.45

In 1217 many brothers were sent to lands beyond the mountains and the sea, and Saint Francis set out for France himself. At Florence Cardinal Hugolino, the bishop of Ostia, forbade him to go on. The cardinal said there were churchmen at the Pope’s court who would gladly hinder the new Order. Saint Francis protested that it would shame him to stay behind while the brothers he had sent to far countries suffered for the Lord’s sake. “God has chosen and sent the brothers for the good and the salvation of the souls of all the people of this world.” Hugolino still said no, and Saint Francis sent another brother in his place. In time the cardinal would become his protector and his friend.46

Two years later Saint Francis did go abroad. He left two vicars to govern the brothers in his place and took ship for the East with Peter Cathanii, a learned man of noble birth. A crusading army lay before the city of Damietta in Egypt.47

Saint Francis Goes to the Sultan

The Christian armies of the Fifth Crusade were besieging Damietta. Before one battle in August 1219, Saint Francis told his companion that the Lord had shown him the Christians would not prosper if they fought. “If I tell them this, I will be thought a fool,” he said, “and if I keep silent, I will not escape my conscience.” His companion answered, “Take no notice of how men judge you, for this is not the first time you have been thought a fool.”48

Saint Francis gave his warning, and the soldiers paid no attention. They attacked, and they fled. Six thousand men were killed or taken prisoner. Saint Francis mourned for them all, and most of all for the Spaniards.49

A bishop named Jacques de Vitry was with the army. He knew the brothers already, and he thought them humbler than any other religious men of his time. They had no monasteries and no churches. They had no fields or vineyards or animals, no houses and no possessions, and nowhere “to lay their heads.”50

Then Saint Francis went without fear to the camp of the Sultan, the Muslim ruler of Egypt, “armed with the shield of faith.” Saracens, as the Christians called the Muslims, seized him on the road. Jacques de Vitry wrote that he told them, “I am a Christian. Take me to your lord.” Jordan of Giano wrote that he was beaten and insulted on the way. He did not know their language, so as the blows fell he kept crying out one word: “Soldan! Soldan!”51

At last they dragged him before the Sultan, and when the Sultan saw him, his harshness turned to gentleness. He received Saint Francis with honor and offered him gifts, and Saint Francis would not take them. For some days the Sultan listened closely while Saint Francis preached Christ to him and to his men, and he “looked on him as a man unlike all other men.” In the end the Sultan grew afraid that some of his soldiers would be won over to the Lord by Saint Francis’s words and go across to the Christian army. So he had him led back to the crusaders’ camp with every respect and in safety. Before he let him go, he said to him, “Pray for me, that God may reveal to me the law and faith that pleases him more.”52

Saint Francis had preached among the Saracens for many days and won little. He had hoped to die for the faith, but God did not grant it. He came back alive, carrying the Sultan’s request for his prayers.53

Back in Italy, things were going badly. In the East a woman with the gift of prophecy cried out to the brothers: “Go back, go back! Because Brother Francis is away, the Order is troubled, split and scattered.” And it was true.54

Saint Francis Gives Up Leading His Order

From the time he came home from beyond the sea, his eyes gave him pain, and the pain stayed with him to the end. At home he found the trouble the prophetess had spoken of. The two vicars had added new fasts to the brothers’ life. One brother had gone to the Pope’s court against Saint Francis’s wishes. He had come back with letters for the Poor Ladies, the order of sisters that had begun with Saint Clare. Another was gathering lepers to begin a new order of his own.55

He had often brooded over the dangers to his little flock: enemies outside it, and brothers inside it who might one day rebel. One night he had a dream. He saw a little black hen, like a tame dove, with feathers down her legs and over her feet. She had more chicks than anyone could count. They pressed eagerly around her, but she could not gather them all under her wings. When Saint Francis woke, he understood the dream himself. “I am this hen,” he said, “small in size and black by nature.” The chicks were his brothers. There were so many of them now that his strength was not enough to shield them from the men who troubled them and the tongues that spoke against them. He would give them into the care of the Church of Rome, their mother, and she would be zealous for the glory of their poverty.56

So he did not go to the troublemakers. He went to Pope Honorius III. He lay down at the Pope’s door to wait, for he did not dare to knock loudly at the room of so great a prince. When the Pope came out, Saint Francis bowed to him and said, “Father Pope, may God give you peace.” Then he asked for a helper. “You have given me many popes. Give me one to whom I can speak when I need to, who will hear and settle my cases and my Order’s in your place.” He asked for the Lord of Ostia, Cardinal Hugolino, and the Pope granted it. As the Order’s protector, Hugolino cancelled the letters, and the brother who was gathering lepers was driven from the Pope’s court in disgrace.57

At the end of September 1220 the brothers met in chapter, the gathering of the whole Order. In front of them all, Saint Francis gave up the leadership of the family he had founded. “From now on I am dead to you,” he said. “But here is Brother Peter Cathanii. Let us all, you and I, obey him.” He bowed low before Peter and promised him obedience, and the brothers wept.58

Then Saint Francis raised his clasped hands and prayed. “Lord, I entrust to you the family that you have entrusted to me until now.” From then on he asked to have a guardian of his own, a brother whom he must obey. He would obey even “a novice of one hour,” he said, if that novice were made his guardian.59

That winter a simple brother named James brought a leper covered with sores out of the hospital to the church at the Portiuncula. Saint Francis, who called lepers his “brother Christians,” scolded James. “You should not lead the Christians around, because it is not proper for you or for them.” At once he was sorry, for he feared he had shamed the sick man. He confessed his fault to Peter Cathanii and named his own penance. “Let this be my penance: that I eat from one dish together with my brother Christian.” “Brother, do as you please,” said Peter.60

At the table one dish stood between Saint Francis and the leper. The man’s fingers were bent and bleeding. When he dipped them into the dish, blood and pus ran down into it. The brothers grieved to see it, but they did not dare say a word. Saint Francis ate with him.61

In March 1221 Peter Cathanii died. The man Saint Francis had chosen to obey was gone, and a brother named Elias took his place.62

At Pentecost that year about three thousand brothers came to the Portiuncula. They lived and slept under shelters in a wide fenced field, and the people of the countryside brought bread and wine in plenty. Saint Francis was too weak by then to speak to so many. He sat at Elias’s feet, and when he had something to say he tugged at Elias’s tunic. Elias bent down to listen. Then he straightened up and announced, “Brothers, this is what the Brother says.”63

Saint Francis Grieves for His Order

Saint Francis had put away every kind of envy but one. Whenever he saw someone poorer than himself, he envied him at once and was afraid of losing the contest. One day while out preaching he met a very poor man. “This man’s need puts us to great shame,” Saint Francis said, “and sharply rebukes our poverty. … I chose Poverty for my riches and for my Lady. And see how she shines more brightly in that man.”64

At the Portiuncula the brothers had only one small hut, thatched with straw, with walls of woven branches and mud. Every year they all gathered there for their chapter, and every year there were more of them. Before one chapter the people of Assisi built a great house of stone and mortar there while Saint Francis was away. They built it in a few days, with great devotion and without his knowledge.65

When Saint Francis came back and saw the house, he grieved, and not quietly. He said that whatever pride was seen in that place would soon spread through the whole Order, and everyone would take it as an example. He was the first to climb onto the roof. With his own strong hand he began tearing off the tiles, and he called the brothers up to help him pull down “that monster set against poverty.” They were throwing the tiles to the ground when knights of Assisi came to him. “Brother, this house belongs to the town of Assisi,” they said, “and we are here for the town. Do not destroy what is ours.” “If it is yours,” Saint Francis answered, “I will not touch it.” And he and the brothers came down from the roof.66

The pull came from inside the Order too. One of the ministers, the brothers set over the others, asked leave to keep his valuable books. “I am not going to lose the book of the Gospel, which I have promised to obey, for the sake of your books,” Saint Francis told him. Then the ministers had the Gospel words “Take nothing with you on your journey” taken out of the Rule. They were the very words the book had opened at for Saint Francis and his first brothers in the church of Saint Nicholas. When the Holy Spirit made it known to him, he spoke in front of some of the brothers. “The ministers think they can deceive the Lord and me,” he said. He wanted the Rule to say at its beginning and at its end that the brothers were bound to keep the Holy Gospel of our Lord Jesus Christ.67

On 29 November 1223 Pope Honorius III confirmed the final Rule of the Friars Minor in a bull, a sealed letter that is still kept in Assisi. Its sixth chapter told the brothers to make nothing their own: “neither house, nor place, nor anything.” It also gave the reason Saint Francis had lived by since he gave his clothes back to his father: “the Lord made himself poor for us in this world.”68

Sometimes the pull came quietly, in the shape of a single book. A novice could read the psalms, though not very well. He was a new brother, still learning the life. The Minister General, the brother who now led the Order, had given him leave to own a psalter, a book of the psalms. But he wanted Saint Francis’s leave as well. “Father, it would be a great comfort to me to have a psalter,” he said. Saint Francis answered him with a story of knights. He told how Charlemagne, Roland and Oliver had fought the pagans to the death and had died in battle as martyrs for the faith of Christ. “But now there are many who want honor and the praise of men just for telling the story of what they did.”69

Some days later, as Saint Francis sat by the fire, the novice spoke to him about the psalter again. “Once you have a psalter,” Saint Francis said, “you will want a breviary too.” A breviary is the book of the daily prayers. “And once you have a breviary, you will sit in a chair like a great lord of the Church and say to your brother, ‘Bring me my breviary!’” As he spoke, he took up some of the ashes and put them on his head. He rubbed his hand round and round over his head, the way a man washes his hair, and he said again and again, “I, a breviary! I, a breviary!” The novice was amazed and ashamed. Then Saint Francis told him, “Brother, I too have been tempted to have books.”70

Many months later, at the Portiuncula, the novice spoke to him about the psalter once more. This time Saint Francis said, “Go and do as your minister tells you.” The brother turned and started back the way he had come. Saint Francis stayed where he was on the road and began to think about what he had just said. At once he called after him, “Wait for me, brother, wait!” He caught up with him. “Come back with me, brother, and show me the place where I told you to do as your minister says about the psalter.” When they came to the place, Saint Francis knelt before the brother. “My fault, brother, my fault! Whoever wants to be a Lesser Brother should have nothing but his tunic, as the Rule allows him, and a cord, and drawers, and shoes for those who plainly need them.”71

A brother once asked him why he had handed all the brothers over to others, as if they no longer belonged to him. “Son, I love the brothers as much as I can,” Saint Francis answered. “But if they followed in my footsteps, I would love them more, and I would not make myself a stranger to them. Some of those set over them are drawing them toward other things. They hold up the examples of the men of old and think little of my warnings. But the end will show what they are doing.”72

A little later his illness pressed very hard on him. He raised himself up in his bed with great force of spirit. “Who are they who have snatched my Order, mine and the brothers’, out of my hands? If I get to the general chapter, I will show them what my will is!” The brother asked whether he would not at least change the ministers who had abused their freedom for so long. Saint Francis groaned. “Let them live as they like,” he said. “The ruin of a few is less harm than the ruin of many.”73

Once he was troubled by the bad example some brothers gave, and he took his trouble to prayer. The Lord rebuked him. “Why are you troubled, little man? Have I set you as shepherd over my Order in such a way that you do not know I am its chief protector? … I called the brothers, and I will keep them and feed them. … So do not be troubled, but work out your own salvation. Even if the Order comes down to three brothers, it will stand firm forever through my gift.” From then on Saint Francis would say that one good man can overcome a great crowd of imperfect brothers. A single ray of light, he said, drives away countless shadows.74

The First Christmas Manger at Greccio

Christmas mattered to him more than any other day. He called it “the feast of feasts,” the day God became “a tiny infant, hanging at a human breast.” One year Christmas fell on a Friday, a day without meat, and a brother spoke of fasting. “I want even the walls to eat meat on such a day,” Saint Francis told him. “If they cannot, they should at least be greased on the outside!” About fifteen days before Christmas in 1223, he sent for John of Greccio, a noble of that town whom he loved. He told John what he wanted:75

“I want to call to mind that Child who was born in Bethlehem. I want in some way to see with my bodily eyes his hardships as a baby: how he lay in a manger on the hay, with the ox and the donkey standing by.”

Saint Francis to John of Greccio76

John made everything ready. Brothers came from many places, and the men and women of the town came carrying candles and torches. “The night was lit up like the day.” The woods rang with their voices, and the rocks gave back the sound. Saint Francis came and saw the manger and the hay, and the ox and the donkey standing by. He was full of sighs, overcome with tenderness, and filled with joy.77

A priest said Mass over the manger. Saint Francis was a deacon, one rank below a priest. He sang the Gospel in a strong and clear voice, and then he preached about “the birth of the poor King and the little town of Bethlehem.” Often when he said “Bethlehem,” it came out like the bleating of a sheep. When he spoke the name of Jesus he licked his lips, as if to taste it.78

A man there saw a vision. A little child lay lifeless in the manger, and Saint Francis seemed to come near and wake him from a deep sleep. The people kept the hay, and afterward it healed sick animals and helped women in hard labor. In time a church was built over the place of the manger, the manger that the Church now calls the first Nativity scene.79

Two things filled his memory. One was the humility of God made man, and the other was the love of Christ’s suffering. At Greccio he had shown the first to everyone. The second was waiting for him on a mountain.80

Saint Francis Receives the Wounds of Christ

In the summer of 1224 Saint Francis climbed a mountain called La Verna. He meant to keep a Lent there, a long season of prayer and fasting that would end on the feast of Saint Michael in September. He prayed, and then he took the Gospel book from the altar and had his companion open it three times in the name of the Holy Trinity. Years before, the book had shown him how to live. Now it opened at the Passion of Christ all three times.81

One morning in mid-September he was praying on the mountainside. It was about the feast of the Holy Cross. He saw a seraph coming down toward him. The seraph was an angel with six wings, and between the wings was the figure of a man nailed to a cross. Saint Francis was filled with joy and with grief at the same time. He could not understand what it meant.82

Then marks like the wounds of nails began to appear in his hands and his feet. The Church calls them the stigmata. In his palms the nails seemed to have round black heads. Their points were bent back as if they were made of his own flesh. His right side showed a wound that often bled, and his tunic and drawers were often spotted with blood. The vision taught him that he was to be made like Christ crucified, “not by martyrdom of the body, but by a fire in the soul.”83

He did not want anyone to know. A brother persuaded him that such gifts are given for the sake of others too, and so he told the brothers about the vision. But he never told the words the seraph had spoken to him. “My secret is mine,” he used to say.84

One of his companions on the mountain was Brother Leo. Leo was struggling with a temptation of the spirit, and he longed to have something written in Saint Francis’s own hand. He was afraid to ask. One day Saint Francis called him without being asked. “Bring me paper and ink,” he said, “for I want to write down the words of God and his praises, which I have meditated on in my heart.” Then he wrote:85

“You alone are the holy Lord God, who work wonders. … You are humility. You are patience. You are security. You are rest. You are gladness and joy. … You are our hope. You are our faith. You are our great sweetness. You are our eternal life, great and wonderful Lord, almighty God, merciful Savior.”

Saint Francis, Praises of God, written for Brother Leo on La Verna, 122486

On the other side of the sheet he wrote the old blessing from the Book of Numbers. He ended it with the words “The Lord bless you, Brother Leo.” Across Leo’s name he drew a Tau, a cross shaped like the letter T. “Take this paper,” he told him, “and keep it carefully until the day of your death.” The temptation left Leo at once. He kept the sheet, and on it he wrote that Saint Francis made it after the vision of the seraph, when “the hand of the Lord was upon him.” The sheet is still kept in Assisi.87

Saint Francis cared for Leo like a son. He wrote him a short letter in his own hand. It is kept today in the cathedral of Spoleto. “I speak to you, my son, as a mother does,” he wrote. Then he added: “And if you need it for your soul, or for some other comfort of yours, and you want, Leo, to come to me, come.”88

For the two years he had left, he hid the wounds as well as he could. He wore woolen socks with a piece of hide laid over the wounds of his feet. When people asked to kiss his hand, he held out only his fingers, or sometimes his sleeve. A brother who saw his feet asked, “What is this, good brother?” “Mind your own business,” said Saint Francis. Another found blood on his tunic. Saint Francis touched a finger to his own eye and said, “Ask what that is, if you don’t know that it is an eye.”89

When prayer came over him in front of others, he would make a little cell of his cloak or hide his face in his sleeve. He seemed less a man praying than a man who had become a prayer.90

Saint Francis Writes the Canticle of the Sun

His eyes were failing now. Elias persuaded him at last to let the doctors try to save them, and Cardinal Hugolino joined in. “You ought not to be cruel to yourself,” the cardinal told him. For years Saint Francis had been hard on his own body, which he called “brother body.” In this one thing his deeds did not match his teaching.91

One winter night at the hermitage of Sartiano, a temptation of the flesh came over him while he prayed. He took off his robe and beat himself with his cord. “There, brother donkey,” he said. “This is how you take the whip.” The temptation did not leave. So he went out into the garden and threw himself naked into the deep snow. Then he made seven balls of snow and set them in front of him, and he spoke to his body.92

“Look, this big one is your wife. Those four are your two sons and two daughters. The other two are the servant and the maid. Hurry and clothe them all, because they are dying of cold. But if caring for so many is too much for you, serve God alone.” The temptation left him, and he went back to his cell praising God. A brother at prayer had seen it all by bright moonlight. When Saint Francis found out, he was distressed. He asked the brother to tell no one while he himself was alive.93

Even now he did not think he had done enough. “Let us begin, brothers, to serve the Lord God,” he would say, “for until now we have made little or no progress.” He longed to go back to where he had started. He wanted to serve the lepers again and to be looked down on as he once had been.94

The doctors tried fire. A surgeon was to burn his face with a hot iron from the ear to the eyebrow. When the iron was brought, Saint Francis spoke to it. “My brother fire,” he said, “be kind to me at this hour, be courteous, for I have long loved you in the Lord.” The brothers could not bear to watch, and they ran away. When they came back he was smiling. “You of faint heart and poor spirit, why did you run away? I tell you the truth: I felt no heat of fire and no pain in my flesh.” But the burning did not cure him.95

Two years before his death he lay ill at San Damiano, where Saint Clare and her sisters lived. His cell there was a little hut made of woven mats. For sixty days and more his eyes could not bear the light of day or even the light of a fire. Mice swarmed the cell. They ran over him day and night so that he could neither pray nor rest, and when he ate they climbed onto his table. One night he was worn out, and he began to feel sorry for himself. He prayed to Christ for patience, and the Lord answered him:96

“Suppose the whole mass of the earth and the whole frame of the world were gold beyond price. Suppose all your pain were taken away. Suppose you were given a reward in exchange for the heavy sufferings you are enduring: a treasure of glory so great that all that gold would be nothing beside it … Would you not rejoice, and gladly bear for a moment what you are bearing now?” “I would rejoice indeed,” said the saint. “Yes, I would rejoice beyond measure.” “Then be glad,” the Lord said to him, “for your sickness is the pledge of my kingdom.”

The Lord and Saint Francis, as told by Thomas of Celano, Second Life97

In the morning he told his companions a story. If an emperor gave a whole kingdom to one of his servants, would the servant not rejoice? And if he gave him his whole empire, how much more? Then Saint Francis sat down and thought for a while. At last he began, “Most High, almighty, good Lord.” He set the words to music and taught his companions to sing them. The song praised God for the very things whose light he could no longer bear: Brother Sun, Sister Moon and the stars. Then came Brother Wind, Sister Water and Brother Fire, and the earth, our sister and mother. He made it in the Umbrian speech of his own people, not in Latin:98

“Altissimu onnipotente bonsignore, tue so’ le laude la gloria e l’honore et onne benedictione.”

In plain English: “Most High, all-powerful, good Lord, yours are the praise, the glory, the honor and every blessing. … Praised be my Lord for our sister, mother earth, who sustains and governs us and brings forth various fruits with colored flowers and grass.”

Saint Francis, Canticle of the Creatures, c. 122599

He loved to sing. Sometimes he would lay a stick over his left arm and draw a little bow across it, as if he were playing a fiddle. Then he would sing in French about the Lord until he ended in tears. Now he wanted Brother Pacifico to go through the world with other brothers singing his new song. Pacifico had once been crowned King of Verse. After the song they were to ask the people for one gift, the way wandering singers called minstrels did. “We are the minstrels of the Lord, and this is the reward we ask of you: that you be in a state of true repentance.”100

By now he called his pains his sisters. A companion once urged him to have the books of the prophets read to him for comfort. “I have already made so much of the Scriptures my own,” Saint Francis answered, “that I have more than enough to meditate and reflect on. I need no more, my son. I know Christ, the poor man crucified.”101

Saint Francis Makes Peace in Assisi

In the spring of 1226 he was in the city of Siena, where he began to vomit blood. It went on all night, and the brothers gathered around him in grief. “Father, what shall we do without you?” they asked. “You have always been a father and a mother to us.” He called a priest and had him write down his will for the brothers in three parts. They should always love one another. They should always love and keep “our Lady Poverty.” And they should always be faithful to the leaders and clergy of Holy Mother Church.102

From Siena he was moved to Cortona, where his belly and legs swelled. He asked to be taken home to Assisi, and Elias brought him there. He was lodged in the bishop’s palace. Every night the people of Assisi set a watch of their own men around the palace walls. They feared that if he died in the night, the brothers would carry his body away to another city.103

That summer the bishop of Assisi and the podestà, the chief official of the town, were bitter enemies. The bishop had cut the podestà off from the Church. The podestà had forbidden anyone to sell the bishop anything or to deal with him. “It is a great shame for us servants of God,” Saint Francis said, “that the bishop and the podestà hate each other so.”104

So he added a verse to his song. “Praised be my Lord for those who forgive for love of you, and bear sickness and trouble,” it ran. “Blessed are those who bear them in peace, for by you, Most High, they will be crowned.” He sent one brother to ask the podestà to come to the bishop’s palace. He sent two more to sing the song there in the courtyard, before the bishop and the podestà.105

The podestà stood with his hands joined as if he were hearing the Gospel, and he listened with many tears. When the song ended he spoke out before them all. “Even if a man had killed my own brother or my son, I would forgive him!” He threw himself at the bishop’s feet, and the bishop raised him up. “Since I am quick to anger by nature,” the bishop said, “it is right that you should pardon me too.” The two men embraced. In the square at Bologna, Saint Francis had made peace with a sermon. Now he made it from his sickbed with a song.106

Saint Francis asked a brother whether his conscience was right to trouble him about the way he had treated his body. The brother said that his body had obeyed him in everything and had never spared itself. Then Saint Francis spoke to it. “Rejoice, brother body, and forgive me, for see, now I gladly do what you desire.”107

A doctor came from the town of Arezzo. Saint Francis was not afraid of death, and he asked him for the truth. The doctor said that by all the rules of medicine the illness could not be cured. Death would come at the end of September or on the fourth of October. Saint Francis stretched out his hands. “Welcome, my Sister Death!” He called for Brother Leo and Brother Angelo to sing him the song. They sang it grieving and in tears. Before the last verse he added one more: “Praised be my Lord for our sister, bodily death, from whom no living person can escape.”108

In those days Leo thought to himself that Saint Francis, his father in God, was close to death. If only he could have his father’s tunic when his father died, it would be a great comfort to his soul. He said nothing. A little later Saint Francis called him. “I give you this tunic. Take it, so that from now on it is yours. Though I wear it while I live, at my death let it pass to you.”109

Before he left the bishop’s palace, he blessed the brothers. He could no longer see. He crossed his arms so that his right hand came down on the head of the brother at his left. “On whom am I holding my right hand?” he asked. “On Brother Elias,” they told him. “That is what I want,” he said, and he blessed him. Then he spoke to them all. “I am hurrying to the Lord, and I trust that I am going now to my God, whom I have served in my spirit.” He asked them to carry him down to the Portiuncula. He wanted to give his soul back to God in the place where he had first known the way of truth.110

The Death of Saint Francis

In the week that he died, Saint Clare lay ill at San Damiano. She was afraid that she would die before him and never see him again, and she grieved bitterly. Saint Francis loved her as a father. He could not go to her, so he sent her his blessing in a letter. He gave the brother who carried it a message for her: “Go and tell Sister Clare not to grieve or be sad because she cannot see me now. Let her know that she will surely see me before her death, and that she and her sisters will be greatly comforted when they do.”111

Shortly before he died he also wrote a few lines for her sisters. In them he said once more what he had wanted all his life. “I, little Brother Francis, want to follow the life and poverty of our most high Lord Jesus Christ and of his most holy mother, and to hold to it to the end.” He begged them, “my ladies,” never to leave that life and poverty because of anyone’s teaching or advice.112

He wanted to ask Jacoba of Settesoli, a devoted noblewoman in Rome, for a piece of ash-grey cloth and some of her almond sweets. The brothers had written the letter and were looking for a messenger when someone knocked at the door. It was Jacoba herself. She had come from Rome with the cloth and the sweets, and with candles and incense besides. She said she had been told in prayer to hurry if she wanted to find him alive. The rule kept women out of the brothers’ house, but Saint Francis set it aside for her. “This rule is not to be kept for that lady, whom such great faith and devotion have brought here from so far away.”113

He sent for Bernard, his first brother, and Bernard asked for his blessing. Saint Francis was blind by now. He reached out his right hand, and it came down on the head of Brother Giles instead. “This is not the head of my brother Bernard,” he said. Bernard came closer, and Saint Francis laid his hand on him and blessed him. “The first brother that the Lord gave me was Brother Bernard,” he said.114

Then the brothers did as he told them:

“He was worn out by the grave sickness that was the end of all his sufferings, and he had himself laid naked on the bare ground. He did this so that he might wrestle naked with his naked enemy in that last hour, while the enemy could still rage against him. … He was laid on the ground, and his sackcloth garment was taken off. Then he raised his face to heaven, as he used to do. His whole mind was fixed on that [heavenly] glory. With his left hand he covered the wound in his right side, so that it would not be seen. And he said to the brothers, ‘I have done my duty. May Christ teach you yours.’”

Thomas of Celano, Second Life115

The guardian of the house brought him a tunic and a pair of drawers. He brought a sackcloth cap as well, to cover the scars that the doctors’ iron had left on his face. These things were only lent to him, the guardian said. He must accept them under obedience, and he had no right to give them to anyone. Saint Francis rejoiced with a glad heart. Twenty years before, in the bishop’s house, he had said that he would go naked to the Lord. He was keeping his word. Even the clothes he would die in were not his own.116

He blessed all the brothers and laid his hand on each head, beginning with his vicar. Bread was brought, blessed and broken, and he gave each of them a piece in memory of the Last Supper the Lord ate with his disciples. To the doctor he said, “Brother doctor, boldly predict that death is near, for to me it will be the gate of life.” He asked the brothers to lay him naked on the ground again when he was dead. They were to leave him there “for as long as it would take to walk a mile at an easy pace.”117

With what voice he had left, he prayed the psalm that begins, “With my voice I cried to the Lord.” A brother he loved very much spoke for all of them. Their father was leaving his sons, the brother said, and he begged him to forgive them all. “See, my son, God is calling me,” Saint Francis answered. “I forgive all the offences and faults of my brothers.” Then he asked for the Gospel book. He had it read from the place in Saint John that begins, “Six days before the Passover, Jesus, knowing that his hour had come to pass from this world to the Father.” He had himself laid on sackcloth and sprinkled with ashes, for soon he would be earth and ashes himself.118

It was Saturday, 3 October 1226. After evening prayer a great flock of larks came down over the roof of the house where he lay. They flew round and round it in a wide circle and sang, as if they were praising the Lord with him. In the first hour of the night, Saint Francis died.119

Saint Francis Is Declared a Saint

All that night the brothers and the people sang God’s praises around his body. There were so many lights that it seemed like a watch kept by angels. Within days Elias wrote to the whole Order. “Before I begin to speak, I sigh,” his letter began. “We are orphans without a father, robbed of the light of our eyes.” Then he wrote of a wonder. “I announce to you a great joy and a new miracle.” Their father had appeared like one crucified, with five wounds in his body. While he lived there had been no beauty in his face. After death his face shone with a wonderful whiteness, and his stiff limbs became soft like the limbs of a little child.120

In the morning the people and clergy of Assisi came for his body. They carried him toward the city with hymns and trumpets, and everyone held a branch of olive or some other tree. On the way they stopped at San Damiano and set him down in the church. The sisters received Holy Communion through a small window in their wall, and now it was opened. Saint Clare came to it with her daughters, “to see the father who would not speak to them again, nor come back to them.”121

The brothers lifted his body from the bier and held it up in their arms before the window for a long time. The sisters wept and cried out to him in low voices. “Father, father, what shall we do?” Caught between sorrow and joy, they kissed his wounded hands. Then the brothers carried him away, and the window was closed. He had kept his promise. Saint Clare had seen him once more, and she and her sisters had been comforted.122

The people buried him in the church of San Giorgio, where he had first learned his letters and first preached. They chose it because they feared the men of Perugia would carry him off by force. On the very day of the burial, a girl was brought to his coffin. For a year her neck had been bent so that her head lay against her shoulder. She put her head under the coffin, and through the merits of the saint she rose with her neck straight.123

By then his friend Cardinal Hugolino had become Pope Gregory IX. In the summer of 1228 the Pope fell out with the people of Rome and came north. He visited the Poor Ladies and went on to Assisi, where he greeted the tomb with sighs and tears. Miracles worked through the saint were heard, examined and approved. When the cardinals met again in Perugia, they said that they hardly needed miracles at all:124

“There is no need … for miracles to prove the most holy life of this most holy man. We have seen it with our eyes, touched it with our hands, and tested it under the teaching of truth.”

The cardinals at Perugia, 1228, as told by Thomas of Celano, First Life125

On 16 July 1228 the Pope came to Assisi to declare his friend a saint. He preached on a verse from the Bible, “Like the morning star amid the mist,” and as he spoke of Saint Francis he was bathed in tears. The miracles were read aloud. Then the Pope raised his hands. He decreed that Saint Francis “be numbered in the list of the saints, and that his feast be kept on the day of his death.” The Te Deum, the Church’s hymn of thanks, rang out, and the people held up green olive branches. At last the Pope came down and kissed the tomb. In the letter that announced the news, he wrote that the Church now had in heaven “a patron whose friendship We enjoyed on earth.” The Church keeps his feast on 4 October.126

Pope Gregory laid the first stone of a great new church in the saint’s honor, and Elias built it. In May 1230 the body was carried there from San Giorgio in a wooden chest. It lay on a cart drawn by oxen draped in purple, with the city’s armed men all around. At the church the citizens seized the body themselves and kept the friars from honoring it. Pope Gregory wrote in anger that they had dishonored the sacred rite. The body was laid deep beneath the high altar, so well hidden that in time its exact place was forgotten. The man who had asked to die on the bare ground passed out of sight for nearly six hundred years.127

How Saint Francis Changed the Church

His brothers carried his memory far. Around 1230 Jordan of Giano was traveling in Germany with small relics of Saint Francis. At one house the brothers came out to meet him in procession with crosses and incense, palm branches and candles. They were singing, “This is the lover of the brothers.” Jordan remembered what he carried inside his robe. “Rejoice, brothers,” he told them, “for I know that you have praised not me as myself, but in me our Father, blessed Francis.” He took out the relics and laid them on the altar.128

The family Saint Francis left grew into many branches. In time the Friars Minor became three orders: the Friars Minor themselves, the Conventuals and the Capuchins. The Poor Ladies became the Poor Clares, and the Rule of Saint Clare still carries the lines he wrote for her sisters before he died. A Third Order gathered men and women who wanted to live his way in the world.129

His tomb stayed lost for centuries. After several failed searches, Pope Pius VII allowed a new one in 1818. The friars dug for some fifty-two nights through solid rock until they reached a Roman stone coffin inside an iron cage. In it lay his remains with a few coins, some small beads and a ring. A crypt was later built beneath the church so that pilgrims could pray at his tomb. In 1934 the remains of Brother Leo and three other companions were brought into the crypt to lie near him, and so was the casket of Jacoba of Settesoli.130

In 1939 Pope Pius XII named Saint Francis patron of Italy together with Saint Catherine of Siena. Forty years later Pope Saint John Paul II named him patron of those who care for the natural world.131 In 2013 the cardinals met to choose a new pope. When they had chosen him, one cardinal embraced him and said, “Don’t forget the poor!” The new pope thought of Saint Francis of Assisi, “the man of poverty, the man of peace, the man who loves and protects creation,” and he took his name. He named one of his teaching letters to the whole Church Laudato si’, after the refrain of the Canticle, and he signed another at the tomb in Assisi.132

For the eight hundredth anniversary of his death, Pope Leo XIV proclaimed a Year of Saint Francis. On 21 February 2026 the friars carried his remains up from the crypt into the lower church. For the first time anyone could come and see them: the bones of a small man about five feet two inches tall, whose feet were deformed. They lay there for a month, and nearly four hundred thousand people booked a place to see him.133

In Bologna the crowd had pushed forward to tear a scrap from his robe. A scrap of cloth was nearly all he had. Eight hundred years later the pilgrims came to file past him once more. When the diggers reached him in 1818, his head was resting on a rough stone. Long before, a bishop who knew the brothers had written that they had nowhere “to lay their heads.” Saint Francis himself once looked back on those first days and put them into one line:134

“And we wanted to have no more.”135

A Note on the Sources

The earliest Life is Thomas of Celano’s First Life of 1228–1229. His Second Life of 1244–1247 and the Legend of the Three Companions of 1246 add the memories of brothers who lived with Saint Francis. The voice from the San Damiano crucifix is first told in those two books. Many stories of the last years come from the Mirror of Perfection, which gathers the companions’ memories. They include the leper’s dish, the night of the mice, the peace in the bishop’s palace, Jacoba’s visit, Bernard’s blessing, the message to Saint Clare and the larks. So do the novice and his psalter and the words the ministers took out of the Rule. The Mirror’s own closing note gives 1228, but Boehmer and Howell place its present form around 1318. Celano reads the bride of the feast as the religious life that Saint Francis took up. The words about his Lady Poverty come from the Second Life. Celano does not date the snow figures, and the order of the cautery and the night at San Damiano is not certain. Howell’s note names Brother Leo as the brother of the parchment and the tunic. Jacques de Vitry says nothing of a trial by fire before the Sultan or of a secret wish for baptism, so those later stories are not told here. The wolf of Gubbio and “perfect joy” come from the Little Flowers, compiled after 1322. The prayer “Lord, make me an instrument of your peace” was first printed in Paris in 1912 without an author’s name. Saint Francis died at nightfall on 3 October 1226; Elias counted that hour as the start of Sunday, 4 October. Robinson dates Saint Clare’s flight to 1212, while the witnesses at her process point to about 1211. Jordan dates the first missions abroad to 1219, but Howell says 1217, and this life follows Howell. The details of the 1818 search come from secondary accounts.

How This Life Was Researched

This life draws on about thirty sources in five languages: Latin, Italian, French, Spanish and English. Saint Francis’s own writings were read in the 1904 Quaracchi critical edition. Celano’s two Lives were read in Howell’s English of 1908. The passages on the bride, the tunic, the last days and the window at San Damiano were read again in d’Alençon’s Latin edition of 1906. The witnesses include Brother Elias, in the letter he sent to the whole Order within days of the death. Two outsiders also saw Saint Francis: Jacques de Vitry at Damietta in 1219 and Thomas of Spalato at Bologna in 1222. The sworn testimony from Saint Clare’s canonization process in 1253 and papal documents from 1223 to 2026 stand beside the early Lives.
