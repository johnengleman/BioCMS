The Church remembers Saint Francis of Assisi as a man through whom God worked many wonders, in his life and at his tomb. This list gathers those wonders as the oldest witnesses told them: healings, visions, and signs. Each account names who told it and when.

Pope Gregory IX canonized Saint Francis at Assisi on 16 July 1228, less than two years after his death. To canonize is to declare before the whole Church that a person is a saint in heaven. Before the Pope did so, the miracles worked at the tomb were read aloud in his presence. His letter of canonization is called a bull, and it speaks of “many brilliant miracles.”

The friar Thomas of Celano wrote the first Life of Saint Francis in 1228–29. He set down the miracles that were read before the Pope, and that list comes first below.

The other accounts come from the early Lives, the first books about Saint Francis. They are Celano’s two Lives (1228–29 and 1244–47), the Legend of the Three Companions (1246), and Saint Bonaventure’s Life (1260s). Two later books add more. The Mirror of Perfection (about 1318) keeps the memories of Saint Francis’s companions, and the Little Flowers (after 1322) is a later collection of stories about him and his companions.

Miracles read at the canonization, 1228

Thomas of Celano set these down in 1228–29, in sections 127 to 150 of his First Life. He says they are the miracles that were read before Pope Gregory IX and proclaimed to the people. He says he wrote them “to stir up the devotion of the people of this time and to strengthen the faith of those to come.”

A girl’s bent neck is healed, 1226

On the day Saint Francis was buried, a girl was brought to his coffin. For a year her neck had been so bent that her head touched her shoulder, and she could look up only sideways. She put her head under the coffin for some time. Then through the merits of the saint she raised her neck, and her head went back to its place. Celano says she was astonished at the sudden change and ran away weeping. A hollow could be seen in the shoulder where her head had rested so long.

Status: first of the miracles read before Pope Gregory IX at the canonization in 1228. Told by Thomas of Celano in his First Life (1228–29).

A bent-legged boy of Narni is healed

A boy of Narni had one leg bent so far back that he could walk only with two sticks. He was a beggar, and he had been ill so long that he no longer knew his own father and mother. Through the merits of Saint Francis God set him free. He could go wherever he wished without a stick, praising and blessing God and his saint.

Nicholas of Foligno’s drawn-up leg is healed

Nicholas of Foligno had his left leg drawn up, and the pain was so great that his screams kept his neighbors awake at night. He had spent so much on doctors that he was deep in debt, and none of it had helped. At last he made vows to God and to Saint Francis and had himself carried to the tomb. He spent the night there in prayer. Then he stretched out his leg and went home joyfully, without a stick.

A boy with a bent leg is healed

A boy came to the tomb with one leg bent so far that the knee touched his breast and the heel touched his buttocks. His father was wearing sackcloth for him, and his mother was doing penance for him too. Suddenly God healed the boy so completely that he ran about the streets, sound and merry, giving thanks to God and to Saint Francis.

A man of Fano with ulcers is healed

A man of Fano was bent double, and his legs were full of ulcers. The smell was so bad that the hospital refused to take him in or keep him. He begged Saint Francis for his compassion, and before long he rejoiced to be set free through the saint’s merits.

A Gubbio girl’s contracted hands are healed

A little girl of Gubbio had contracted hands, and for a year she had lost the use of all her limbs. Her nurse carried her to the tomb with a waxen image, to ask for her healing. They stayed there eight days. Then one day all her limbs were restored, and she could use them as before.

A crippled boy of Montenero is healed

A crippled boy of Montenero lay for several days before the doors of the church where Saint Francis rests. From the waist down he had no strength at all. One day he was carried in and touched the tomb, and he came out whole.

The boy himself used to tell what he had seen. As he lay before the tomb, a young man in the brothers’ robe stood over it with pears in his hands. He offered the boy a pear and told him to get up. “See, I am bound together and cannot get up at all,” the boy said, and he ate the pear. The young man offered him a second one and again told him to rise. As the boy reached for it, the young man took his hand, led him out, and vanished from his sight. The boy saw that he was healed and cried out, showing everyone what God had done in him.

Two people carried in baskets are healed

A woman of Coccorano had lost the use of every limb except her tongue. She was carried in a basket to the tomb, and after she had stayed a while before it she rose up healed. A citizen of Gubbio brought his son to the tomb in a basket too. The boy was so bent that his withered legs touched his buttocks. His father received him back sound and whole.

A bent beggar of Narni is healed

Bartholomew of Narni was a very poor old man. He had fallen asleep under a walnut tree and woken up so bent that he could not walk. For six years he lived as a beggar. One night Saint Francis appeared to him in a vision and told him to go to a certain bath, where he would be set free. In the morning Bartholomew told the bishop. The bishop made the sign of the cross over him, blessed him, and told him to hurry to the bath. Bartholomew dragged himself along on a stick until he was worn out. Then a voice said to him, “Go in the peace of the Lord, for I am the one you made your vow to.” He lost the road in the dark, and the voice put him right. In the bath he felt a hand on his foot and a hand on his leg, stretching it gently. He jumped out of the bath healed, praising and blessing the almighty Creator and Saint Francis his servant.

Six blind people receive their sight

Celano tells of six blind people who received their sight. A woman named Sibyl had been blind for years. She was brought to the tomb in sorrow and went home rejoicing. A blind man of Spello received his sight before the tomb. A woman of Camerino had lost the sight of her right eye. Her parents made a vow and laid on the eye a cloth that Saint Francis had touched. Then they gave thanks to the Lord God and to Saint Francis for her sight. A woman of Gubbio made a vow and saw again. A citizen of Assisi had been blind for five years. He had been a close friend of Saint Francis, and he always spoke of that friendship when he prayed to him. He touched the tomb and was healed. Albertino of Narni had been blind for about a year, and his eyelids hung down to his cheeks. He made a vow to Saint Francis and received his sight at once. Then he set out to visit the tomb.

Peter of Foligno is freed from demons

Peter of Foligno was on his way to the shrine of Saint Michael at Monte Sant’Angelo, to keep a vow or to do penance for his sins. He drank from a spring on the road, and it seemed to him that he had swallowed devils. For three years they tormented him, and he did things horrible to see. Then he came to the tomb of Saint Francis. The devils were raging and tearing at him as he touched the tomb, and there God set him free. Celano calls it a clear and open miracle.

A Narni woman is freed by a vision

A woman of Narni was out of her mind and did horrible things. Saint Francis appeared to her in a vision and told her to make the sign of the cross. She said she could not, so he made it over her himself. He drove out her madness and her demonic fancies.

Celano adds that many other men and women tormented by devils were set free through the merits of Saint Francis.

A black-skinned baby of Todi is healed

Matthew was an infant of Todi. For eight days he lay as if dead, with his mouth shut and his eyes sightless. The skin of his face, hands, and feet had turned as black as a pot, and foul blood ran from his mouth. Everyone gave up hope. His mother knelt and humbly called on the name of Saint Francis, and she made a vow. As she rose from prayer the boy opened his eyes, saw the light, and began to feed. Soon the black skin fell away and he grew strong. His mother asked him, “Who set you free, my son?” He lisped, “Ciccu, Ciccu.” That was as close as a baby could come to saying Francis.

Four healings, including a boy who fell

A lad fell from a very high place and lost his speech and the use of his limbs. He lay three days without food or drink, and people thought he was dead. His mother sent for no doctors. She begged Saint Francis to heal him and made her vow, and she received him alive and whole. Then she began to praise the almighty power of the Savior.

Celano adds three more. A lad named Mancino was dying, and everyone had given him up. He called on the name of Saint Francis as well as he could, and he recovered at once. A boy of Arezzo named Walter had a fever and two abscesses, and the doctors had given him up. His parents made a vow to Saint Francis, and the boy was healed. Another man near death had a waxen image made as an offering. He was freed from all his suffering before the image was finished.

A withered hand, dropsy, and gout are healed

Celano tells several more healings. A woman had lain in bed for years and could not turn or move. She made a vow to God and to Saint Francis, and she was freed from all her sickness. A woman of Narni had not used her hand for eight years. Saint Francis appeared to her in a vision and stretched it out, and it was as good as the other. A lad of Narni had been swollen with sickness for ten years, and his mother made a vow for him. He was healed. A man of Fano with dropsy was freed from it through Saint Francis. A citizen of Todi had gout so badly that he could neither sit nor rest, and doctors, baths, and medicines had done nothing. One day he made a vow in the presence of a priest and prayed to the saint, and his health came back.

A paralyzed woman and Bontadoso are cured

A woman in Gubbio lay paralyzed. She called on the name of Saint Francis three times, and she was healed.

A man named Bontadoso suffered so much in his hands and feet that he could not move or turn, and he could no longer eat or sleep. A woman came to him and urged him to make a vow to Saint Francis. In his pain he answered, “I don’t believe he is a saint.” The woman kept urging him. At last he made this vow: “I promise myself to Saint Francis. I will believe that he is a saint if he cures me of this illness within three days.” Through the merits of God’s saint he was set free. He walked, ate, and slept, giving glory to almighty God.

A man shot in the eye recovers

A man had been shot in the head with an iron arrow. It had gone in through the eye socket and stuck there, and the doctors could do nothing. With humble devotion he made a vow to Saint Francis, hoping to be saved through his prayers. While he slept, Saint Francis told him in a dream to have the arrow taken out through the back of his head. This was done the next day, and the man recovered without great difficulty.

Imperatore of Spello and a friar are healed

Imperatore of Spello had a rupture for two years, and he had to wear a truss. He went to the doctors, but they asked a price he could not pay. He did not have enough to live on for a single day. So he turned to God for help. He humbly called on the merits of Saint Francis out of doors, at home, and wherever he went. In a short time he was fully cured by God’s grace and the merits of Saint Francis.

A friar in the March of Ancona had a fistula, and the doctors had given him up. He asked his minister for permission to visit the tomb, trusting that the saint’s merits would win him a cure. The minister refused, because of the snow and rain. The friar was still upset at the refusal. One night Saint Francis stood by him. “Son, be anxious no more,” he said. “Take off the fur coat you are wearing, throw away the plaster and the bandage, and keep your Rule, and you will be set free.” In the morning the friar did all of it, and he gave thanks to God for his quick healing.

Two lepers, Atto and Bonuomo, are healed

Atto was a lad of San Severino. He was covered with scabs, and the doctors judged him a leper. His limbs were swollen, he could not walk, and he lay on his sickbed while his parents grieved. At last his father decided to entrust him to Saint Francis. “Will you make a vow to Saint Francis, my son, so that it may please him to free you from this sickness?” “I will, father,” the boy said. His father had paper brought and measured the boy’s height and girth. He told his son to make his vow and to promise a candle of that height every year of his life. Atto raised himself as well as he could, clasped his hands, and humbly begged Saint Francis for his compassion. When he had finished his prayer, he was healed of his leprosy. He rose up giving glory to God and to Saint Francis, and he began to walk with joy.

In Fano a lad named Bonuomo was held by all the doctors to be paralyzed and leprous. His parents devoutly offered him to Saint Francis. He was cleansed of the leprosy, the paralysis left him, and he was fully well.

A deaf-mute boy speaks, Castel della Pieve

A boy of Castel della Pieve was a beggar who had been deaf and mute from birth. His tongue was so short that it seemed to have been cut off. One evening he asked by signs for shelter at the house of a man named Mark, and Mark gladly took him in as a servant. One night the boy was waiting on Mark and his wife at supper. Mark said to his wife, “I would count it the greatest of miracles if Saint Francis gave this boy hearing and speech.” Then he made a vow to the Lord God. If Saint Francis worked this, he would hold the boy dear for his sake and provide for him all his life. As soon as the vow was finished, the boy spoke. “Saint Francis lives,” he said. Then he looked behind him. “I see Saint Francis standing up there, and he is coming to give me speech.” He asked, “What then shall I say to the people?” Mark answered, “You shall praise the Lord and save many people.” Mark rose up in great joy and told everyone what had happened. All who had known the boy ran together, and they gave humble praise to God and to Saint Francis. The boy’s tongue grew, and he spoke properly as if he had always talked.

The last four cures in Celano’s list

Celano ends his list with four cures. A boy named Villa could neither speak nor walk. His mother made a waxen image in faith and carried it with great reverence to the tomb. When she came home, she found her son walking and talking. A man in the diocese of Perugia could not speak, and his swollen throat kept his mouth gaping open. As he was about to climb the steps to the tomb he vomited much blood, and then he began to speak and could open and shut his mouth. A woman’s throat burned so badly that her tongue dried to the roof of her mouth. She could not speak, eat, or drink. In her heart she made a vow to Saint Francis. Suddenly a small round stone came out of her throat, and she showed it to everyone standing by. A boy of Greccio had lost his hearing, memory, and speech. His parents had great trust in Saint Francis, and they made a vow with humble devotion. Through the favor of Saint Francis, God gave the boy every sense he had lacked.

Saint Francis foretells a convent at San Damiano

Saint Francis was repairing the church of San Damiano. Celano says he begged for oil there in French. Whenever the Holy Spirit filled him with fire, he spoke burning words in French. Innocent III’s vision of the Lateran, about 1209–10

Pope Innocent III told Saint Francis about a vision he had had before Saint Francis came to him. In the vision the Lateran church was about to fall. A small, humble religious man held it up with his back. The Legend of the Three Companions tells this in its account of Saint Francis’s visit to Rome.

Saint Francis preaches to the birds near Bevagna

Near Bevagna Saint Francis came upon a gathering of birds, including doves and rooks. He went among them and touched them with his tunic. He blessed them and made the sign of the cross over them, and he gave them permission to fly away. Then he went on his way with his companions, giving thanks to God.

Saint Francis quiets the swallows of Alviano

At Alviano Saint Francis spoke to the swallows. He said, “My sisters, the swallows, it is time for me to speak too, because you have been saying enough all this time. Listen to the word of God. Be silent and quiet until the sermon is finished.” The swallows fell silent at once and did not move until the sermon ended. The people who saw it said, “Truly this man is a saint and a friend of the Most High.” With great devotion they hurried to touch at least his clothes, praising and blessing God.

A thirsty man finds water in a rock

Saint Francis was very weak, and he wanted to go to a hermitage where he could give himself more freely to prayer. He borrowed a poor man’s donkey to ride. Saint Francis feels no pain from the hot iron

Greccio’s wolves and hail stop after a promise

Wolves were killing animals and people at Greccio, and hail ruined the crops every year. One day Saint Francis was preaching to the people. “To the honor and praise of almighty God, listen to the truth I announce to you,” he said. He pledged his word that the plague would leave them if each of them confessed his sins and did worthy penance. Then the Lord would look on them and multiply them. But if they were ungrateful and went back to their old ways, the plague would return and be worse. Through the saint’s merits and prayers the destruction stopped from that hour. Wolves and hail did no more harm, and when hail came toward their borders it stopped or passed elsewhere. Later the people grew rich and forgot God who had saved them. Celano says the evils returned, with pestilence and the sword, and at last fire destroyed their fortress.

Private Visions, Voices, and Struggles

These are things that passed between God and Saint Francis in secret, or that one companion alone saw: a vision, a voice, or a struggle with temptation. The early writers tell them as Saint Francis or the companion told them. Each account says who told it, as far as the book says.

Saint Francis prays for a returning brother at Siena

Celano tells of a well-known brother who left the Order and wandered alone, and who came back after a storm of temptations. When he reached the brothers’ house at Siena, Saint Francis was there. Saint Francis saw him, hurried to a cell, and shut himself in. He told the brothers that he had fled to prayer to free the one who had erred. “I saw in my son something that gave me good reason to be displeased. But by the grace of my Christ, all that led him astray is now gone.” The brother then fell on his knees and confessed his fault.

Status: a private experience Saint Francis told his brothers. Told by Thomas of Celano in his Second Life (§32–33).

Saint Francis sees a devil on a brother’s back

Celano tells of a brother who would not obey Saint Francis’s vicar and followed another brother as his superior. Saint Francis was there, and he sent a messenger to warn him. The brother then threw himself at the vicar’s feet and obeyed. Saint Francis sighed and said to his companion, the messenger: “Brother, I saw a devil on that disobedient brother’s back, gripping him by the neck. Celano writes that for more than two years a very heavy temptation of the spirit weighed on Saint Francis. He afflicted his body, prayed earnestly, and wept many tears. Saint Francis meets a temptation with seven snowballs

Devils attack Saint Francis; a brother sees a throne

Celano says Saint Francis once came with a companion to a church far from any home, sent him to the hospital for the night, and stayed alone to pray. A vision of crumbs stands for the Rule

Celano says that Saint Francis was very anxious while the brothers discussed the confirmation of the Rule, their written way of life. He saw a vision in the night. A voice from above told him, “Francis, make one host out of all the crumbs, and give it to all who want to eat.” Those who despised it or did not take it with devotion were marked with leprosy. Pope Nicholas V sees Saint Francis upright, 1449

The Franciscan history Annales Minorum prints a letter that tells a story from 1449. In the letter a bishop named Jacopo says that Cardinal Astorgio told him what he saw at the tomb with Pope Nicholas V. They went down at night and found Saint Francis’s body standing upright. It faced west, with its eyes raised to heaven and its hands hidden in the robe. The wounds showed fresh blood. The letter says the Pope promised to keep the sight secret.

Status: told in a letter of 1449 by Bishop Jacopo of Lacedonia, who heard it from Cardinal Astorgio. Printed in Wadding’s Annales Minorum, 1732.
