On 15 August 1222, nearly the whole city of Bologna crowded into the square in front of the town hall to hear a man preach. A student named Thomas, from the city of Spalato, stood in the crowd. Years later, in a history of his home city’s bishops, he described the preacher without flattery. His habit, the robe of a man vowed to God, was dirty, Thomas wrote. The man himself was easy to look down on, and his face was plain. He began with three words, “angels, men, demons.” After that he did not preach like a preacher. He talked like a man at a public meeting, and everything he said ran toward one end: to put out old hatreds and make peace.

Bologna’s noble clans had old feuds that had raged with much bloodshed. That day, Thomas says, God gave the plain man’s words such power that those clans were brought to make peace. Many learned men who heard him wondered that a man with no learning could speak like that. Afterward, men and women rushed at him in crowds, trying to touch the hem of his habit or tear off a scrap to carry away.

A scrap of cloth was nearly all anyone could take from him. The man in the dirty habit was Francis of Assisi, and he owned nothing else. Less than twenty years earlier he had been the great spender of Assisi, the cloth merchant’s son whom the young men made king of their feasts. Then he had handed his clothes back to his father in front of a bishop. Later, in the Rule, the written way of life for his brothers, he told them to make nothing their own, “not a house, not a place, not anything.”

Out of that refusal came a family that still lives in the Catholic Church. Francis founded the Friars Minor, the Lesser Brothers, and by 1221 some three thousand of them met around him in a single field near Assisi. The Pope confirmed their Rule in 1223. He set a young noblewoman named Clare on the way that became the Poor Clares. At the town of Greccio he made the Christmas manger, a feeding trough with hay, that a Vatican decree of 2026 calls the first Nativity scene. In Umbrian, the everyday speech of his own town, he wrote a song of praise to God for Brother Sun and Sister Death. Within days of his death, his vicar, the brother who governed the Order in his place, told the whole Order that their father’s body bore the wounds of Christ.

What he wanted fits in a sentence. He wanted to live by the Gospel and to own nothing, because, as he wrote in his Rule, “the Lord made himself poor for us in this world.” Keeping to that sentence took the rest of his life. First his own pride stood in the way, and a disgust so strong that he held his nose at the sight of a lepers’ house two miles off. Then success stood in the way, when the brothers became thousands and some leaders wanted houses and books. At the last his own body stood in the way, half blind, burned by the doctors, and worn out. How he kept the one thing through all three is the story of his life. It begins in a prison, with a young man who laughed at his chains and said the whole world would one day honor him.

The King of the Feasts in Chains

He was born in Assisi in 1181 or 1182, the son of Pietro di Bernardone, a rich cloth merchant. His mother named him John at his baptism; the name the world remembers came later. Thomas of Celano, one of his friars, wrote Francis’s first Life, or biography, within three years of his death. He calls the young man “a shrewd trader, but a very showy spender.” Another Life, the Legend of the Three Companions, was written down in 1246 from the memories of men who had lived with him. It agrees that he was “cheerful and merry by nature.” He loved the French language and sang in it. The young men of Assisi made him leader of their feasts, Celano adds, because they knew he would pay.

In 1202 Assisi was at war with the nearby city of Perugia, and Francis was taken prisoner with many men of his town. Because he was “a noble in his manners,” the Three Companions say, he was held with the captured knights. One day the other prisoners were gloomy, but Francis seemed, if anything, to be joking. A companion told him that only a madman would be cheerful in prison. Francis answered him in a loud voice: “What do you think of me? I will be honored by the whole world.” In Celano’s second Life the others thought him “mad and out of his mind.” He laughed at his chains and said, “One day I will be honored as a saint all over the world.”

Among the prisoners was one knight whom all the rest avoided. He had done some harm to another prisoner, and Celano calls him “very proud and unbearable.” Francis alone refused to shun him. He kept company with the man and urged the others to do the same, until, Celano says, they made peace with him. The young man who dreamed of being honored was already making peace.

After a year the two cities came to terms, and the prisoners went home. Francis fell into a long illness. At last he could walk outside again, leaning on a stick. The fields and vineyards around Assisi, Celano says, no longer gave him any delight. Soon he was planning to ride to war again.

The Bitter Thing Turns Sweet

The plan was to ride to the region of Apulia with a nobleman of Assisi and win a knighthood there. Francis told his friends he was going to be “a great prince,” and one night he dreamed of a house full of weapons. First he gave his new clothes to a poor knight. He got as far as the town of Spoleto. There, in the night, a voice asked him “who could do more for him, the servant or the lord?” “The lord,” Francis said. “Then why are you looking for the servant instead of the lord?” The voice told him to go back to his own country, and he went.

At home the old life seemed to take him back. His friends made him king of their feast once more. When they set out afterward, Francis walked behind them with a staff, silent, falling farther and farther behind. As he told it later, a sweetness came over him so strong that he could neither speak nor move. The others noticed and asked whether he was thinking of taking a wife. “I will marry a bride more noble and more beautiful than any you have ever seen,” he answered. “She will be more beautiful than all the others, and wiser than them all.”

On a pilgrimage to Rome he traded clothes with a beggar and stood among the poor at the door of St Peter’s church, begging in French. He was still ashamed, Celano says, to beg before people who knew him. Back in Assisi he took to praying in a cave outside the town while a friend waited nearby. In prayer an answer came. Everything he had loved with a worldly love, the voice told him, he must now despise. The things he had hated would become great sweetness to him.

Lepers had always filled him with disgust. When he saw their houses even two miles away, Celano says, he would turn his face aside and hold his nose. One day, riding near Assisi, he met a leper, and the Three Companions say that he “forced himself.” He got down from his horse, gave the man money, and kissed his hand, and the leper gave him the kiss of peace. Then Francis rode on. In Celano’s later version, he looked back across the open country a moment afterward and could not see the man anywhere. He was filled with wonder and joy.

A few days later he took a large sum to the lepers’ hospital, gave each of them money, and kissed each one’s hand, and, Celano adds, their mouths. What had been bitter to him, the Three Companions say, was now changed into sweetness. Some twenty years later, near death, Francis began his Testament, his last words to his brothers, with this moment:

“The Lord gave me, Brother Francis, to begin to do penance in this way. When I was in my sins, it seemed too bitter to me to see lepers. The Lord himself led me among them, and I showed them mercy. And when I left them, what had seemed bitter to me turned into sweetness of soul and body. And afterward I stayed a little while and left the world.”

— Francis of Assisi, Testament, 1226

In his telling there is no horse, no road, and no kiss. There is the Lord, who led him, and a bitterness that turned to sweetness in body and soul. In his own memory, everything else followed from that.

Not long after, he passed San Damiano, a little church outside Assisi so old it seemed ready to fall, and he felt led to go in and pray. The early Lives tell what happened next. As he prayed before a painted image of Christ on the cross, the image spoke to him. Celano says its lips moved and it called him by name. The Three Companions give the words: “Francis, do you not see that my house is being destroyed? So go and repair it for me.” Trembling and amazed, Francis answered, “Lord, I will gladly do it.”

He took the words at their plainest: the house was the crumbling church in front of him. He began at once with what he had, giving the priest money to keep a lamp burning before the crucifix. From then on, Celano says, he could not stop weeping. He told one close friend what had happened, and the friend wept with him.

Then Francis did something that brought his old life down on his head. He took scarlet cloth from his father’s stock, rode to the town of Foligno, and sold both the cloth and the horse. He walked back with the money, kissed the poor priest’s hands, and offered it for the church. The priest thought it was a trick and, afraid of Francis’s parents, would not keep it. Francis threw the money onto a windowsill, Celano says, “caring as little for it as for dust.” Pietro di Bernardone was about to learn where his cloth had gone.

Naked in the Bishop’s House

Francis hid. For a month he stayed in a cave. When he finally came into the town, people called him mad and threw mud and stones at him. His father dragged him home and chained him in a dark place for days. His mother, alone at home while her husband was away, set him free. When Pietro came back, he went to complain to the consuls, the men who governed the town. They answered that his son was no longer their business: “Since he entered the service of God, he is no longer under our authority.” So Pietro took his complaint to the bishop.

Francis answered the summons. “I will come to the lord bishop,” he said, “because he is the father and lord of souls.” The bishop received him with joy and told him to give the money back and trust in the Lord. What happened next took place in the bishop’s house, before a crowd that had gathered there. Francis went into the bishop’s room and took off every piece of clothing. He came out naked and laid the clothes and the money before the bishop. Under the bright clothes of the merchant’s son, the people saw, he had been wearing a hair shirt, a rough shirt worn to punish the body. Then he spoke:

“From now on I can freely say ‘Our Father in heaven,’ and no longer ‘father Pietro di Bernardone.’ Look, I give him back not only the money, but all my clothes as well. So I will go to the Lord naked.”

— Francis, in Thomas of Celano, Second Life, 1244–1247

Pietro, hot with grief and anger, carried the money and the clothes home. The people watching turned against him. Then, moved by what they had seen, they wept. The bishop put his arms around the naked young man and covered him with his own cloak. From that day the bishop of Assisi was Francis’s helper.

The break with his family was complete, and it stayed painful. One cold day his own brother passed him and said mockingly, “Ask Francis to sell you a penny’s worth of sweat now.” Francis smiled. “Indeed, I will sell my sweat to my Lord, at a very high price.” Whenever his father cursed him, Francis would ask a beggar to bless him instead.

Soon after, he went off through the woods singing God’s praises in French. Robbers stopped him and asked who he was. “I am a herald of the great King,” he said. “What is that to you?” They beat him and threw him into a pit full of snow. “Lie there,” they told him, “you peasant herald of God!” They meant it as an insult. Francis shook off the snow, climbed out of the pit, and went on through the woods praising the Creator aloud. After that he worked for some days as a kitchen helper in a monastery, and a friend in the town of Gubbio gave him a tunic. Then he went back to San Damiano to keep his promise to the crucifix.

One Book Opened Three Times

He repaired the church alone, gathering and carrying the stones himself. Once he went to beg oil for the lamp and came to a house where men were gambling. He was so ashamed to ask in front of them that he turned away. Then he went in after all, told them aloud that he had been ashamed, and begged in French. Whenever the fire of the Spirit came over him, Celano says, he broke into French. That day, still in French, he told the people around him that women given to God would one day live in a monastery there.

The priest of San Damiano fed him on syrups and sweets. Francis argued with himself about it. “You will not find this priest everywhere,” he said, and he took a bowl from door to door and begged for scraps. Looking down at the bowl of mixed leftovers, he was filled with disgust. Then he ate, and the Three Companions say that no rich syrup had ever tasted so good to him.

He repaired another abandoned church, a little chapel of the Virgin Mary called the Portiuncula, the Little Portion. He dressed as a hermit, a man who lives alone to pray. Then, at Mass one day, probably in 1209, he heard the Gospel in which Christ sends out his twelve apostles to preach. Afterward he asked the priest to explain it. When he understood, he said, “This is what I want, this is what I am looking for, this is what I long to do from the depths of my heart.” He took off his shoes, put down his staff, traded his leather belt for a cord, and made himself a rough tunic in the shape of a cross. From then on he greeted everyone with words he said the Lord had shown him: “May the Lord give you peace.”

He was not alone for long. One of the first to join him was Bernard, a rich man of Assisi. One night Bernard asked him what a man should do with goods his lord had given him. Give them back, Francis said. Then he would give away everything he had, Bernard answered, as Francis thought best. At dawn the two of them, with a man named Peter who also wanted to join, went to pray at the church of St Nicholas by the marketplace. None of them knew where to find the right words in the Gospel book, for they were, the Three Companions say, men without learning. So Francis took the closed book, bowed before the altar, and opened it three times, in honor of the Trinity, the one God who is Father, Son and Holy Spirit.

The first time, the book opened at these words: “If you want to be perfect, go and sell all that you have and give to the poor. And you will have treasure in heaven.” Francis rejoiced and thanked God. At the second opening came, “Take nothing with you on the road.” At the third came, “If anyone wants to come after me, let him deny himself.” Francis turned to the other two. “My brothers, this is our life and Rule, and the life of all who will want to join our company. So go and do what you have heard.”

Bernard sold everything and gave the money to the poor of the city, and Peter did the same as far as he could. Both put on the same rough habit as Francis.

Others came, until there were eight, and Francis sent them out two by two to preach. On the roads people mocked them, pushing dice into their hands and pulling at their hoods from behind. Francis told the little band that many would follow them: “Frenchmen are coming, Spaniards hurrying, Germans and English running.” Near the end of his life, he looked back on those first days in his Testament. “And we wanted to have no more,” he wrote. “And we were uneducated and subject to everyone. And I worked with my hands, and I want to work.”

When the brothers numbered twelve, Francis had their way of life written down “in a few words and simply,” as he put it later. Then the twelve set out for Rome to ask the Pope to approve it.

The Pope Who Dreamed of Him

In Rome they found their own bishop, the bishop of Assisi. At first he feared they meant to leave his diocese, the district in his care. Then a cardinal, one of the Pope’s chief advisers, questioned Francis at length and urged him to become a monk or a hermit instead. Francis refused. In the end the cardinal gave way and took the case to the Pope himself. “I have found a man of most perfect life,” he told Innocent III, a man who wished to live by the Gospel.

The next day Francis stood before the Pope. Innocent blessed the brothers and sent them out: “Go with the Lord, brothers, and preach repentance to all, as the Lord is pleased to inspire you.” Yet he had doubts. A life like theirs seemed to him “too hard and rough” for those who would come after them, and he told Francis to pray and learn God’s will.

Francis prayed, and the answer came to him as a story. In a wilderness there lived a woman, poor but very beautiful. A king loved her, and she bore him sons, and in time the sons were sent to their father’s court. Francis told the story to the Pope and explained it. “My lord, I am that poor woman, whom God loved and in his mercy has made beautiful in this way.” The king was God, and the sons were the brothers.

Innocent was amazed beyond measure, the Three Companions say, and for a reason of his own. Before Francis ever came to Rome, the Pope had seen in a vision the church of St John Lateran, his own cathedral, about to fall. A man of a religious order, small and lowly, was holding it up with his own back. “Surely,” Celano has him say, “this is the man who will hold up the Church of Christ by his deeds and his teaching.” Innocent embraced Francis and approved his rule by word of mouth. He gave the brothers leave to preach, and the twelve received the tonsure, the shaved crown that marked a man in the service of the Church.

Six years later, in July 1216, Innocent III died at Perugia. Jacques de Vitry, a churchman about to be made a bishop, found him dead but not yet buried. Thieves had stripped the body in the night, and it lay nearly naked in the church. “I saw with my own eyes,” Jacques wrote, “how short and empty is the false glory of this world.” He found one comfort at the Pope’s court: the Friars Minor, men and women who had left everything for Christ. By day they went into the cities to preach. By night they prayed in their hermitages. The women lived by the work of their hands. Their only distress was that people honored them more than they wished.

The first woman to follow Francis was Clare, from a noble house in Assisi. She had wanted so much to hear and see Francis that she began slipping out of her father’s house to meet him in secret.

Clare Leaves by the Walled Door

The Life of Clare, written some forty years later, says Francis was just as eager to meet her. She came to him with a single trusted companion so that no one would know. “To her his words seemed like a flame, and his deeds more than human.” He persuaded her to keep her virginity for Christ. She put herself entirely in his hands, “seeing him, after God, as the guide of her steps.”

As Palm Sunday, the Sunday before Easter, drew near, Clare asked Francis what to do. He told her to go to the blessing of the palms in the cathedral in her fine clothes and ornaments, and to leave the world that night. On Palm Sunday the others pressed forward to receive their branches. Clare stayed in her place out of modesty, and the bishop came down the steps himself and put a palm into her hands. Her Life goes on:

“The next night Clare began to carry out the saint’s command. With some trusted companions she began the escape she had longed for. She did not want to leave by the usual door. So, with a strength that amazed her, she broke open another door, which was walled up with a mass of beams and stones. Leaving behind her home, city and family, Clare hurried to St Mary of the Portiuncula. There the friars, who were keeping watch at the little altar with lighted torches, received the virgin Clare.”

— Life of St Clare, ascribed to Thomas of Celano, c. 1255–1261

There, by the light of those torches, she took off her ornaments, and the friars cut off her hair. It was Palm Sunday of 1212. Francis took her first to a house of Benedictine nuns, an older order, at San Paolo. Her family came after her with force and promises. Clare took hold of the altar cloths and uncovered her head to show her cut hair. After a few days at another convent, she went on Francis’s advice to San Damiano, the church he had rebuilt with his own hands. There, as he had once said in French that they would, women given to God came to live. Clare stayed in that house for forty-two years.

Francis watched over the new community closely. When Clare fasted too hard, her sisters later testified, he and the bishop of Assisi ordered her to eat at least half a roll of bread a day.

Meanwhile the brothers spread, and some of the best-loved stories about Francis come from these years. Near the town of Bevagna he preached to a gathering of doves, rooks and other birds, walking among them and brushing them with his tunic. He told them to praise the Creator who had given them feathers for clothing and wings for flight. In the town of Alviano, when swallows chattered as he tried to preach, he turned to them. “My sisters, the swallows, it is now time for me to speak too, because you have been saying enough all this time.”

In 1217 many brothers were sent to lands beyond the sea, and Francis decided to go too. He chose France, because its people showed a special reverence for the Body of Christ, the bread of the Mass. In the city of Florence, his companions remembered, he met Cardinal Hugolino, the bishop of Ostia. Hugolino, who would become his protector and friend, forbade him to cross the mountains, because some churchmen at the Pope’s court would gladly block his Order. Francis protested that the brothers were not meant for Italy alone. “God has chosen and sent the brothers for the good and the salvation of the souls of all the people of this world,” he said. Hugolino still said no, so Francis sent Brother Pacifico, once crowned King of Verse, to France in his place.

Two years later, at the Portiuncula, Francis sent the brothers out to France, Germany, Hungary and Spain, and this time he went himself. Leaving two vicars in charge at home, he sailed east with Peter Cathanii, a learned man of noble birth. A Christian army of the crusades lay before the city of Damietta in Egypt.

Unarmed into the Sultan’s Camp

The armies of the Fifth Crusade were besieging Damietta. Before a battle, Francis told his companion the Lord had shown him that the Christians would fail if they fought. “If I tell them this, they will take me for a fool,” he said. “But if I keep quiet, I will not escape my conscience.” His companion answered with a brother’s dry humor: “Pay no attention to being judged by men. You are not just now starting to be taken for a fool.” The soldiers ignored his warning. They attacked and fled, and Celano counts six thousand killed or captured. Francis mourned for them all, Celano says, and especially for the Spaniards.

Later he left the Christian lines and went, unarmed, toward the camp of the Sultan of Egypt, the Muslim ruler. Jacques de Vitry, now bishop of Acre, was with the army. He wrote down what he and others saw, calling the Muslims Saracens, as Christians then did:

“We saw the first founder and master of this Order, … a simple and unlettered man, loved by God and men, named Brother Francis. He came to the Christian army before Damietta, in the land of Egypt. He was carried away by such drunkenness and fire of the spirit that he went without fear to the camp of the Sultan of Egypt, armed with the shield of faith. When the Saracens seized him on the road, he said, ‘I am a Christian. Take me to your lord.’ They dragged him before the Sultan. When that cruel beast saw the man of God, he was turned to gentleness. For some days he listened very closely as Francis preached the faith of Christ to him and to his men.”

— Jacques de Vitry, bishop of Acre, Historia orientalis, c. 1220–1225

The road there was not gentle. Jordan of Giano, a brother who later wrote a chronicle of these years, says Francis was beaten and insulted on the way. He did not know the language, so as the blows fell he kept crying for the Sultan: “Soldan, soldan!” Celano says the Sultan received him with honor and offered him gifts. Francis “despised them like dung,” and the Sultan “looked on him as a man unlike almost all other men.” After some days, Jacques writes, the Sultan began to fear that the preacher’s words would win over some of his soldiers. He had Francis taken back to the Christian camp “with all respect and safety.” At the end he said to him, “Pray for me, that God may be pleased to show me the law and faith that please him more.”

In a letter home in the spring of 1220, Jacques was honest about the result. Francis had preached the word of God to the Saracens for many days, he wrote, and had achieved little. Celano adds that God did not grant him the martyrdom, the death for Christ, that he wanted. He had walked into the enemy’s camp armed only with the faith he preached, and he came back alive, carrying a Sultan’s request for his prayers.

Back in Italy, things were going badly. Jordan says a woman with the gift of prophecy cried out to the brothers in the East. “Go back, go back, because with Brother Francis away the Order is troubled, split and scattered.” And it was true, Jordan adds.

“I Am Dead to You”

His companions said later that the eye disease that would torment him to the end began after he came back from the East. On the road home he rode a donkey, and a brother from a noble family of Assisi walked behind him. The brother was thinking that this man’s parents and his own had never played together as equals. Though the brother had said nothing aloud, Francis got down at once. “No, brother, it is not right for me to ride while you walk. In the world you were nobler and more powerful than I.” The brother wept and confessed his thought.

At home Francis learned that the two vicars he had left had added new fasts to the brothers’ life. One brother, who looked after Clare’s sisters, the Poor Ladies, had got letters from the Pope’s court against Francis’s wishes. Another was starting a new order of his own.

Before he went to Rome, Celano says, Francis dreamed of a little black hen, like a tame dove, with feathered legs. She had more chicks than she could cover with her wings. He explained the dream himself. “I am this hen,” he said, “small in size and black by nature.” The chicks were the brothers, too many now for him to shelter, and he must give them into the care of the Roman Church.

So he went, Jordan says, to Pope Honorius III, and not to the troublemakers. He lay outside the Pope’s door, not daring to knock at the room of so great a prince, and waited until the Pope came out. “Father Pope, may God give you peace,” he said. The Pope answered, “May God bless you, son.” A pope, Francis said, was a great man with great affairs, whom poor men could rarely reach. “You have given me many popes; give me one to whom I can speak when I must.” “Whom do you want, son?” the Pope asked. “The Lord of Ostia.” Honorius granted it, and Cardinal Hugolino became the Order’s protector. He cancelled the letters and sent the founder of the new order away in disgrace.

At the end of September 1220, on the feast of St Michael, the brothers met in chapter, the gathering of the Order, and Francis gave up its government.

“From now on I am dead to you. But here is Brother Peter Cathanii. Let all of us, you and I, obey him.”

— Francis, at the chapter of September 1220, in Thomas of Celano, Second Life

He bowed before Peter and promised obedience, and the brothers wept. Then Francis raised his clasped hands. “Lord, I entrust to you the family that until now you have entrusted to me.” From then on he asked to be given a guardian of his own, a brother in charge of him. He said he would obey even a beginner, “a novice of one hour, if he were given to me as my guardian.”

His companions remembered one day from that winter. A simple brother had brought a leper covered with sores out of the hospital to the Portiuncula. Francis called lepers “brother Christians,” and he scolded the brother: “You should not lead the Christians around. It is not proper, for you or for them.” At once he blamed himself, afraid he had shamed the sick man. He confessed his fault to Peter Cathanii and named his own penance: “I will eat from one dish together with my brother Christian.” “Brother, do as you please,” said Peter. At the table one dish stood between Francis and the leper, whose fingers were drawn together and bleeding. When he dipped them, blood and pus ran down into the dish. The brothers grieved, but they dared not say a word. One of them who saw it wrote it down.

Peter Cathanii died on 10 March 1221. Elias, a brother who had come home from the East with Francis, took his place as vicar. At Pentecost that year, the feast fifty days after Easter, the brothers met at the Portiuncula, and Jordan of Giano saw it. About three thousand brothers came. They lived, ate and slept under shelters in a wide, fenced field, with twenty-three tables in rows. Local people brought great loads of bread and wine. After seven days the brothers closed the gate and spent two more days giving away what they had been given. Jordan wrote that he never saw such a chapter again.

Too weak by then to speak to so many, Francis sat at Elias’s feet and tugged at the vicar’s tunic when he had something to say. Elias would bend down, listen, straighten up, and announce, “Brothers, this is what the Brother says.” That was how Francis asked for volunteers to go to Germany, and about ninety brothers stood up, ready to die there. Jordan had hoped to stay out of it. “I came to know you, not to go with you,” he protested. He was sent to Germany anyway.

As the Order grew, so did the pull toward owning things. When the people of Assisi built a stone house for the chapter at the Portiuncula, Francis climbed onto its roof and began tearing off the tiles. He stopped only when knights of the town told him it belonged to the commune, the town government. At Bologna he heard of a “house of the brothers,” and ordered everyone out, even the sick. A minister, one of the brothers in charge of others, asked leave to keep his valuable books. “I am not going to lose the book of the Gospel, which I have promised to obey, for the sake of your books,” Francis told him. Even his tonsure made a point. “Be careful not to give me a large tonsure,” he told the barber. “I want my simple brothers to have a share in my head.”

The struggle wore him down, and sometimes broke out in anger. Once, ill in bed, he raised himself up. “Who are they who have snatched my brothers’ Order and mine out of my hands? If I get to the general chapter, I will show them what my will is!” In prayer, Celano says, God answered him. “Why are you upset, you little man?” Even if his Order were reduced to three brothers, it would stand, “by my gift.”

On 29 November 1223 Pope Honorius III confirmed the final Rule of the Friars Minor in a bull, a formal letter of the Pope, called Solet annuere. Its sixth chapter kept the heart of it all: the brothers were to make nothing their own. The original is still kept in the friars’ house at Assisi, the Sacro Convento. That Christmas Francis was at Greccio.

A Living Child in the Hay

Christmas mattered to him more than any day. Celano says he kept it as “the feast of feasts,” the day God became “a tiny baby, nursing at a human breast.” About fifteen days before Christmas in 1223, the third year before his death, he sent for a man of Greccio named John. John was a noble whom Francis loved, and Francis told him his wish:

“I want to remember the Child who was born in Bethlehem. In some way I want to see with my own eyes the hardships he suffered as a baby: how he lay in a manger on the hay, with the ox and the donkey standing by.”

— Francis to John of Greccio, in Thomas of Celano, First Life, 1228–1229

John prepared everything. Brothers came from many places, and the townspeople came with candles and torches. The night, Celano writes, “was lit up like the day.” The woods rang with their voices, and the rocks gave back the sound. Francis arrived, saw the manger with its hay and the ox and the donkey, and was full of sighs, overcome with tenderness, and filled with joy. A priest said Mass over the manger. Francis was a deacon, a minister who serves the priest at the altar. Dressed as a deacon, he sang the Gospel in a strong, clear voice. Then he preached about “the birth of the poor King and the little town of Bethlehem.” Whenever he said “Bethlehem,” Celano says, it came out like the bleating of a sheep. When he spoke the name of Jesus he licked his lips, as if tasting it.

There, Celano says, a certain man saw a vision. In the manger lay a little child, lifeless, and Francis seemed to come near and wake him as if from a deep sleep. Bonaventure, who wrote a new Life for the Order a generation later, names the man as John of Greccio. He says the child woke as Francis took him in both arms. The people kept the hay, and Celano records that sick animals who ate it were healed, and that women in hard labor gave birth safely. In time an altar and a church were built over the place where the manger stood.

One year Christmas fell on a Friday, a day without meat, and the talk turned to fasting. Francis scolded a brother: “You sin, brother, when you call the day a Child was born to us ‘Friday.’ I wish,” he said, “that even the walls could eat meat on such a day, or, if they cannot, that at least they could be greased on the outside!” He wanted grain scattered on the roads that day for his sisters the larks, and extra hay for the oxen and donkeys. Yet once at dinner a brother spoke of the poverty of the Virgin Mary, and Francis got up from the table. He ate the rest of his bread on the bare ground, sobbing.

Two things, Celano says, filled his memory: the humility of the Incarnation, when God became a man, and the love shown in the Passion, when Christ suffered and died. At Greccio he had made the first visible to everyone. The second was waiting for him on a mountain.

Wounds He Would Not Show

In the summer of 1224, two years before his death, Francis went up Mount La Verna. He meant to keep a Lent there, a season of prayer and fasting. It would run from the feast of Mary’s Assumption into heaven, on 15 August, to the feast of St Michael at the end of September. Bonaventure says he opened the Gospel book three times there, as he once had with Bernard and Peter, and each time it opened at the Passion of Christ.

One morning, near the feast of the Holy Cross in mid-September, Francis saw a seraph, a high angel with six wings, coming down toward him. Between the wings was the figure of a man nailed to a cross. Francis felt joy and grief together and could not understand it. Then marks like nail wounds began to appear in his hands and feet. Bonaventure describes the nails as having round black heads in the palms, with points bent back, as if formed of the flesh itself. His right side showed a wound that often bled. His tunic and underclothes, Celano says, were many times spotted with blood. Bonaventure says the vision taught him that he was to be made like Christ crucified, “not by martyrdom of the body, but by setting the heart on fire.”

He wanted no one to know. One of the brothers persuaded him that such gifts are given for the sake of others too, and Francis told the brothers about the vision. The words the seraph had spoken he never told anyone. “My secret is mine,” he used to say. One of the brothers with him, Leo, was going through a temptation, Celano says, not of the flesh but of the spirit. He longed to have something written in Francis’s own hand. Without being asked, Francis called for paper and ink and wrote out the praises of God:

“You alone are the holy Lord God, who work wonders. … You are humility. You are patience. You are security. You are rest. You are gladness and joy. … You are our hope. You are our faith. You are our great sweetness. You are our eternal life, great and wonderful Lord, almighty God, merciful Savior.”

— Francis, Praises of God, written for Brother Leo on La Verna, 1224

On the other side he wrote an old blessing from the Bible’s Book of Numbers. He ended it with “May the Lord bless you, Brother Leo.” Across Leo’s name he drew the Tau, a cross in the shape of the letter T. “Take this paper,” he told him, “and keep it carefully until the day you die.” The temptation left Leo at once. He kept the parchment and wrote his own note on it. Francis had written it, he said, after the vision of the seraph and the marks of Christ in his body, when “the hand of the Lord was upon him.” The parchment is still kept at the Sacro Convento in Assisi, whose records mention it by 1348.

Francis loved Leo the way a mother loves a child, and he said so. “I speak to you, my son, as a mother does,” he wrote in a short letter in his own hand, which is still kept in Spoleto. Then he added: “And if you need it for your soul, or for some other comfort of yours, and you want, Leo, to come to me, come.”

For the two years he had left, Francis hid the wounds as best he could. He washed only his fingers, wore woolen socks with skin sewn over the wounds of his feet, and gave people only his sleeve to kiss. When one brother saw his feet and asked, “What is this, good brother?” Francis told him, “Mind your own business.” Another brother found blood on his tunic, and Francis put a finger to his eye: “Ask what that is, if you don’t know it to be an eye.” Brother Pacifico, kissing his hands, winked at another brother, and Francis called him back. “May God forgive you, brother, for you sometimes cause me much distress.” Pacifico only asked, “What distress have I caused you, dearest mother?” Francis made no answer.

When prayer came over him in public, Celano says, Francis would make a little room for prayer out of his cloak, or hide his face in his sleeve. He seemed less a man praying than a man who had become a prayer. But the little room was growing dark. His eyes were failing, and Elias, his vicar, at last persuaded him to let the doctors try to save them.

Blind Among the San Damiano Mice

Cardinal Hugolino joined the doctors. “You should not be cruel to yourself,” the cardinal told him, as his companions remembered it. Francis had in fact been cruel to his body for years, and Celano admits it plainly: in the care of “brother body,” Francis’s deeds did not match his words. Near the end Francis said so himself. “Rejoice, brother body, and forgive me, for look, now I will gladly do what you want.”

His companions remembered a time at San Damiano, near Clare and her sisters, when he stayed in a little hut of woven branches. His eyes were so diseased that for sixty days and more he could not bear daylight, or even firelight. Mice swarmed the hut. They ran over him day and night, so he could neither pray nor rest, and climbed onto his table even while he ate. One night, worn out and feeling sorry for himself, he prayed for patience. Celano gives the answer he received:

“Suppose the whole mass of the earth and the whole machine of the world were priceless gold. Suppose that, in exchange for the heavy sufferings you are enduring, all your pain were taken away. And suppose you were given as a reward a treasure of glory, so great that all that gold would be nothing beside it … Would you not rejoice, and gladly bear what you are bearing now?” “I would rejoice indeed,” said the saint. “Yes, I would rejoice beyond measure.” “Then be glad,” the Lord said to him, “for your sickness is the pledge of my kingdom.”

— The Lord and Francis, in Thomas of Celano, Second Life

In the morning he told his companions a parable. If an emperor gave a whole kingdom to one of his servants, would the servant not rejoice? And if he gave him his whole empire, would he not rejoice much more? Then he sat down, thought for a while, and began, “Most high, almighty and most gracious Lord.” He set the words to music and taught his companions to sing them. It was a song of praise for the creatures, some of them lights he could no longer bear. It named Brother Sun, Sister Moon and the stars, Brother Wind, Sister Water, Brother Fire, and the earth itself.

“Altissimu onnipotente bonsignore, tue so’ le laude la gloria e l’honore et onne benedictione.” Most High, all-powerful, good Lord, yours are the praise, the glory, the honor and every blessing.

— Francis, Canticle of the Creatures, first line, c. 1225

He made it in his own Umbrian speech rather than in Latin. “Praised be my Lord,” one verse ran, “for our sister, mother earth, who sustains and governs us and brings forth many fruits with colored flowers and grass.” He wanted Brother Pacifico, the old King of Verse, to go through the world with other brothers singing it. After the song they were to ask the people for one gift. “We are the Lord’s minstrels, and this is the reward we ask of you: that you live in true repentance.” He had always been a singer. Celano had seen him lay a stick over his left arm and draw another across it like a bow on a fiddle. He sang in French about the Lord until he ended in tears.

His pains he no longer called pains, but sisters. A companion once urged him to have the books of the Prophets read to him for comfort. He answered, “I have already learned so much of the Scriptures that I have plenty to pray over and think about. I need no more, my son: I know Christ, poor and crucified.”

The doctors tried fire. The surgeon was to burn his face from ear to eyebrow with a hot iron, and as it was brought, Francis spoke to it. “My brother fire,” he said, “be kind to me in this hour, be courteous, for I have loved you in the Lord for a long time.” The brothers could not bear to watch and fled. When they came back, Francis was smiling. “You faint-hearted, weak-spirited ones, why did you run away? I tell you the truth: I felt no heat from the fire and no pain in my flesh.” If the flesh was not burned enough, he told the doctor, he should burn it again. “I have seen wonderful things today,” the doctor said. It did not cure him.

In the spring of 1226, in the city of Siena, he began vomiting blood. His companions remembered the night the brothers asked what they would do without him. He had a priest write down his will in three parts. They should always love one another and Lady Poverty, and always be faithful to the bishops and clergy of Holy Mother Church. From Siena he was moved to the town of Cortona, where his belly and legs swelled. He asked to be taken home to Assisi, and Elias brought him. Another time, when he lay ill in the town of Nocera, the people of Assisi sent knights to carry him home. They feared that if he died elsewhere, another city would keep his body.

In Assisi he stayed in the bishop’s palace, and the town set a guard around it every night. His companions remembered a bitter quarrel between the bishop and the podestà, the chief magistrate of the town. The bishop had excommunicated the podestà, shutting him out of the Church. The podestà had forbidden anyone to sell the bishop anything or deal with him. “It is a great shame for us, the servants of God,” Francis said, “that the bishop and the podestà hate one another like this.” He added a verse to his song: “Praised be my Lord for those who forgive for love of you, and bear sickness and trouble. Blessed are those who bear them in peace, for by you, Most High, they will be crowned.” Then he sent two brothers to sing it in the palace courtyard before both men.

The podestà stood with his hands joined, his companions said, listening as if to the Gospel, with many tears. When it was done he declared that he forgave the bishop. “Even if it were someone who had killed my own brother or my son, I would forgive him!” He threw himself at the bishop’s feet, and the bishop raised him up. “I am quick to anger by nature,” he said, “so it is right that you should forgive me too.” The two men embraced. The man who had made peace in the square at Bologna made it one last time from his sickbed, with a song.

Elias worried what the townspeople would think of such singing so close to death. “Let me, brother,” Francis answered, “rejoice in the Lord, and in his praises, and in my own sicknesses.” Francis asked a doctor from the town of Arezzo for the truth, because he was not afraid of death. By all the rules of medicine, the doctor said, the illness could not be cured, and Francis would die at the end of September or on the fourth of October. Francis stretched out his hands. “Welcome, my Sister Death!”

Naked on the Portiuncula Floor

He asked Brother Angelo and Brother Leo to sing the song to him. While they sang it, grieving and in tears, he added one more verse before the end:

“Praised be my Lord for our sister, bodily death, from whom no living person can escape.”

Before he left the bishop’s palace he blessed the brothers, crossing his arms so that his right hand rested on the head of Elias. “On whom am I holding my right hand?” he asked. Then he asked to be carried to the Portiuncula, where, Celano says, he had first known the way of truth. Clare was ill at San Damiano and grieved that she could not see him. His companions remembered his message: she should put aside her sorrow, because she and her sisters would see him and be comforted. Shortly before he died he also wrote a few lines to the sisters, which Clare later put in her Rule. He begged them, “my ladies,” never to leave the poverty of Christ for anyone’s teaching or advice.

He had asked for a few things from Rome: a piece of ash-grey cloth from Jacoba of Settesoli, a noblewoman devoted to him, and some of her almond sweets. His companions remembered that while the letter was still being written, she knocked at the door. She had come from Rome with the cloth, the sweets, candles and incense. In prayer, she said, she had been told to hurry if she wanted to find him alive. The rule kept women out of the brothers’ house. Francis waved it aside for her. “This rule does not have to be kept for that lady, whom such great faith and devotion have brought here from so far away.”

Then he asked for something stranger. Celano tells it:

“He was worn out by the illness that ended all his sufferings. He asked to be laid naked on the bare ground. In his last hour the enemy could still attack him, and he wanted to wrestle naked with a naked enemy. … He lay on the ground, with his sackcloth garment taken off, and he raised his face to heaven, as he always did. With his whole mind on that glory of heaven, he covered the wound in his right side with his left hand, so that no one would see it. And he said to the brothers, ‘I have done my duty. May Christ teach you yours.’”

— Thomas of Celano, Second Life

Then the guardian of the house brought him a tunic, a pair of underclothes, and a sackcloth cap to cover the scars the doctors’ fire had left on his face. These things, he told Francis, were only lent to him, by an order he must obey, and he had no right to give them away. Francis rejoiced. To the end he would own nothing, not even the clothes he died in.

He blessed all the brothers, beginning with his vicar, laying his hand on each head. Bread was blessed and broken, and he gave each of them a piece, remembering the Lord’s last supper with his disciples. To the doctor he said, “Brother doctor, tell me boldly that death is near. To me it will be the gate of life.” He asked to be laid naked on the ground again after death, and left there “for as long as it takes to walk a mile at an easy pace.” “See, my son, God is calling me,” he told one of them. “I forgive my brothers all their offenses and faults.” He had a passage of John’s Gospel read aloud, about the Lord’s last Passover. With what voice he had left, he prayed the psalm that begins, “With my voice I cried out to the Lord.”

It was the evening of Saturday, 3 October 1226. After vespers, the evening prayer, his companions remembered, a great flock of larks came down over the roof of the house where he lay. They flew in a wide circle around it, singing, as if praising the Lord with him. At nightfall Francis died, covered in sackcloth and ashes. Within days Elias wrote to the brothers in every province of the Order, and his letter began with a cry. “Before I begin to speak, I sigh,” Elias wrote. “We are orphans without a father, robbed of the light of our eyes.”

His Friend Becomes the Pope

Then Elias came to his great news. “I announce to you a great joy and a new miracle,” he wrote. “Such a sign has not been heard of since the beginning of the world, except in the Son of God, who is Christ the Lord.” Not long before his death, Elias wrote, their father had appeared as one crucified, bearing five wounds in his body. His hands and feet showed the blackness of nails, and his side often bled. In life there had been no beauty in his face, and no limb of his body was free of suffering. After death his face became very beautiful, shining with a wonderful whiteness, and his stiff limbs became soft, like the limbs of a little child.

The next day the people of Assisi carried his body toward the town with hymns and branches of trees, and stopped at San Damiano. The brothers opened the little window through which the sisters received Communion. Clare came with her daughters, Celano says, “to look at the father who did not speak to them.” His companions remembered that the brothers held the body at the window a long time, until Clare and her sisters were comforted, as he had promised. Then the people buried him in the church of San Giorgio, where he had first learned to read and first preached. They chose it, Jordan of Giano says, for fear the people of Perugia would carry him off by force. On the very day of the burial, Celano records, a girl was brought whose head had been bent down onto her shoulder for a year. She put her head under the coffin and rose with her neck straight.

Cardinal Hugolino, who had protected the brothers for years, was now Pope Gregory IX. In the summer of 1228 a quarrel with the Romans drove him from Rome. He came north, visited the Poor Ladies, and at Assisi went to the tomb, Celano says, sighing, weeping, and bowing his head. The miracles reported at the grave were examined. Meeting again at Perugia, the cardinals declared that they hardly needed miracles at all:

“There is no need … for miracles to prove the most holy life of this most holy man. We have seen it with our eyes, touched it with our hands, and tested it by the teaching of truth.”

— The cardinals at Perugia, 1228, in Thomas of Celano, First Life

On 16 July 1228 the Pope came to Assisi to canonize his friend, to declare him a saint. He preached on a verse from Ecclesiasticus, a book of the Old Testament: “Like the morning star in the midst of a cloud.” As he spoke of Francis he was bathed in tears. A subdeacon, a cleric of lower rank, read out the miracles, and a cardinal spoke about them. The people, Celano says, grew tired of waiting. Then the Pope raised his hands and decreed that Francis “be entered in the list of saints, and that his feast be kept on the day of his death.” The Te Deum, the Church’s great hymn of thanks, was sung. The crowd held up green olive branches, and the Pope came down and kissed the tomb. In his bull of canonization he wrote that the Church now had in heaven “a patron whose friendship we enjoyed on earth.” The feast of St Francis is kept on 4 October.

Gregory himself laid the first stone of a great new church for the saint, and asked Thomas of Celano to write the first Life. Elias built the church. In May 1230 the body was carried there from San Giorgio in a wooden chest. It rode on a cart drawn by oxen draped in purple, with the city’s armed men around it. At the church the citizens seized the body themselves and kept the friars from honoring it. Gregory wrote in anger that they had dishonored the sacred rite. The body was laid deep beneath the high altar, so well hidden that in time its exact place was forgotten. Under the new church, the man who had wanted to die on the bare ground passed out of sight for nearly six hundred years.

A Hidden Tomb, a World’s Patron

His brothers carried him everywhere. Around 1230 Jordan of Giano, now in Germany, was carrying relics of Francis, remains of the saint kept with honor, that Thomas of Celano had given him. At one house the brothers came out to meet him in procession, with crosses, censers of incense, palm branches and candles. They were singing “Hic est fratrum amator”: this is the one who loves his brothers. Then he remembered what he was carrying inside his habit. “Rejoice, brothers,” he told them, “for I know that you have praised not me as myself, but in me our father, blessed Francis …” He took out the relics and laid them on the altar.

The family Francis left took many forms. By the early 1900s the Friars Minor had grown into three branches: the Friars Minor themselves, the Conventuals, and the Capuchins. The Poor Ladies of San Damiano became the Poor Clares, nuns who live apart from the world under the Rule of St Clare. A Third Order, begun around 1221, gathered lay people, men and women who stayed in the world but wanted to live his way. Clare’s Rule, approved in 1253, still carries the lines Francis wrote to her sisters before he died. The bull that approved it was found in 1893, wrapped in an old cloak of Clare’s. Pope Honorius III granted the indulgence of the Portiuncula, a pardon of the punishment due for sins, kept on 2 August. The 2026 decree that opened the Year of St Francis names three eight hundredth anniversaries leading up to it: Greccio, the Canticle, and the stigmata, Christ’s wounds in his body.

His memory grew through the Lives. Celano wrote two, and Bonaventure wrote a new Life for the Order, finished in 1263. Three years later a general chapter, a meeting of the whole Order, ordered the older Lives destroyed. In 1939 Pope Pius XII named Francis, together with St Catherine of Siena, patron of Italy.

In 1979 Pope John Paul II named him patron of those who promote ecology. In 2013, during the conclave, the closed meeting where cardinals elect a pope, one cardinal embraced the newly elected pope. “Don’t forget the poor!” he said. The new pope thought of Francis of Assisi, “the man of poverty, the man of peace, the man who loves and protects creation,” and took his name. Two years later Pope Francis wrote an encyclical, a teaching letter to the whole Church, on the care of creation. He named it Laudato si’, “Praised be,” after the refrain of the Canticle. In it he quoted the verse on forgiveness that, his companions said, Francis added to make peace between a bishop and a podestà. On 3 October 2020 he signed another encyclical, Fratelli tutti, at the tomb in Assisi.

The tomb was found again only in 1818, after several failed searches. That year Pope Pius VII allowed a new one, and the friars dug for some fifty-two nights through solid rock. On 12 December 1818 they reached a Roman stone coffin inside an iron cage. In it lay the remains, a few coins, some small beads, and a ring. His head had been resting on a rough stone. The Pope confirmed the relics in 1820, and a crypt, an underground chapel, was built so pilgrims could reach him. In 1934 the remains of four of his companions, Leo and Angelo among them, and the casket of Jacoba of Settesoli were brought into the crypt to lie near him.

In 1978 an examination of the remains showed a small man, about five feet two inches tall, with deformed feet. For the eight hundredth anniversary of his death, Pope Leo XIV proclaimed a Year of St Francis, from 10 January 2026 to 10 January 2027. On 21 February 2026 the friars carried his remains up from the crypt into the lower church. For a month, for the first time, anyone could see them. Nearly four hundred thousand people booked a place.

In Bologna, eight centuries earlier, the crowd had pushed forward to tear a scrap from his habit, because a scrap was all he had. In Assisi they filed past a man who had given back even that. Near the end of his life, remembering the first brothers, he had put the whole of it in one line of his Testament. “And we wanted to have no more.”

A Note on the Sources

The voice from the San Damiano crucifix first appears in the Three Companions and Celano’s second Life. The leper’s dish, the mice, the peace in the bishop’s palace, Jacoba’s visit and the larks come from the Mirror of Perfection, a book of the companions’ stories. Its closing note says 1228, but scholars place its present form around 1318. Jacques de Vitry knows nothing of a trial by fire before the Sultan or of a secret wish for baptism, so those later stories are left out. So are the wolf of Gubbio and “perfect joy,” from the Little Flowers, put together after 1322. The prayer “Lord, make me an instrument of your peace” was first printed, without an author, in Paris in 1912. Francis died at nightfall on 3 October 1226, which Elias counted as the start of Sunday, 4 October. Clare’s translator dates her flight to 1212; the witnesses heard before she was declared a saint point to about 1211. The details of the 1818 search come from later accounts.

How This Life Was Researched

This life draws on about thirty sources in five languages: Latin, Italian (with the Umbrian of the Canticle), French (with one Old French chronicle), Spanish and English. Francis’s own writings were read in the 1904 Quaracchi edition and checked against page scans. The witnesses include Brother Elias’s letter to the Order, written within days of the death. They also include two outsiders who saw Francis: Jacques de Vitry at Damietta in 1219, and Thomas of Spalato at Bologna in 1222. Jordan of Giano’s chronicle gives his own account of the chapter of 1221. Beside the early Lives stand the sworn testimony heard in 1253 before Clare was declared a saint, and papal documents from 1223 to 2026.
