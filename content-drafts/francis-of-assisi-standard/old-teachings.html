Francis wrote little, and simply. His Rules, his Testament, a handful of letters, short sayings called Admonitions, and his songs of praise survive. The passages below are our own English translations: the Latin from the 1904 Quaracchi critical edition, and the Canticle from the thirteenth-century Assisi manuscript. The Testament, the letter to Leo, the Praises, and the last will for Clare were checked against page scans of that edition; the other Latin was read in a scanned text. The famous prayer that begins “Lord, make me an instrument of your peace” is not his; it was first printed, unsigned, in Paris in 1912.

What was bitter became sweet

For Francis, conversion began with the thing he could least bear. The Lord “led” him among the lepers, he wrote, and what had been bitter turned into sweetness. His own account puts that change at the root of his new life.

“When I was in sins, it seemed too bitter to me to see lepers. The Lord himself led me among them, and I showed them mercy. And when I left them, what had seemed bitter to me turned into sweetness of soul and body.”

— Francis of Assisi, Testament, 1226 (our translation from the Latin)

He left these words in his Testament, near the end of his life, and put them first, before anything else he wanted his brothers to remember. He lived them: he kissed a leper’s hand on the road, gave alms in the leper house, and near death still wanted to go back and serve the lepers.

The form of the Gospel

Francis believed God had shown him a way of life that was simply the Gospel, lived literally. His brothers were to own nothing, because Christ had made himself poor.

“The Most High himself showed me that I should live according to the form of the holy Gospel. And I had it written in a few words and simply, and the lord pope confirmed it for me.”

— Francis of Assisi, Testament, 1226 (our translation from the Latin)

In the Rule of 1223 he wrote: “The brothers shall make nothing their own, not house, nor place, nor anything. … For the Lord made himself poor for us in this world.” He asked that his words be understood “simply and purely,” as they were written. When he heard the Gospel of the sending of the Twelve, he threw away his shoes, staff, and belt, and he died in a habit that had been lent to him.

Brothers who mother each other

Francis asked his brothers to care for each other with a mother’s tenderness, and he used the same image for every Christian who carries Christ in the heart.

“For if a mother feeds and loves her son in the flesh, how much more carefully should one love and feed his spiritual brother?”

— Francis of Assisi, Rule of 1223, ch. 6 (our translation from the Latin)

In his Letter to All the Faithful he wrote: “We are his mothers when we carry him in heart and body through love and a pure and sincere conscience.” In a note to Brother Leo, written in his own hand, he spoke “as a mother would,” and ended: “if you need it for your soul, or for some other comfort, and you want, Leo, to come to me, come.”

Mercy without limit

Francis could be severe with brothers who refused the Rule, but he asked those in charge to be merciful without condition. He told one minister to take whatever stood in his way, even from the brothers, as grace.

“No brother, however much he has sinned, should leave your presence without mercy, if he asks mercy; if he does not ask, you ask him.”

— Francis of Assisi, Letter to a Minister (our translation from the Latin)

He wrote this to a minister whose name the letter does not keep. On his deathbed he forgave all his brothers, “as well absent as present,” as Celano records.

Patience shows what we are

Francis held that a person’s worth is what he is before God, and that patience is tested only when others oppose us.

“You cannot tell how much patience and humility a servant of God has while everything pleases him. But when the people who should satisfy him do the opposite, he has as much patience and humility as he shows then, and no more.”

— Francis of Assisi, Admonition XIII (our translation from the Latin)

In another Admonition he wrote, “As much as a person is before God, so much he is, and no more.” And in another: “Where there is love and wisdom, there is neither fear nor ignorance. Where there is patience and humility, neither anger nor disturbance. Where there is poverty with joy, neither greed nor avarice.” The true peacemakers, he wrote, are those who keep peace in mind and body, whatever they suffer, for the love of Christ.

Praise through the creatures

Francis saw every creature as a brother or sister, and the whole world as a reason to praise God. He preached to birds and spoke to fire as “brother fire.”

“Praised be my Lord for our sister, mother earth, who sustains and governs us and brings forth many fruits with colored flowers and grass.”

— Francis of Assisi, Canticle of Brother Sun, San Damiano, about 1225 (our translation from the Umbrian Italian)

He made the Canticle, ill and nearly blind, in a hut near San Damiano, in the Italian of Umbria rather than Latin. Its last line sums up what he asked of everyone: “Praise and bless my Lord, give him thanks, and serve him with great humility.” Even death was “our sister, bodily death, from whom no living person can escape.”

Work and simplicity

Francis wanted his brothers to work with their hands, and he told those who could not read not to trouble about learning letters but to long for the Spirit of the Lord.

“And I worked with my own hands, and I want to work.”

— Francis of Assisi, Testament, 1226 (our translation from the Latin)

In the Testament he remembered the first days: “we were uneducated and subject to everyone.” The Rule of 1223 asks that preaching be brief and that brothers who cannot read not trouble to learn. He himself carried stones to rebuild San Damiano and begged for what he ate.

Poverty to the end

Francis wanted to follow the poverty that Christ and his mother had lived, and he asked Clare and her sisters never to leave it, whatever anyone advised them.

“I, little Brother Francis, want to follow the life and poverty of our most high Lord Jesus Christ and of his most holy mother, and to hold to it to the end.”

— Francis of Assisi, last will for Clare and her sisters (our translation from the Latin)

He wrote it shortly before he died, and Clare kept it by copying it into her own Rule. In his Testament he wrote of going back to “the Lord’s table,” begging alms from door to door. On his deathbed he would accept his habit only as a loan.

A servant to everyone

Francis called himself the servant of his brothers and of everyone. He gave up the government of the Order in 1220, saying, “Henceforth I am dead to you,” and promised obedience to the man who took his place.

“I think myself the greatest of sinners, for if God had followed any other wicked man with such great mercy he would have been tenfold more spiritual than I.”

— Francis of Assisi, in Thomas of Celano’s Second Life, 1244–47 (tr. A. G. Ferrers Howell)

He signed his letter to all Christians “brother Francis, their servant and subject,” and his Testament “your little one and servant.” When a brother urged him in his illness to have the Prophets read to him, he answered, “I need no more, my son: I know Christ, the poor man crucified.”
