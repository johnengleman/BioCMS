This list gathers the healings, visions, and other wonders attributed to St. Francis of Assisi, with their sources and their status. When Pope Gregory IX canonized Francis in 1228, his bull Mira circa nos spoke of “many brilliant miracles” but named none. The first group below is the list that Thomas of Celano, his first biographer, says was read aloud before the Pope at the canonization, after the cardinals had heard and examined the miracles brought to them. The bull names none of them, so we cannot say which ones the Church formally approved. The other accounts come from the early Lives (Celano, 1228–29 and 1244–47; the Legend of the Three Companions, 1246; Bonaventure, 1260s) or from later collections (the Mirror of Perfection, about 1318, and the Little Flowers, after 1322), and each entry says which. The list makes no claim of scientific proof, and it does not dismiss any account. Where a story is late, legendary, or was doubted, the entry says so.

A girl with a bent neck, Assisi, 1226

On the day Francis was buried, a girl whose neck was bent was healed beneath his coffin. Celano puts her first in his list of the miracles at the tomb.

Status: read before Pope Gregory IX at the canonization in 1228, according to Celano. The bull of canonization names no miracle.

During Francis’s life

The crucifix that spoke, San Damiano, about 1205–06

While Francis prayed in the ruined church of San Damiano, the painted crucifix spoke to him: “Francis, seest thou not that My House is being destroyed? Go therefore, and repair Me it.” In Celano’s Second Life the image moved its lips and called him by name. Celano’s First Life tells of his visit to the church but says nothing of a voice.

Status: reported in the early Lives (Legend of the Three Companions, 1246; Celano, Second Life, 1244–47); not in the earliest Life.

Francis had borrowed a poor man’s ass, and on the mountain the man collapsed with thirst. Fire that did not burn

Before a surgeon cauterized his face from the ear to the eyebrow for his eye disease, Francis spoke to the fire: “Be kind to me at this hour, be courteous, for I have loved thee of old in the Lord.” The brothers fled. When they came back he told them, “I felt no heat of fire nor any pain in my flesh,” and the surgeon said, “I have seen wondrous things to-day.”

Status: reported in the early Lives (Celano, Second Life §166, with the surgeon and brothers as witnesses; Bonaventure).

The stigmata, La Verna, 1224

Two years before his death, on La Verna, Francis saw a six-winged seraph fixed to a cross, and marks of nails appeared in his hands and feet, with a wound in his right side that bled. He hid them; Brother Rufino touched the side wound by accident, and Elias saw it. Bonaventure dates the vision about the feast of the Exaltation of the Cross and says that more than fifty brothers, Clare and her sisters, cardinals, and Pope Alexander IV saw the wounds. The bull of canonization does not mention them, and Bonaventure’s own stories show that some doubted them in the thirteenth century.

Status: reported in the early Lives (Celano, First Life; Bonaventure); contested then; venerated in the Catholic Church.

Source: Thomas of Celano, First Life (1228–29), §94–96, 112–113, tr. A. G. Ferrers Howell, 1908, https://archive.org/download/the-first-life-of-saint-francis-brother-thomas-of-celano

The body after death, and the knight Jerome

Brother Elias, writing within days, says that Francis’s body, rigid and marked by suffering in life, became after death very beautiful, shining with whiteness, and its stiff limbs became soft “like those of tender children” (our translation from the Latin). Celano and Bonaventure agree: after his death Francis’s flesh was white and his limbs soft, the nail marks black, “formed out of his flesh,” and the side red; Bonaventure compares it to “a rose most fair.” At Assisi a knight named Jerome, who doubted, moved the nails with his own fingers in front of his fellow citizens and then swore to it on the Gospels.

Status: reported by Elias’s letter of October 1226 and the early Lives (Celano, First Life; Bonaventure).

Source: Brother Elias, letter to the provinces, 1226, in Wadding, Annales Minorum, vol. 2 (1732), pp. 149–150 (page scan checked), https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_3Dn8hVWLUNwC

More accounts from Celano’s Second Life and the Mirror of Perfection

These accounts were added after a second reading of Celano’s Second Life (1244–47) and the Mirror of Perfection (about 1318). Celano names a witness for some of them; the Mirror is late and is marked so.

The leper who vanished

After Francis got down from his horse and kissed the leper, Celano’s Second Life says, he mounted again and looked round, and there was no leper anywhere on the open plain.

Status: reported in the early Lives (Celano, Second Life only; Francis’s own Testament tells the meeting without this detail).

Source: Thomas of Celano, Second Life (1244–47), §9, tr. A. G. Ferrers Howell, 1908, https://archive.org/details/livesofsfranciso00thom

The defeat at Damietta foretold, 29 August 1219

In the crusader camp before Damietta, Francis warned that if the army fought on a certain day it would lose. The soldiers laughed at him, attacked, and were routed; Celano counts six thousand killed and captured.

Status: reported in the early Lives (Celano, Second Life. Jacques de Vitry, who was in the crusader camp, also places Francis there).

Source: Thomas of Celano, Second Life (1244–47), §30, tr. A. G. Ferrers Howell, 1908, https://archive.org/details/livesofsfranciso00thom

Brother Leonard’s thought

On the road home from the East, Francis, riding an ass, got down at the very moment Brother Leonard was resenting, in silence, that the son of a merchant rode while he walked, and said it was not right that he should ride while Leonard went on foot.

Status: reported in the early Lives (Celano, Second Life; in Celano’s telling, Leonard himself confessed his thought to Francis).

Source: Thomas of Celano, Second Life (1244–47), §31, tr. A. G. Ferrers Howell, 1908, https://archive.org/details/livesofsfranciso00thom

Wolves and hail at Greccio

Francis promised the people of Greccio that if they confessed and changed their lives, the wolves and hailstorms that troubled them would stop. They did, Celano says, until the town fell back into its old ways.

Status: reported in the early Lives (Celano, Second Life; a moral tale in Celano’s telling).

Source: Thomas of Celano, Second Life (1244–47), §35–36, tr. A. G. Ferrers Howell, 1908, https://archive.org/details/livesofsfranciso00thom

Civil strife at Perugia foretold

Francis rebuked the people of Perugia for the evil they had done to their neighbors and warned them of what would follow; Celano says civil war broke out in the city days later. The Mirror of Perfection repeats the story.

Status: reported in the early Lives (Celano, Second Life; Mirror of Perfection).

Source: Thomas of Celano, Second Life (1244–47), §37, tr. A. G. Ferrers Howell, 1908, https://archive.org/details/livesofsfranciso00thom

Canon Gideon of Rieti

At Rieti, during his last illness, a dissolute canon named Gideon begged for the sign of the cross. Francis rebuked him, then blessed him, and Gideon was healed; Celano adds that he later died when a roof fell on him.

Status: reported in the early Lives (Celano, Second Life; with a moral of Celano’s own).

Source: Thomas of Celano, Second Life (1244–47), §41, tr. A. G. Ferrers Howell, 1908, https://archive.org/details/livesofsfranciso00thom

The nail parings

A brother of the Marsica, tormented by temptation, asked for Francis’s nail parings. Francis called for scissors at once and gave them to him, and the temptation left him.

Status: reported in the early Lives (Celano, Second Life).

Source: Thomas of Celano, Second Life (1244–47), §42, tr. A. G. Ferrers Howell, 1908, https://archive.org/details/livesofsfranciso00thom

The doctor’s dinner at Rieti

Francis told the brothers to give the eye doctor a good dinner, though they had almost nothing. A woman came to the door with a basket of fine bread, fish, crayfish pasties, honey, and grapes; the Mirror says she was sent by the lady of a castle. The doctor told the brothers that neither they nor the world knew Francis’s holiness as they ought.

Status: reported in the early Lives (Celano, Second Life, with the doctor as witness; the Mirror of Perfection places it at Fonte Colombo).

Source: Thomas of Celano, Second Life (1244–47), §44, tr. A. G. Ferrers Howell, 1908, https://archive.org/details/livesofsfranciso00thom

Source: The Mirror of Perfection (compiled about 1318), ch. CX, tr. Sebastian Evans, 1899, https://archive.org/details/mirrorofperfecti00leob

Cloth for six tunics

Francis asked his warden for cloth for one tunic, and a man at the gate offered cloth for six. “Thanks be to Him who seems to take thought for us alone!” Francis said.

Status: reported in the early Lives (Celano, Second Life).

Source: Thomas of Celano, Second Life (1244–47), §43, tr. A. G. Ferrers Howell, 1908, https://archive.org/details/livesofsfranciso00thom

Parsley in the dark

During his last illness the cook said he could not find parsley in the dark. Francis told him to bring the first herbs he touched, and among the wild herbs there was a leaf of parsley.

Status: reported in the early Lives (Celano, Second Life).

Source: Thomas of Celano, Second Life (1244–47), §51, tr. A. G. Ferrers Howell, 1908, https://archive.org/details/livesofsfranciso00thom

A famine foretold, and a visit after death

Francis told a brother that God would not allow famine on the earth while a certain servant of his lived. After Francis’s death he appeared to the same brother to say that the famine was now coming, and Celano writes that he and others witnessed the famine that followed.

Status: reported in the early Lives (Celano, Second Life).

Source: Thomas of Celano, Second Life (1244–47), §52–53, tr. A. G. Ferrers Howell, 1908, https://archive.org/details/livesofsfranciso00thom

“Welcome, Lady Poverty!”

On the road from Rieti to Siena to have his eyes treated, Francis met three poor women, who greeted him, “Welcome, Lady Poverty!” and then vanished. Celano calls it “a wondrous thing, of doubtful interpretation, but most certain truth.”

Status: reported in the early Lives (Celano, Second Life; the physician and brothers were with him).

Source: Thomas of Celano, Second Life (1244–47), §93, tr. A. G. Ferrers Howell, 1908, https://archive.org/details/livesofsfranciso00thom

The bishop who could not speak

Bishop Guido of Assisi once walked into Francis’s cell at the Portiuncula unannounced, and found himself trembling, unable to speak, and driven backward. His speech came back when he confessed his fault.

Status: reported in the early Lives (Celano, Second Life).

Source: Thomas of Celano, Second Life (1244–47), §100, tr. A. G. Ferrers Howell, 1908, https://archive.org/details/livesofsfranciso00thom

Warmth felt from afar

The abbot of San Giustino near Perugia asked Francis to pray for him. Francis stopped to pray, and the abbot, already some distance away, felt a sudden warmth.

Status: reported in the early Lives (Celano, Second Life).

Source: Thomas of Celano, Second Life (1244–47), §101, tr. A. G. Ferrers Howell, 1908, https://archive.org/details/livesofsfranciso00thom

Demons driven from Arezzo

When Arezzo was torn by civil war, Francis saw demons over the city and sent Brother Silvester to the gate to order them out in God’s name and his. Peace returned.

Status: reported in the early Lives (Celano, Second Life).

Source: Thomas of Celano, Second Life (1244–47), §108, tr. A. G. Ferrers Howell, 1908, https://archive.org/details/livesofsfranciso00thom

The sow and the lamb

Near Gubbio a sow killed a newborn lamb. Francis grieved for it and cursed the sow, which sickened and died within three days.

Status: reported in the early Lives (Celano, Second Life; a harsh tale, not the wolf of Gubbio).

Source: Thomas of Celano, Second Life (1244–47), §111, tr. A. G. Ferrers Howell, 1908, https://archive.org/details/livesofsfranciso00thom

The lute at Rieti

Ill at Rieti, Francis asked a brother to borrow a lute to soothe him, but the brother was afraid of what people would say. That night a lute was heard playing in the room, with no player to be seen.

Status: reported in the early Lives (Celano, Second Life).

Source: Thomas of Celano, Second Life (1244–47), §126, tr. A. G. Ferrers Howell, 1908, https://archive.org/details/livesofsfranciso00thom

A waterfowl, bees, a pheasant, and a cicada

A waterfowl given to Francis on the lake of Rieti stayed in his open hands while he prayed. A pheasant sent to him kept returning to his cell and would not eat away from him. Bees filled the cup he had used during a forty-day fast. A cicada on a fig tree near the Portiuncula came when he called her and sang with him, and stayed eight days until he gave her leave to go.

Status: reported in the early Lives (Celano, Second Life).

Source: Thomas of Celano, Second Life (1244–47), §167–171, tr. A. G. Ferrers Howell, 1908, https://archive.org/details/livesofsfranciso00thom

A hood saved from the fire, and bones on the altar

A brother’s hood thrown into a large fire was pulled out unharmed at Francis’s word. At Monte Casale, when the brothers neglected to move relics as he had told them, they later found the bones laid on the altar.

Status: reported in the early Lives (Celano, Second Life; of legendary character).

Source: Thomas of Celano, Second Life (1244–47), §154, §202, tr. A. G. Ferrers Howell, 1908, https://archive.org/details/livesofsfranciso00thom

A purse that became a snake, and a capon that became a fish

Near Bari, Celano says, a purse of coins that a brother wanted to pick up turned out to hold a snake. At Alessandria a man who kept a piece of capon from Francis’s table to shame him found that it looked to everyone like fish.

Status: of legendary character (Celano, Second Life).

Source: Thomas of Celano, Second Life (1244–47), §68, §78–79, tr. A. G. Ferrers Howell, 1908, https://archive.org/details/livesofsfranciso00thom

Peace between the bishop and the governor of Assisi, 1226

When the bishop and the governor of Assisi were at odds, Francis added a verse on pardon to his Canticle and sent two brothers to sing it before them. The governor forgave the bishop at once, and the two embraced.

Status: late account (Mirror of Perfection, compiled about 1318, from older memories of the companions).

Source: The Mirror of Perfection (compiled about 1318), ch. CI, tr. Sebastian Evans, 1899, https://archive.org/details/mirrorofperfecti00leob

The priest’s vineyard at Rieti

A priest near Rieti complained that visitors to Francis had ruined his vineyard. Francis promised him that it would yield at least twenty measures of wine, and it did.

Status: late account (Mirror of Perfection, compiled about 1318, from older memories of the companions).

Source: The Mirror of Perfection (compiled about 1318), ch. CIV, tr. Sebastian Evans, 1899, https://archive.org/details/mirrorofperfecti00leob

Chub in winter, and the bread that healed

Lying ill in the bishop’s palace, Francis said he could eat a little chub, and three large ones arrived from Rieti, though it was winter. A brother kept a morsel of the bread Francis blessed and shared before his death, and the Mirror says many sick people were healed after tasting it.

Status: late account (Mirror of Perfection, compiled about 1318, from older memories of the companions).

Source: The Mirror of Perfection (compiled about 1318), ch. CXI, LXXXVIII, tr. Sebastian Evans, 1899, https://archive.org/details/mirrorofperfecti00leob

After Francis’s death: other accounts

The bolster and the girdle, France and elsewhere

Within three years of the death, Celano wrote that in France “the king and queen and all the magnates ran to kiss and worship the bolster which Saint Francis had used in his sickness,” and that people were cured by pieces of his girdle.

Status: reported in the early Lives (Celano, First Life).

Source: Thomas of Celano, First Life (1228–29), §120, tr. A. G. Ferrers Howell, 1908, https://archive.org/download/the-first-life-of-saint-francis-brother-thomas-of-celano

Pope Gregory IX’s dream of the side wound

Pope Gregory IX, Bonaventure says, “felt some doubt and hesitation of mind with regard to the wound in the side.” Francis appeared to him in a dream, stern, lifted his arm, showed the wound, and told him to fill a vessel with its blood, and the Pope held it full to the brim. Gregory told the story, Bonaventure says, with many tears.

Status: reported in the early Lives (Bonaventure).

Source: Bonaventure, Legenda maior (1260s), ch. 15, English tr. (Washbourne, 1868), https://archive.org/download/St.BonaventureTheLifeOfSt.FrancisOfAssisi_201903

A doubting friar who washed the saint’s feet

A friar who preached and who doubted the stigmata saw Francis in a vision with muddy feet. He washed them and touched the places of the nails.

Status: reported in Bonaventure; of legendary character.

Source: Bonaventure, Legenda maior (1260s), ch. 15, English tr. (Washbourne, 1868), https://archive.org/download/St.BonaventureTheLifeOfSt.FrancisOfAssisi_201903

John of Ilerda, stabbed

In Ilerda in Catalonia a man named John was stabbed so deeply that the matter from the wound put out six candles. Francis, in a friar’s habit, came in by the window and unwrapped his bandages, and John was healed.

Status: reported in the early Lives (Bonaventure).

Source: Bonaventure, Legenda maior (1260s), ch. 15, English tr. (Washbourne, 1868), https://archive.org/download/St.BonaventureTheLifeOfSt.FrancisOfAssisi_201903

Roger of Potenza and the wounded palm

Roger, a canon of Potenza, doubted the stigmata as he looked at a picture of Francis, and he received a wound like an arrow’s in his palm. He was healed after he confessed. Bonaventure says the account was authenticated by the bishop’s seal.

Status: reported in the early Lives (Bonaventure; the seal is mentioned only by him).

Source: Bonaventure, Legenda maior (1260s), ch. 15, English tr. (Washbourne, 1868), https://archive.org/download/St.BonaventureTheLifeOfSt.FrancisOfAssisi_201903

The Roman lady’s picture

A woman of Rome saw the stigmata appear on a painted portrait of Francis. When she doubted, they vanished.

Status: legendary (reported in Bonaventure).

Source: Bonaventure, Legenda maior (1260s), ch. 15, English tr. (Washbourne, 1868), https://archive.org/download/St.BonaventureTheLifeOfSt.FrancisOfAssisi_201903

“Many” raised from the dead

Bonaventure claims that many were raised from the dead through Francis. The sources read for this list give no names.

Status: reported in the early Lives (Bonaventure; no named cases read).

Source: Bonaventure, Legenda maior (1260s), ch. 15, English tr. (Washbourne, 1868), https://archive.org/download/St.BonaventureTheLifeOfSt.FrancisOfAssisi_201903
