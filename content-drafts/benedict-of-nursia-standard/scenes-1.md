# Benedict of Nursia: scene cards, group 1 (SC-01 to SC-05)

Main source for all five cards: W-S1 = Gregory the Great, *Dialogues* II, English by "P.W." (Paris 1608), ed. E. G. Gardner (London 1911). Archive.org id cu31924029222076, OCR text read this session. Rights: public-domain. The Latin original was not reached in three tries (Latin Library pages carry no text). So all quotes below are the 1608 English, each with a `plain:` line. The plain lines are our own English, checked against the 1608 text only, not against Gregory's Latin. Page numbers are the 1911 book pages as printed in the OCR.

## SC-01 — The broken sieve (Effide, St Peter's church)
- Status: full
- When and where: undated. Gardner's note says Benedict was born about 480 and left Rome "probably a few years before 500" [W-S1n, Intro p.51 note]. The CE argues he was at least 19-20 [W-S2, via notes-witnesses]. Gregory gives no age and no date. Place: "Enside" in the 1608 text, which Gardner says to read as Enfide, now the mountain village of Affile, between Olevano and Subiaco [W-S1n ch.1 p.52]. Church of St Peter.
- Who was there: Benedict; his nurse, "which did tenderly love him"; "other virtuous men, which for charity lived in that place"; the neighbours who owned the sieve; the townsmen [W-S1 p.52].
- What happened, in order:
  1. Benedict gives up school, his father's house and his wealth, "with a resolute mind only to serve God" [W-S1 p.51].
  2. His nurse alone will not give him up and follows. They stay at Enside in St Peter's church with other devout men [p.52].
  3. The nurse borrows a sieve to clean wheat. It is left on the table and breaks in two. She weeps, "because she had borrowed it" [p.52].
  4. Benedict, moved by pity, takes both pieces away, prays with tears, and gets up to find it whole with no sign of the break [p.52].
  5. He comforts her with good words and hands the sieve back [p.52].
  6. Everyone nearby hears of it. The townsmen hang the sieve at the church door as a memory [p.52].
  7. Benedict, "desiring rather the miseries of the world than the praises of men", slips away from his nurse in secret toward Subiaco [p.52-53].
- Details: the sieve hangs over the church door "many years after, even to these very troubles of the Lombards" [p.52]. Gregory says the nurse's tears were about owing the neighbours for a thing she borrowed. Gregory calls Benedict "devout and religious youth" [p.52].
- Words spoken: none recorded. Gregory says only "good words" of comfort.
- Inner life: none from Benedict. Gregory's reason for the flight is the "desiring rather ... than the praises of men" line [p.52].
- Testimony (Gregory, W-S1 p.51, public-domain). Why he left Rome:
  > "But for as much as he saw many by reason of such learning to fall to dissolute and lewd life, he drew back his foot, which he had as it were now set forth into the world, lest, entering too far in acquaintance therewith, he likewise might have fallen into that dangerous and godless gulf: wherefore, giving over his book, and forsaking his father's house and wealth, with a resolute mind only to serve God, he sought for some place, where he might attain to the desire of his holy purpose: and in this sort he departed, instructed with learned ignorance, and furnished with unlearned wisdom."
  
  plain: He saw that many people who studied in Rome ended up in loose and bad lives. So he stepped back before he went too deep into that world, in case he fell into the same pit. He gave up his books, his father's house and his money. He wanted only to serve God, so he went looking for a place to do it. He left with a learned ignorance and an unlearned wisdom.
- Sieve passage (exact, W-S1 p.52): "it fell so out that his nurse borrowed of the neighbours a sieve to make clean wheat, which being left negligently upon the table, by chance it was broken in two pieces: whereupon she fell pitifully a weeping, because she had borrowed it."
  
  plain: His nurse borrowed a sieve from the neighbours to clean wheat. It was left carelessly on the table and broke in two. She wept, because it was not hers.
- What changed: he is known in the town on the first day he leaves the world, so he runs from the praise.
- Not in the sources: his age in Gregory's words; his parents' names; the nurse's name; what the schools or his teachers were; any words from Benedict to the nurse; how she took his leaving; how he got away. Do not describe the sieve's shape or the church.
- Sources: W-S1 p.51-53; W-S1n (Gardner notes, Intro p.51 and ch.1 p.52); W-S2 for the 19-20 argument (from notes, not re-read).

## SC-02 — Bread on a rope (Subiaco cave)
- Status: full
- When and where: Subiaco ("Sublacum"), "distant almost forty miles from Rome". A spring makes a lake, then a river. The cave is a "strait cave" under a high rock. Gardner says it is now the Sagro Speco, high on the mountain-side above the town [W-S1n ch.1 p.53]. Duration: three years [W-S1 p.53]. No dates.
- Who was there: Benedict; the monk Romanus, who lived "not far off, under the rule of Abbot Theodacus" (Gardner: also written Adeodatus) [p.53].
- What happened, in order:
  1. On the road, Romanus meets him, asks where he goes, hears his purpose, keeps it secret, helps him, and gives him "the habit of holy conversation" [p.53].
  2. Benedict lives in the cave three years, "unknown to all men" except Romanus [p.53].
  3. Romanus steals hours from his own duties, and sometimes a loaf from his own food, and carries it to him [p.53].
  4. No path runs from Romanus's cell to the cave, because a high rock hangs over it. So Romanus lets the loaf down on a long rope from the top [p.53].
  5. He ties a little bell to the rope, so Benedict hears when he comes and is ready [p.53].
  6. One day "the old enemy of mankind", envying them, throws a stone and breaks the bell. Romanus keeps serving "by all the possible means he could" [p.53].
- Details: the rope, the bell, the rock, a loaf. Gregory's reading: God wanted Benedict's life known "as an example", like a candle on a candlestick [p.53].
- Words spoken: none.
- Testimony (Gregory, W-S1 p.53, public-domain):
  > "And because from Romanus' cell to that cave there was not any way, by reason of an high rock which did hang over it, Romanus, from the top thereof, upon a long rope, did let down the loaf, upon which also with a band he tied a little bell, that by the ringing thereof the man of God might know when he came with his bread, and so be ready to take it."
  
  plain: There was no path from Romanus's cell to the cave, because a high rock hung over it. So Romanus let the loaf down from the top on a long rope. He tied a small bell to the rope, so Benedict would hear it ring and know that the bread had come, and be ready to take it.
- What changed: Benedict has the solitude he wanted, kept alive by one friend and a thin rope.
- Not in the sources: any talk between Romanus and Benedict; what Benedict ate besides bread; what he prayed; the cave's furniture; the cold; how the habit looked; how Romanus found the cave. The stone is the devil's, in Gregory's words; do not say who threw it in human terms.
- Sources: W-S1 p.53; W-S1n ch.1 (Sagro Speco; Theodacus/Adeodatus).

## SC-03 — Easter in the cave
- Status: full
- When and where: Easter Sunday, year not given, during the three years in the Subiaco cave. The priest lives "a good way off" and has to search "amongst the steep hills, the low valleys and hollow pits" [W-S1 p.53-54].
- Who was there: Benedict; an unnamed priest.
- What happened, in order:
  1. God appears to the priest as he has his Easter dinner ready [p.53].
  2. The priest takes the food and goes up Easter day itself and hunts for the "man of God" in the hills and hollows until he finds the cave [p.54].
  3. They pray together, sit, give thanks to God, and talk of spiritual things at length [p.54].
  4. The priest says it is Easter and they should eat. Benedict answers [p.54].
  5. The priest insists: it is the feast of the Resurrection, so fasting is not right today, and he is sent to eat with him [p.54].
  6. They say grace and eat. After talk, the priest goes back to his church [p.54].
- Details: Gregory explains that Benedict had been away from people so long that he did not know it was Easter [p.54]. Right after, shepherds find him; they see his clothes of animal skins through the bushes and take him for "some beast"; many are turned to a better life, and his name spreads [p.54].
- Words spoken (W-S1 p.53-54, 1608 English, exact):
  - God to the priest: "Thou hast provided good cheer for thyself, and my servant in such a place is afflicted with hunger."
    plain: You have prepared a good meal for yourself, while my servant in such a place is hungry.
  - Priest: "Rise up, brother, and let us dine, because to-day is the feast of Easter."
    plain: Get up, brother, and let us eat, because today is the feast of Easter.
  - Benedict: "I know that it is Easter with me and a great feast, having found so much favour at God's hands as this day to enjoy your company."
    plain: It is Easter for me, and a great feast, because God has shown me this much kindness, to enjoy your company today.
  - Priest: "Verily, to-day is the feast of our Lord's Resurrection, and therefore meet it is not that you should keep abstinence, and besides I am sent to that end, that we might eat together of such provision as God's goodness hath sent us."
    plain: Truly, today is the feast of our Lord's Resurrection. So it is not right for you to fast. I am also sent for this reason: so that we may eat together the food God has sent us.
- Testimony (Gregory, W-S1 p.54, public-domain): the three lines above are the testimony; the full exchange above is within the 200-word limit.
- What changed: the hermit's secret ends. The priest, then the shepherds, carry his name out, and visitors start to come.
- Not in the sources: the priest's name or church; the food; the year; weather; whether Benedict argued or wept; any words of the shepherds. Do not say Benedict was thin or ragged beyond "apparel made of skins". Gregory gives the priest's insistence, not Benedict's tears.
- Sources: W-S1 p.53-54.

## SC-04 — The blackbird and the briers
- Status: full
- When and where: "upon a certain day", year not given, alone in the Subiaco wilderness, after the Easter visit [W-S1 p.55]. Gardner's note: the briers are said to have become the roses still seen at the Sagro Speco garden; this is "piously said", not Gregory [W-S1n ch.2 p.55].
- Who was there: Benedict alone. Gregory says Benedict told the story to his disciples himself: "as himself did afterward report unto his disciples" [p.55].
- What happened, in order:
  1. A little black bird, "a merle or an ousel", flies around his face, so near he could have caught it by hand [p.55].
  2. He makes the sign of the cross and the bird flies off [p.55].
  3. At once he is hit by a temptation of the flesh worse than any in his life. The memory of a woman he once saw is put in his mind; he nearly wants to leave the wilderness [p.55].
  4. Helped by grace, "he came to himself". He sees thick briers and nettles close by, takes off his clothes, and rolls in them until his flesh is torn [p.55].
  5. Gregory's reading: by the wounds of his body he healed the wounds of his soul. From then on he never felt that temptation again [p.55].
  6. Many then leave the world to become his pupils [p.55].
- Details: the bird, the briers, the nettles, his torn flesh. Gregory says the bird "might have" been taken by hand. The woman is not named.
- Words spoken: none recorded.
- Inner life: only through Gregory's report of what Benedict told his disciples. Benedict said it ended for good.
- Testimony (Gregory, W-S1 p.55, public-domain):
  > "But, suddenly assisted with God's grace, he came to himself; and seeing many thick briers and nettle-bushes to grow hard by, off he cast his apparel, and threw himself into the midst of them, and there wallowed so long that, when he rose up, all his flesh was pitifully torn: and so by the wounds of his body, he cured the wounds of his soul ... From which time forward, as himself did afterward report unto his disciples, he found all temptation of pleasure so subdued, that he never felt any such thing."
  
  plain: Then, helped suddenly by God's grace, he came to his senses. He saw many thick briers and nettles close by. He threw off his clothes and threw himself into the middle of them. He rolled there so long that when he got up, his whole body was badly torn. So by the wounds of his body he healed the wounds of his soul ... From that time on, as he himself told his disciples later, he found all temptation of this kind so beaten down that he never felt it again.
  (The "..." cuts Gregory's longer reflection on turning pleasure into pain.)
- What changed: he is free of this temptation for life, and Gregory says this is why he becomes a "master of virtue" and others come to him.
- Not in the sources: the woman's name; his age; the season; any prayer words; any doctor or healing of the wounds; the exact kind of bird beyond "merle or ousel" (blackbird); the "roses" (only a later local tale in Gardner's note). Tell the temptation plainly and briefly.
- Sources: W-S1 p.54-55; W-S1n ch.2 p.55.

## SC-05 — The poisoned glass
- Status: full
- When and where: after the briers; year not given. A monastery "not far from the place where he remained" [W-S1 p.56]. Gregory does not name it. Gardner's note says it "is said to have been at Varia (the modern Vicovaro)", near Mandela [W-S1n ch.3 p.56]. Say "a monastery nearby".
- Who was there: Benedict; the whole community of a monastery whose abbot had died. Gregory names no monk.
- What happened, in order:
  1. The abbot is dead. The whole convent comes to Benedict and begs him very earnestly to take charge [p.56].
  2. He refuses for a long time. He says their ways differ from his and they will never agree [p.56].
  3. At last, "overcome with their entreaty", he agrees [p.56].
  4. He sees that regular life is kept, so that none can stray from the path "on the one side or on the other" [p.56].
  5. The monks rage. They accuse themselves for having asked for him; they cannot live "in unlawful sort" under him, and they are loath to give up old ways [p.56].
  6. Some plot to kill him and agree to poison his wine [p.56].
  7. By custom the glass is handed to the abbot to bless. He puts out his hand and makes the sign of the cross. The glass, held far off, breaks in pieces "as though the sign of the cross had been a stone thrown against it" [p.56].
  8. He knows the glass held "the drink of death". He stands, calls the monks together, and speaks "with a mild countenance and quiet mind" [p.56].
  9. He leaves, goes back to the wilderness, and lives alone "with himself, in the sight of his Creator" [p.56-57].
- Details: wine; a glass; the custom of offering the cup to the abbot to bless. Gregory's gloss on "dwelt with himself" (habitare secum): had he stayed against his own mind, he might have lost his own devotion and the eyes of his soul might have turned from contemplation, and "he should both have lost himself, and yet not found them" [p.57]. Gardner also notes Rule ch.2 on the abbot who has done all he can with a stubborn flock [W-S1n ch.3 p.56].
- Words spoken (Benedict, W-S1 p.56-57, exact 1608 English):
  > "Almighty God have mercy upon you, and forgive you: why have you used me in this manner? Did not I tell you before hand, that our manner of living could never agree together? Go your ways, and seek ye out some other father suitable to your own conditions, for I intend not now to stay any longer amongst you."
  
  plain: May almighty God have mercy on you and forgive you. Why have you treated me like this? Did I not tell you beforehand that our ways of life could never go together? Go, and find another father who suits your own ways, because I will not stay with you any longer.
- Inner life: Gregory's gloss only ("dwelt with himself"). No words of prayer from Benedict.
- Testimony: the speech above is the testimony (Gregory, W-S1 p.56-57, public-domain, 61 words).
- What changed: his first try at leading a community fails, and he learns that a way of life cannot be forced; he goes back alone.
- Not in the sources: the monks' names; the abbot's name; how many monks; any shouting; whether he drank; what the monks said back; how long he stayed as abbot; how he left or whether anyone stopped him. Gregory does not give "Vicovaro". Do not mix this cup with the later loaf of Florentius (SC-07).
- Sources: W-S1 p.56-58; W-S1n ch.3 p.56.
