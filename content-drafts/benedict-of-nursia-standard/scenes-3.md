## SC-11 — Scholastica's storm
- Status: full
- When and where: One day of her yearly visit, from daytime into night. A place "not far from the gate" that "did belong to the Abbey", near Monte Cassino. Gregory gives no year and no month [W-S1 p.94]. Gardner gives the tradition of 10 Feb 543 (from notes-witnesses; Gregory says nothing of it).
- Who was there: Benedict; his sister Scholastica, "dedicated from her infancy to our Lord"; "his monks" who went with him. Gregory's listener Peter is only the audience [W-S1 p.94-95].
- What happened, in order:
  1. Gregory opens by saying Benedict "would have done" one thing "and yet he could not" [W-S1 p.94].
  2. Scholastica comes once a year. Benedict goes with his monks to meet her. They spend the whole day "in the praises of God and spiritual talk" [W-S1 p.94].
  3. Near night they eat supper together. As they sit at the table, she asks him to stay all night and talk of the joys of heaven [W-S1 p.94].
  4. He refuses: he "might not by any means tarry all night out of his Abbey". The sky was so clear that no cloud was seen [W-S1 p.94].
  5. She joins her hands, lays them on the table, bows her head on them and prays. When she lifts her head, lightning, thunder and heavy rain fall. No one can put a head out of the door [W-S1 p.94].
  6. Gregory adds that she wept a "flood of tears" on the table, and that the rain came in the same instant she raised her head [W-S1 p.94-95].
  7. Benedict complains. She answers (below). He is "not able to go forth" and stays "against his will". They stay awake all night and "comfort one another" with spiritual talk [W-S1 p.95].
  8. Next day she goes back to her convent ("Nunnery") and he to the abbey [W-S1 p.96, ch.34].
  9. Three days later, standing in his cell and lifting his eyes, he sees her soul "in the likeness of a dove" go up to heaven. He thanks God with "hymns and lauds", tells his monks, and sends them to bring her body to his abbey, to be buried in the grave he had made ready for himself [W-S1 p.96].
- Details: A table with supper on it. Clear sky, then storm. Gregory's moral: "a woman which of long time had not seen her brother" could do more than he, "God is charity", so "she did more which loved more" [W-S1 p.95]. Gregory's frame (ch.33 opening): Paul asked three times to have the thorn removed and was refused, so even a saint does not get all he wants.
- Words spoken:
  - Scholastica's request: Gregory gives it only in summary ("entreated him to stay there all night, that they might spend it in discoursing of the joys of heaven"). No exact words.
  - Benedict: "God forgive you, what have you done?" [W-S1 p.95]. plain: "May God forgive you. What have you done?"
  - Scholastica: "I desired you to stay, and you would not hear me, I have desired our good Lord, and he hath vouchsafed to grant my petition: wherefore if you can now depart, a God's name return to your monastery, and leave me here alone." [W-S1 p.95]. plain: "I asked you to stay and you would not listen to me. So I asked our good Lord, and he has agreed to my request. Now, if you can leave, go back to your monastery in God's name, and leave me here alone."
- Inner life: Gregory says what Benedict wanted: "no question but he would have had the same fair weather to have continued" [W-S1 p.95]. Benedict himself says nothing about his state of mind. Gregory reads Scholastica's prayer as love.
- Testimony (Gregory, W-S1 p.94, public-domain; excerpt of about 150 words):
  > And she coming thither on a time according to her custom, her venerable brother with his monks went to meet her, where they spent the whole day in the praises of God and spiritual talk : and when it was almost night they supped together, and as they were yet sitting at the table, talking of devout matters, and darkness came on, the holy Nun his sister entreated him to stay there all night, that they might spend it in discoursing of the joys of heaven. But by no persuasion would he agree unto that, saying that he might not by any means tarry all night out of his Abbey. At that time, the sky was so clear that no cloud was to be seen.

  plain: She came, as she did each year. Her brother went with his monks to meet her, and they spent the whole day praising God and talking of holy things. Near night they ate supper together. As they sat at the table, with darkness coming on, she asked him to stay all night so that they could talk of the joys of heaven. He would not agree. He said he could not stay a whole night away from his abbey. The sky was so clear that not one cloud could be seen.
- What changed: The strict abbot stays the night against his will; Gregory says her love moved God where his rule did not. Three days later she is dead and shares his grave.
- Not in the sources: no place name for the meeting house; no name for her convent in Gregory (CE "Benedictine Order", newadvent.org/cathen/02443a.htm, says a convent about five miles from Monte Cassino; Dudden "Plumbariola" is "supposed", per notes); no year; no twin; no words from Scholastica before the dialogue; no description of her face or dress; no list of what they ate; no word of what they said all night (only "spiritual and heavenly talk"); the dove is Benedict's vision, no one else saw it; no one else is said to have felt the storm as a miracle except Gregory.
- Sources: W-S1 p.93-96 (read in full); CE (newadvent.org/cathen/02443a.htm, public-domain, English, read).

## SC-12 — The tower window
- Status: full (English text repaired from OCR; Latin not reached)
- When and where: A night, "in the dead of the night", before matins. A tower at Monte Cassino with one flight of stairs. Benedict slept at the top, deacon Servandus at the foot. A large room before the tower held both men's disciples [W-S1 p.96]. Gregory gives no date.
- Who was there: Benedict. Servandus, deacon and abbot of the monastery that the noble Liberius founded in Campania; he came often because he was "full of heavenly doctrine". The monks of both houses slept in the large room. Theoprobus ("the religious man") is sent to Cassino town that night. Germanus, Bishop of Capua, is the soul Benedict sees [W-S1 p.96-97].
- What happened, in order:
  1. Servandus visits often. They talk of heaven, to "taste of those joys" with longing, since they cannot yet "perfectly feed" on them [W-S1 p.96].
  2. At bedtime Benedict goes up the tower. He rises early, before matins, while the monks still rest. He goes to the window of his chamber and prays [W-S1 p.97].
  3. In the dead of night he sees a light brighter than day. As he later told it, the whole world was gathered under one beam of the sun before his eyes [W-S1 p.97].
  4. He sees the soul of Germanus carried by angels "in a fiery globe" to heaven [W-S1 p.97].
  5. He wants a witness. He calls Servandus by name, in a very loud voice, two or three times. Servandus, troubled by the cry, goes up in haste. He sees only "a little remnant of the light" [W-S1 p.97].
  6. Benedict tells him all. He sends to Cassino and tells Theoprobus to send a man to Capua that night. The messenger finds Germanus dead, and learns he died at that very time [W-S1 p.97].
  7. Peter says he cannot see how one man can see the whole world. Gregory answers: to the soul that sees the Creator, all creatures are "nothing"; the soul is "enlarged" and "above itself" [W-S1 p.97-98].
- Details: Light that "banished away the darkness". The globe is "fiery". Heaven and earth were not made smaller; Gregory says the "soul of the beholder" was enlarged [W-S1 p.98].
- Words spoken: Benedict called Servandus "by his name" "with a very loud voice"; no words given beyond that. Gregory to Peter [W-S1 p.98]: "all creatures be as it were nothing to that soul which beholdeth the Creator". plain: "To a soul that sees the Creator, all created things are as good as nothing."
- Inner life: Gregory's explanation: "being rapt up in the light of God, it is inwardly in itself enlarged above itself, and when it is so exalted and looketh downward, then doth it comprehend how little all that is" [W-S1 p.98]. plain: "Caught up into God's light, the soul grows wider than itself. From that height it looks down and sees how small everything is." This is Gregory's reading, not Benedict's own words. "As himself did afterward report" shows Benedict was the source.
- Testimony (Gregory, W-S1 p.97, public-domain; OCR repaired; about 95 words):
  > Standing there, all on a sudden in the dead of the night, as he looked forth, he saw a light, which banished away the darkness of the night, and glittered with such brightness, that the light which did shine in the midst of darkness was far more clear than the light of the day. Upon this sight a marvellous strange thing followed, for, as himself did afterward report, the whole world, gathered as it were together under one beam of the sun, was presented before his eyes

  plain: As he stood there, suddenly, in the dead of night, he looked out and saw a light that drove the darkness away. It shone brighter than daylight. Then something strange and wonderful followed. As he told it himself later, the whole world was gathered together, as if under one ray of the sun, and shown before his eyes.
  OCR note: the OCR of p.97 is garbled at "marvellous" and at "the whole world, gathered as it were together under one beam of the sun" (it reads "wholgjworld ... tngpther. undgr one beam oFlihe s,an^jwas"). I repaired both. The repair of "one beam of the sun" is confirmed by Peter's restatement two sentences later on the same page, which OCRs cleanly: "the whole world, as it were under one sunbeam, was presented before his eyes" [W-S1 p.97-98]. A check against a clean scan is still open.
- What changed: Benedict has the answer to his early wish. The soul that sees God sees creation as small. Servandus and Theoprobus can confirm the death hour.
- Not in the sources: the bishop's age, name of the Capua messenger, how many miles, what the stars or moon looked like; no word on Benedict's posture beyond "standing"; no word that he wept; no word on what Servandus said when he came up; the tower's name or place within the abbey. The "window of his chamber" is Gregory's phrase.
- Latin: Gregory's Latin was not reached (la.wikisource, documentacatholicaomnia, thelatinlibrary: no text). Lead only: a Benedict feast antiphon on cantusindex.org carries a Latin phrase "velut sub uno solis radio" (seen only in a search summary; not a fetched quotation). Do not quote it.
- Sources: W-S1 p.96-98.

## SC-13 — Death standing in the chapel
- Status: full
- When and where: "The same year" Gregory says he told the day of his death. Death on the sixth day after the grave was opened. Place: the oratory at Monte Cassino. Gregory gives no year, month or day [W-S1 p.99].
- Who was there: Benedict; his disciples (they held him up); "some of which did live daily with him, and some dwelt far off" (he told the near ones to keep it secret and gave the far ones a sign) [W-S1 p.99]. Two unnamed monks, one in his cell and one far away, had the vision.
- What happened, in order:
  1. In the year of his death he tells the monks the day. He tells those present to keep it secret and tells the absent what sign to watch for [W-S1 p.99].
  2. Six days before, he orders his sepulchre opened [W-S1 p.99].
  3. A fever follows; "burning heat" and weakness grow every day [W-S1 p.99].
  4. On the sixth day he has the monks carry him into the oratory. He receives the body and blood of Christ [W-S1 p.99].
  5. His disciples hold up his weak body. He stands with his hands lifted to heaven, praying, and dies [W-S1 p.99].
  6. The same day, two monks (one in his cell, one far off) see one vision: a road from his cell, east to heaven, hung with tapestry and shining with lamps. A man in reverent dress asks who passes this way. They do not know. He tells them [W-S1 p.99-100].
  7. The near monks knew he was dead; the far monks knew by the sign he had given [W-S1 p.100].
  8. He is buried in the oratory of St. John the Baptist, which he built when he overthrew the altar of Apollo. Gregory adds that in the cave where he first lived he still works miracles "if the faith of them that pray requireth the same" [W-S1 p.100].
- Details: Tapestry and "infinite number of lamps" on the road. Burial place is his own built oratory.
- Words spoken: The man on the road [W-S1 p.100]: "This is the way, by which the beloved servant of God, Bennet, is ascended up to heaven." plain: "This is the way by which Benedict, the beloved servant of God, has gone up to heaven." Benedict's own last words are not given.
- Inner life: none given by Gregory. He only says Benedict was "praying" at the end.
- Testimony (Gregory, W-S1 p.99, public-domain; about 120 words):
  > Six days before he left this world, he gave order to have his sepulchre opened, and forthwith falling into an ague, he began with burning heat to wax faint, and when as the sickness daily increased, upon the sixth day he commanded his monks to carry him into the oratory, where he did arm himself with receiving the body and blood of our Saviour Christ ; and having his weak body holden up betwixt the hands of his disciples, he stood with his own lifted up to heaven, and as he was in that manner praying, he gave up the ghost.

  plain: Six days before he left this world, he ordered his grave to be opened. Soon after, he fell sick with a fever, and the burning heat made him weak. As the illness grew each day, on the sixth day he told his monks to carry him into the oratory. There he strengthened himself by receiving the body and blood of Christ. His weak body was held up by his disciples' hands. He stood with his own hands raised to heaven, and while he was praying like this, he died.
  OCR note: the p.99 OCR has a stray "the*" before "sickness"; I removed it.
- What changed: Benedict dies in prayer among his monks. Two witnesses see the road to heaven.
- Not in the sources: no year or date in Gregory (the 543/547 question is for the outline's accuracy note); no last words; no weeping of the monks; no weather; no word on his age; no word on who carried him by name; no Scholastica grave detail beyond ch.34. Gregory does not say it was an altar Mass, only that he received the Body and Blood. The tomb is "the same" grave as Scholastica's only by ch.34 (grave "provided for himself"); ch.37 names the oratory of St. John.
- Sources: W-S1 p.99-100.

## SC-14 — The monks escape to Rome (589-593)
- Status: thin (the sack date cannot be fixed; Paul the Deacon not reached)
- When and where: Night, Monte Cassino, "not long since" before Gregory's writing [W-S1 p.78-79]. Gregory wrote the *Dialogues* in 593 [W-S1 introduction, Gardner]. Sack date: Gardner (note on ch.17) says 589, by the Lombards of the duchy of Benevento. CE "Benedictine Order" says "about the year 580". Wikipedia "Monte Cassino" says "around 570" at the top, "580" in a quotation, and "In 581, during the abbacy of Bonitus" later. The sources do not agree; say "in the 580s" or "about 580-589".
- Who was there: The monks of Monte Cassino; the Lombards. Valentinian "many years had the charge of the Lateran Abbey"; Constantine (Benedict's next abbot), Simplicius (third), Honoratus (abbot of Subiaco) [W-S1 p.51]. Gregory (writing as pope, 593).
- What happened, in order:
  1. Benedict had told Theoprobus that the abbey would be "delivered to the gentiles, to be spoiled and overthrown", and that he scarcely won the monks' lives from God [W-S1 p.78].
  2. Gregory says the fulfilment is now seen: the Lombards "entered in" at night, while the monks slept, and "spoiled all things"; "not one man could they retain there" [W-S1 p.79].
  3. CE: when the Lombards sacked Monte Cassino about 580, the monks fled to Rome. Pope Pelagius II housed them in a monastery next to the Lateran Basilica, and they stayed over 140 years (CE, newadvent.org/cathen/02443a.htm).
  4. Valentinian ran the Lateran abbey; he was one of the four whom Gregory names as his source for Benedict's life [W-S1 p.51].
  5. In 593, three years after he became pope, Gregory wrote the *Dialogues* at the request of monks of his household [Gardner intro, quoting Gregory's letter to Maximianus].
- Details: Gregory compares Benedict to St. Paul: the ship lost its goods but all lives were saved [W-S1 p.79]. Hanging in the church at Effide was the sieve "even to these very troubles of the Lombards" [W-S1 p.52].
- Words spoken (Gregory to Maximianus, Bishop of Syracuse, as quoted in Gardner's introduction, public-domain, 1911 English; the sentence is cut where I stopped reading):
  > "My brethren who dwell familiarly with me ... would have me by all means write something in brief fashion concerning the miracles of the Fathers, which we have heard wrought in Italy. For this purpose I earnestly need the help of your charity, that you should briefly inform me of all those which come back to your memory, or which you have happened yourself to know."

  plain: "The brothers who live close to me want me to write, briefly, about the miracles of the Fathers that we have heard were done in Italy. For this I need your kind help. Please tell me, in short, every one of them that you remember or have seen yourself." (The full letter is not read; the Latin letter itself was not reached.)
- Testimony (Gregory, W-S1 p.51, public-domain, about 80 words):
  > All the notable things and acts of his life I could not learn; but those few, which I mind now to report, I had by the relation of four of his disciples: to wit, of Constantinus, a most rare and reverent man, who was next Abbot after him; of Valentinianus, who many years had the charge of the Lateran Abbey; of Simplicius, who was the third General of his order; and lastly of Honoratus, who is now Abbot of that monastery in which he first began his holy life.

  plain: I could not learn all the notable things in his life. The few I will tell, I had from four of his disciples. They are Constantine, a rare and respected man who became abbot after him; Valentinian, who for many years ran the Lateran abbey; Simplicius, the third head of his order; and Honoratus, now abbot of the monastery where Benedict first began his holy life.
- What changed: The abbey falls as Benedict said, but the monks live; their stay in Rome puts Benedict's disciples in Gregory's reach.
- Not in the sources: the Lombard leader's name (tried: Paul the Deacon IV.17 not reached; latinlibrary page missing; Foulke 1907 text on archive.org did not match "Cassino"); the month of the sack; the number of monks; how long the walk to Rome took; the name of the abbot at the time of the sack (Wikipedia says Bonitus; unchecked in a primary source); that Gregory knew Valentinian personally in Rome (W-S1 p.51 says only that he had it "by relation"); that the monks carried the Rule to Rome (no source read in this session says so).
- Sources: W-S1 p.51, 52, 78-79; Gardner introduction and note on ch.17 (W-S1n; same archive text); CE "The Benedictine Order" (newadvent.org/cathen/02443a.htm, public-domain, 1907); Wikipedia "Monte Cassino" (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Cassino, CC BY-SA; dates unreliable).

## SC-15 — Bombs and a new church (1944-1964)
- Status: full for 1964; the bombing has no named eyewitness
- When and where: Monte Cassino. Bombing 15 Feb 1944. Consecration 24 Oct 1964.
- Who was there: Allied air forces and Gen. Sir Harold Alexander, who ordered it (Wikipedia). In 1964: Paul VI at the abbey itself (vatican.va lists "Paul VI: Visit to Montecassino, 24 October 1964: Consecration of the Abbey Church of the Monastery of Montecassino"). His homily says "Qui" (here) and "Parlano queste mura" (these walls speak), so he spoke on the site. Pacis nuntius is dated "Datum Romae, apud Sanctum Petrum", a formula, so it does not by itself prove where he stood; the travel page and the homily do.
- What happened, in order:
  1. Jan-May 1944: the battle of Monte Cassino; the abbey lay on the Gustav Line (Wikipedia).
  2. 15 Feb 1944: the abbey "was nearly destroyed in a series of heavy, American-led air raids" (Wikipedia). Notes (C-S6) add 230 civilians killed and no Germans in the abbey when it was bombed.
  3. Pius XII, 21 Mar 1947 (C-S4 par.31, from notes; not re-read today): "Practically nothing else survived from the destruction except the sacred crypt in which the relics of the holy Patriarch are preciously kept."
  4. Early 1950s: President Einaudi supports the rebuilding (Wikipedia).
  5. 24 Oct 1964: Paul VI consecrates the rebuilt church and, in Pacis nuntius, names Benedict principal patron of all Europe.
- Details: Paul VI in his homily recalls that, through his office under Pius XII, he was a well-informed witness ("bene informati testimoni") of the Holy See's efforts to spare the abbey, and says the plea "was not heard"; he says "Fratelli, lasciateCi piangere" (Brothers, let us weep) [paraphrase of vatican.va homily, Italian, it/homilies/1964/documents/hf_p-vi_hom_19641024_montecassino.html]. He thanks the abbot, the workers and the Italian authorities. He says the peace had "made the walls rise again".
- Words spoken: Paul VI, Italian, one line (11 words, vatican.va, copyright Dicastery for Communication; one short quotation only): "Parlano queste mura. È la pace che le ha fatte risorgere." plain: "These walls speak. It is peace that has made them rise again."
- Inner life: not applicable to Benedict. Paul VI gives the motive in the homily's last lines: so that the ideal of the spiritual unity of Europe may stay sacred, he named Benedict "Patrono e protettore dell'Europa" (paraphrase).
- Testimony: the Latin text of Pacis nuntius, the consecration clause (one quotation, vatican.va, AAS 56 (1964) pp. 965-967): "Montis Casini templum, anno MCMXLIV, bello per orbem terrarum saeviente, eversum, sed invida pietate deinde refectum, Deo in honorem Beatae Mariae Virginis et Sancti Benedicti hodie rursus consecramus." (copied exactly; "invida" is as the page reads, a likely misprint for another word). plain: "We today consecrate again, to God, in honor of the Blessed Virgin Mary and Saint Benedict, the church of Monte Cassino, which was overthrown in 1944 while war raged over the world, and later rebuilt." Rights: facts-only plus this one quotation.
- What changed: The abbey Benedict wept over is rebuilt, and he becomes patron of Europe on the day it is reopened.
- Not in the sources: a named eyewitness of the 1944 bombing (not found; the outline "Need" is open); the number of aircraft or bombs (not read); the crowd size on 24 Oct 1964 (a search summary said about twenty cardinals and several hundred bishops, from Vatican II; that is not a fetched source, so do not use it); how Paul VI travelled; weather; the relics' exact place in 1964; the "invida" word.
- Sources: Wikipedia "Monte Cassino" (CC BY-SA, read); Paul VI, *Pacis nuntius*, 24 Oct 1964, Latin (vatican.va/content/paul-vi/la/apost_letters/documents/hf_p-vi_apl_19641024_pacis-nuntius.html); Paul VI, homily at Montecassino, 24 Oct 1964, Italian (vatican.va/content/paul-vi/it/homilies/1964/documents/hf_p-vi_hom_19641024_montecassino.html); vatican.va travel page (vatican.va/content/paul-vi/en/travels/documents/montecassino.html); C-S4 from notes-church-miracles.

## FN-1 — How the Rule spread
- Source: CE, "The Benedictine Order", newadvent.org/cathen/02443a.htm (public-domain, 1907). I read it today.
- CE says Benedict did not, strictly, found an order; "we have no evidence that he ever contemplated the spread of his Rule" beyond his own houses. The Maurus-to-Gaul story (543) "cannot be seriously regarded as historical". About 580 the Lombards sacked Monte Cassino; the monks went to the Lateran under Pelagius II and stayed over 140 years. Augustine's monks founded Canterbury soon after 597. By the end of the 8th century the Rule had displaced older codes in France; the Council of Aachen (817) enforced it in the empire. By the 9th century it was the only monastic form in Western Europe except Scotland, Wales and Ireland.
- Today's numbers: not found. CE gives none.

## FN-2 — The "1220 canonization" claim
- Not confirmed. Wikipedia's infobox gives "Canonized 1220, Rome, by Pope Honorius III" with no source in the box. The CE article "St. Benedict of Nursia" (newadvent.org/cathen/02467b.htm) has no match for "1220" or "canoniz". The Roman Martyrology was not reached in this session.
- Advice: do not print 1220. Say Benedict's cult is old: Gregory writes that his cave still works miracles [W-S1 p.100]. The outline's feast notes (21 March in early martyrologies, 11 July since 1969) come from notes-church-miracles, not re-read today.

## FN-3 — John Paul II, *Egregiae virtutis*
- Source: vatican.va, Latin, hf_jp-ii_apl_31121980_egregiae-virtutis.html (la/apost_letters/1980/documents). Read today. Italian version also read; AAS 73 (1981) pp. 258-262 per the footnote in *Slavorum apostoli*.
- Date: 31 December 1980, "Datum Romae, apud Sanctum Petrum", third year of his pontificate. The letter makes Saints Cyril and Methodius, not Benedict, co-patrons of all Europe: "Sanctos Cyrillum et Methodium totius Europae apud Deum caelestes Compatronos constituimus ac declaramus". plain: "We name and declare Saints Cyril and Methodius heavenly co-patrons of all Europe before God." It says that year the Church marks 1,500 years since Benedict's birth, and that Paul VI gave him as patron in 1964. So the outline's "co-patron 1980" must read: the other two are made co-patrons with him.
