The Church remembers Saint Benedict of Nursia as a man through whom God worked many wonders, in his life and in the cave where he first lived. Here we gather those wonders as the oldest witness told them. Nearly all of them come from one book, the second book of the Dialogues by Pope Saint Gregory the Great. He wrote it in 593 or 594, about fifty years after Saint Benedict died. He had the stories from four of Saint Benedict’s disciples, and he names other witnesses story by story. Saint Gregory says that Saint Benedict worked wonders in two ways, sometimes by his prayers and sometimes by the power God gave him.

The Church has honored Saint Benedict as a saint since early times. Saint Gregory already wrote that Saint Benedict worked miracles in his cave, and early lists of saints keep his death day on 21 March. Each source line gives Saint Gregory’s chapter. It also gives the chapter in Luck’s 1880 printing of the 1608 English translation, which has 48 chapters.

During Saint Benedict’s life

A broken sieve is made whole at Enfide

Saint Benedict’s nurse had followed him to Enfide, now called Affile. She borrowed a sieve from the neighbors to clean wheat, and it broke in two. Saint Benedict took the two pieces away and prayed over them with tears. He rose and found that God had made the sieve whole. There was no sign of the break. The people hung it over the church door, so that all who came after would know how God’s grace had worked in him from the start. Saint Gregory says it hung there until the Lombard troubles of his own day.

Status: told by Saint Gregory the Great in his Dialogues (593–94), from Saint Benedict’s disciples.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 1 (ch. 3 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

A priest brings Easter dinner to Saint Benedict’s cave

Saint Benedict was living hidden in a cave at Subiaco. Some distance away, a priest had a good meal ready for Easter Sunday. The Lord appeared to him and said: “You have prepared a good meal for yourself, while my servant in such a place is hungry.” The priest took the food and searched the hills until he found the cave. Saint Benedict had been away from people so long that he did not know it was Easter. The two of them prayed together and gave thanks to God, and then they ate.

Status: told by Saint Gregory the Great in his Dialogues (593–94).

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 1 (ch. 6 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

A poisoned glass breaks near Subiaco

The monks of a nearby monastery asked Saint Benedict to be their abbot after theirs died. He agreed after a long refusal, and he made them keep to the rule of monastic life. Some of the monks decided to poison his wine. He made the sign of the cross over the glass, and it broke in pieces as if a stone had hit it. Saint Benedict told the monks to find another father who suited their ways, and he went back to the wilderness.

Status: told by Saint Gregory the Great in his Dialogues (593–94). The cup appears on the Saint Benedict Medal.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 3 (ch. 9 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

A restless monk is made steady at prayer

A monk in one of Saint Benedict’s monasteries near Subiaco could not stay at prayer. When the others knelt, he went out and let his mind wander. His abbot sent him to Saint Benedict, who scolded him. The monk soon fell back into his old habit. Saint Benedict then came to the house himself. At the hour of prayer he saw a little black boy pulling the monk out by the skirt of his robe. Saint Gregory calls the boy the old enemy. That is his name for the devil. The abbot of that house and Saint Maurus could not see the boy. Saint Benedict prayed that they might. Two days later Saint Maurus saw the boy, but the abbot did not. On another day Saint Benedict found the monk standing idle outside the chapel and struck him with a small rod. From that day the monk stayed at prayer like the others. Saint Gregory says the old enemy was so frightened that he never dared to tempt that monk’s mind again, as if the blow had struck him and not the monk.

Status: told by Saint Gregory the Great in his Dialogues (593–94).

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 4 (ch. 11 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

Water flows from a mountain rock above Subiaco

Three of Saint Benedict’s monasteries stood high on a mountain, and the monks were tired of carrying water up from the lake. One night Saint Benedict climbed up with the boy Saint Placidus, prayed, and laid three stones on the spot. The next day he told the monks to dig there, because almighty God could bring water out of the top of that mountain. They dug, and God brought water out of the rock. Saint Gregory says the spring still flowed in his own day.

Status: told by Saint Gregory the Great in his Dialogues (593–94).

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 5 (ch. 12 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

A Goth’s billhook rises from the lake at Subiaco

A Goth had joined the monks, and he was sent to clear briers by the lake with a billhook. The iron head slipped off the handle into the water and sank out of reach. The Goth told Saint Maurus, and Saint Maurus told Saint Benedict. Saint Benedict put the handle into the water, and the iron rose from the bottom and went back on it. He said to the Goth: “Here is your billhook again. Work on, and be sad no more.”

Status: told by Saint Gregory the Great in his Dialogues (593–94).

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 6 (ch. 13 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

Saint Maurus runs on water for Saint Placidus

The boy Saint Placidus fell into the lake while drawing water, and the current carried him out as far as an arrow flies. Saint Benedict knew it at once in his cell and called for Saint Maurus. Saint Maurus asked his blessing and ran out over the water. He took Saint Placidus by the hair and ran back to land. Saint Benedict credited Saint Maurus’s obedience, and Saint Maurus credited Saint Benedict’s command. Saint Placidus settled it by saying he had seen the abbot’s garment over his head.

Status: told by Saint Gregory the Great in his Dialogues (593–94).

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 7 (ch. 14 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

A crow carries off a poisoned loaf near Subiaco

A priest named Florentius envied Saint Benedict and sent him a poisoned loaf as a gift. Saint Benedict knew what was inside it. At dinner he threw the loaf to the crow that came every day to take bread from his hand. He said: “In the name of Jesus Christ our Lord, take this loaf and leave it where no one can find it.” The crow hopped around the loaf and cried out, but at last it carried the loaf away and came back three hours later for its usual bread.

Status: told by Saint Gregory the Great in his Dialogues (593–94). Saint Gregory’s bird is a crow, and the Saint Benedict Medal shows a raven. This is a different attack from the poisoned glass.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 8 (ch. 15 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

The priest Florentius dies when his room falls

After the loaf failed, Florentius sent seven naked women to dance in the monastery yard before the young monks. Saint Benedict left that place with a few monks. Florentius was glad to hear it. Then his room fell in and killed him, while the rest of the house stood unharmed. Saint Maurus sent word of this to Saint Benedict. Saint Benedict grieved for the priest, and he gave Saint Maurus a penance for being glad.

Status: told by Saint Gregory the Great in his Dialogues (593–94).

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 8 (chs. 16–17 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

The devil calls Saint Benedict blessed, then cursed

On the mountain at Cassino, Saint Benedict smashed the idol of Apollo, knocked down the altar, and built two chapels there. Saint Gregory says the devil then appeared to Saint Benedict in plain sight, but the monks only heard a voice. It called, “Blessed Benedict.” Saint Benedict gave no answer, and the voice changed: “Cursed Benedict, not blessed! What do you want with me? Why do you hunt me down?”

Status: told by Saint Gregory the Great in his Dialogues (593–94).

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 8 (ch. 18 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

Saint Benedict blesses a stone the monks cannot move

The monks were building the cells of the abbey, and they could not move a stone. They believed the devil was sitting on it. They sent for Saint Benedict, and he prayed and blessed the stone. Then the monks carried it away as if it had no weight.

Status: told by Saint Gregory the Great in his Dialogues (593–94).

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 9 (ch. 19 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

The kitchen at Monte Cassino seems to burn

At Monte Cassino Saint Benedict had the monks dig in the ground, and they found an idol of brass. For a little while it lay in the kitchen. Flames seemed to come out of it and spread through the whole kitchen. The monks threw water on the flames and made such a noise that Saint Benedict came to see. He saw no fire. He bowed his head in prayer and told them to bless their eyes, and then they saw that the kitchen was unharmed.

Status: told by Saint Gregory the Great in his Dialogues (593–94).

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 10 (ch. 20 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

A monk killed by a wall lives again

Saint Gregory says the devil threw down a new wall at Monte Cassino, and it killed a young monk, the son of a courtier. The stones had broken his limbs and his bones, and the monks carried him to Saint Benedict in a sack. Saint Benedict had him laid in his cell on the spot where he used to pray. Then he shut the door and prayed harder than he usually did. In that same hour Saint Benedict made the young monk sound again, and he sent him back to work.

Status: told by Saint Gregory the Great in his Dialogues (593–94). Saint Gregory tells of a wall, not a tower.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 11 (ch. 21 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

Saint Benedict knows monks ate outside the monastery

Some monks ate at the house of a religious woman, which the rule of the abbey did not allow. When they came back, they said they had eaten nowhere. Saint Benedict asked: “Why do you tell an untruth? Did you not go into such a woman’s house? Did you not eat such food and drink so many cups?” He told them where they had eaten, what they had eaten, and how many cups they had drunk.

Status: told by Saint Gregory the Great in his Dialogues (593–94).

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 12 (ch. 22 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

Saint Benedict knows a visitor ate on the road

A devout layman went every year to the abbey to ask Saint Benedict’s prayers and to see his brother, the monk Valentinian. Saint Gregory names Valentinian earlier as one of his four sources. The layman always fasted on the road until he reached Saint Benedict. One year a companion on the road urged him to eat. He refused twice, but the third time he gave in. At the abbey Saint Benedict asked him how it happened that the devil failed twice through his companion and then won the third time.

Status: told by Saint Gregory the Great in his Dialogues (593–94).

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 13 (ch. 23 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

Saint Benedict sees through a guard in royal robes

The Gothic king Totila heard that Saint Benedict had the spirit of prophecy. He decided to test him and sent his guardsman Riggo to the abbey in the king’s shoes and royal robes, with three attendants. Saint Benedict sat a little way off. When Riggo came near enough to hear, Saint Benedict said: “Take it off, my son, take off those clothes. What you are wearing is not yours.” Riggo and his attendants fell to the ground. After that they did not dare to come near him, and they went back to the king.

Status: told by Saint Gregory the Great in his Dialogues (593–94). Gardner dates the visit to about 542, and the Catholic Encyclopedia to 543.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 14 (ch. 24 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911; “St. Benedict of Nursia,” Catholic Encyclopedia, 1907, New Advent.

Saint Benedict tells King Totila his future

Then Totila came himself. He saw Saint Benedict sitting some way off and did not dare come near, so he fell to the ground. Saint Benedict told him two or three times to rise. Then Saint Benedict came and lifted him up with his own hands. He told the king: “You do much evil every day, and you have done many great sins. Now at last, stop living in sin. You will enter the city of Rome and cross the sea. You will reign nine years, and in the tenth you will leave this mortal life.” Saint Gregory says Totila was less cruel from then on. He adds that Totila went to Rome, sailed to Sicily, and lost his kingdom and his life in the tenth year of his reign.

Status: a prophecy told by Saint Gregory the Great in his Dialogues (593–94), who tells how it came true.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 15 (ch. 25 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

Saint Benedict says Rome will fall by itself

The bishop of Canosa was a friend of Saint Benedict. Talking with him about Totila, the bishop said that Totila would destroy Rome and leave it empty. Saint Benedict answered: “Rome will not be wiped out by foreigners. It will be so shaken by storms, lightning, whirlwinds and earthquakes that it will fall to ruin by itself.” Saint Gregory heard this from Honoratus, and Honoratus had it from monks who were there. Saint Gregory says he could see it coming true before his eyes in Rome.

Status: a prophecy told by Saint Gregory the Great in his Dialogues (593–94), who had it from Abbot Honoratus. The 1608 English calls the town Camisina, and Gardner says it is Canosa.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 15, Gardner ed., 1911.

Saint Benedict frees a possessed clergyman of Aquinum

A clergyman of Aquinum was possessed by an evil spirit. His bishop sent him to many shrines of the martyrs, but God’s holy martyrs did not heal him. Saint Gregory says this was so that the world would know what great grace was in God’s servant Saint Benedict. At last the clergyman was brought to Saint Benedict. Saint Benedict prayed to Jesus Christ our Lord for help, and at once he cast the old enemy out of the man. Saint Benedict told him not to eat meat and not to be ordained. After many years the man was ordained. The devil came back and tormented him until he died.

Status: told by Saint Gregory the Great in his Dialogues (593–94).

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 16 (ch. 26 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

Saint Benedict foretells the sack of Monte Cassino

A nobleman named Theoprobus was a close friend of Saint Benedict. One day he found Saint Benedict weeping bitterly in his cell and asked why. Saint Benedict answered at once. “By the judgment of almighty God, this whole abbey that I built has been handed over to the pagans, to be plundered and torn down. So has everything I prepared for my brothers. I could hardly win from God that the lives of the monks living in it then should be spared.” Saint Gregory says that years later the Lombards came at night while the monks slept. They took everything, but they could not hold one man.

Status: a prophecy told by Saint Gregory the Great in his Dialogues (593–94), who tells how it came true. Gardner dates the sack to 589. Wikipedia gives about 570 and also 580.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 17 (ch. 27 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911; “Monte Cassino,” Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Cassino.

A hidden flask of wine holds a snake

A lay brother named Exhilaratus was sent to the abbey with two wooden flasks of wine for Saint Benedict. On the way he hid one in a bush for himself and gave the other to Saint Benedict. As the man left, Saint Benedict warned him: “Take care, my son, that you do not drink from the flask you hid in the bush. First tip it over carefully, and you will find what is inside.” Exhilaratus went back and tipped the flask, and a snake leapt out.

Status: told by Saint Gregory the Great in his Dialogues (593–94).

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 18 (ch. 28 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

Saint Benedict knows a monk hid handkerchiefs

Saint Benedict often sent monks to preach to some nuns near the abbey. One monk finished his talk, and the nuns asked him to take some small handkerchiefs. He accepted them and hid them in his bosom for his own use. When he came back, Saint Benedict rebuked him: “How did it happen, brother, that sin has entered your bosom?” The monk had forgotten the handkerchiefs. Saint Benedict said: “Was I not present when you took the handkerchiefs from the nuns and put them in your bosom?” The monk fell at his feet and threw the handkerchiefs away.

Status: told by Saint Gregory the Great in his Dialogues (593–94).

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 19 (ch. 29 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

Saint Benedict knows a candle-bearer’s proud thought

One evening a monk held the candle for Saint Benedict at supper. The monk was the son of a great man, and a proud thought came into his mind. He said nothing, but Saint Benedict knew it. Saint Benedict told him: “Sign your heart, brother. What are you saying? Sign your heart.”

Status: told by Saint Gregory the Great in his Dialogues (593–94).

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 20 (ch. 30 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

Two hundred bushels of meal appear in a famine

A famine struck Campania, and the abbey was down to five loaves. Saint Benedict told the monks: “It is true that there is some want today, but tomorrow you will have plenty.” The next morning two hundred bushels of meal lay in sacks at his cell door. Saint Gregory says almighty God sent them, and that even in his own day no one knew by whose hands. When the monks saw it, they gave thanks to God.

Status: told by Saint Gregory the Great in his Dialogues (593–94).

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 21 (ch. 31 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

Saint Benedict plans a monastery in dreams at Terracina

A good man asked Saint Benedict to build a monastery on his land near Terracina. Saint Benedict sent an abbot, a prior, and some monks. He promised to come on a set day and show them where each building should stand. The night before, he appeared to the abbot and the prior in their sleep and showed them every place. They doubted the vision and waited for him. When he did not come, they went to him and said he had broken his promise. Saint Benedict answered: “Did I not appear to each of you in your sleep?” Then he told them to build by the plan they had seen.

Status: told by Saint Gregory the Great in his Dialogues (593–94).

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 22 (ch. 32 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

Two dead nuns are freed from a Communion ban

Two nuns near the abbey could not control their tongues, so Saint Benedict sent word that he would bar them from Communion unless they changed. They did not change, and they died and were buried in the church. Later a nurse saw them rise from their graves and leave the church whenever the deacon sent away those who did not receive Communion. When Saint Benedict heard of it, he gave an offering with his own hands. He said: “Go, and have this offered to the Lord for them, and they will no longer be barred from Communion.” After it was offered, no one saw them leave the church again.

Status: told by Saint Gregory the Great in his Dialogues (593–94).

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 23 (ch. 33 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

A buried boy is cast out of his grave

A young monk went to his parents’ house without asking Saint Benedict’s blessing, and he died that same day. He was buried, but the next day his body was found thrown out of the grave. He was buried again, and again the body was thrown out. The people who had buried him ran to Saint Benedict and fell at his feet in tears. With his own hands he gave them Holy Communion, the Body of the Lord. He said: “Go, lay this body of the Lord on his breast with great reverence, and bury him.” After that the body lay quietly in the grave.

Status: told by Saint Gregory the Great in his Dialogues (593–94).

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 24 (ch. 34 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

A restless monk meets a dragon at the gate

A monk kept asking to leave the abbey. Saint Benedict grew tired of his pleading and angrily told him to go. As soon as the monk was out of the gate, he met a dragon with its mouth open. He cried: “Help, help! This dragon will eat me up.” The monks who ran out saw no dragon, but they found him shaking and brought him back. He promised never to leave again.

Status: told by Saint Gregory the Great in his Dialogues (593–94).

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 25 (ch. 35 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

A boy with leprosy is healed by Saint Benedict

Saint Gregory heard this story from an honorable man named Anthony. Anthony said that a servant boy of his father’s had leprosy so badly that his hair fell out and his body swelled. Anthony’s father sent the boy to Saint Benedict, and Saint Benedict quickly restored him to health.

Status: told by Saint Gregory the Great in his Dialogues (593–94), who heard it from Anthony himself.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 26 (ch. 36 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

Thirteen shillings are found for a debt of twelve

An honest man owed twelve shillings and asked Saint Benedict for help. Saint Benedict did not have so much money. He told the man to come back in two days and spent that time in prayer. On the third day thirteen shillings were found on the chest where the abbey kept its grain. Saint Benedict had all thirteen given to the man. He told him to pay back the twelve and keep one for his own expenses. Saint Gregory says a disciple named Peregrinus used to tell this story.

Status: told by Saint Gregory the Great in his Dialogues (593–94), as his disciple Peregrinus used to tell it. The 1608 English says shillings, and the Latin says solidi.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 27 (ch. 37 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

Saint Benedict heals a poisoned man by touch

An enemy hated a man so much that he poisoned his drink. The poison did not kill him. It turned his skin many colors, as if he had leprosy. As soon as Saint Benedict touched him, the colors left his body and he was healthy again.

Status: told by Saint Gregory the Great in his Dialogues (593–94), from Saint Benedict’s disciples.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 27 (ch. 38 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

A glass of oil survives, and a barrel overflows

In a famine only a little oil was left in the abbey, and Saint Benedict ordered it given to a subdeacon named Agapitus. The monk who kept the cellar did not obey, so Saint Benedict had the glass of oil thrown out of the window. It fell on rough stones but did not break, and none of the oil was spilled. Saint Benedict had it given to Agapitus whole. Then he knelt in prayer with the other monks. As he prayed, God filled an empty barrel in the room with oil until it ran over the floor. Saint Gregory says Saint Benedict had shown the monks the power of almighty God.

Status: told by Saint Gregory the Great in his Dialogues (593–94).

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, chs. 28–29 (ch. 39 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

Saint Benedict meets the devil dressed as a doctor

Saint Benedict was going to pray at the chapel of Saint John on the mountain top when he met the devil. He was dressed as a doctor and rode a mule, and he carried a horn and a mortar. He said he was going to the monks “to give them a dose.” Saint Benedict went on to pray. The devil found an old monk drawing water, entered him, and threw him to the ground. Saint Benedict came back from his prayers and freed the monk with a light blow of his hand. Saint Gregory adds that God’s servants do miracles sometimes by prayer and sometimes by power.

Status: told by Saint Gregory the Great in his Dialogues (593–94).

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 30 (ch. 40 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

A prisoner of the Goth Zalla is set free

In the time of King Totila a Goth named Zalla persecuted Catholic monks and priests. He tortured a poor countryman to make him say where his money was. The man said he had left all he owned with Saint Benedict. Zalla tied the man’s arms with cords and drove him ahead of his horse to the abbey. They found Saint Benedict sitting at the gate, reading. Zalla shouted at him to hand over the man’s wealth. Saint Benedict looked up and saw the two men, and then he looked at the cords. They fell from the man’s arms faster than anyone could have untied them. Zalla fell to the ground and asked for Saint Benedict’s prayers. Saint Benedict did not rise from his reading. He had Zalla taken in and fed, and he told him to stop his cruelty. Saint Gregory says Saint Benedict did this by his God-given power and not by prayer.

Status: told by Saint Gregory the Great in his Dialogues (593–94). The 1608 English calls him Galla.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 31 (ch. 41 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

Saint Benedict raises a dead child

A countryman laid his dead son at the abbey gate and went to find Saint Benedict. He met Saint Benedict coming back from the fields and cried: “Give me my son, give me my son!” Saint Benedict stopped and said: “Did I take your son from you?” The father answered: “He is dead. Come for the sake of Christ Jesus, and bring him back to life.” Then Saint Benedict said: “Miracles like this belong to the blessed apostles and not to us.” But the father would not leave. So Saint Benedict went to the gate, knelt, and lay on the child’s body. Then he rose and lifted his hands to heaven. “Lord, do not look at my sins, but at the faith of this man who asks for his son’s life. Give back to this body the soul you have taken away.” He had hardly finished when the soul returned, and the child’s body trembled. Saint Benedict took him by the hand and gave him to his father alive and well.

Status: told by Saint Gregory the Great in his Dialogues (593–94), who says Saint Benedict won it from God by prayer.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 32 (ch. 42 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

Saint Scholastica prays down a storm

One evening Saint Benedict’s sister Saint Scholastica asked him to stay the night, but he would not spend a night away from his abbey. She laid her head on her folded hands and prayed to almighty God under a clear sky. When she lifted her head, thunder and heavy rain broke out, and Saint Benedict could not leave. She told him: “I asked you to stay and you would not listen to me, so I asked our good Lord, and he has agreed to my request.” Saint Gregory writes that a woman’s prayer had worked this miracle by the power of almighty God, and that she did more because she loved more.

Status: told by Saint Gregory the Great in his Dialogues (593–94). Tradition gives 10 February 543 for her death.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 33 (ch. 43 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

Saint Scholastica’s soul rises as a dove

Three days after that visit Saint Benedict was standing in his cell. He looked up and saw his sister’s soul rise to heaven in the form of a dove. He thanked God and told the monks, and he had her body brought to the grave he had prepared for himself.

Status: a vision told by Saint Gregory the Great in his Dialogues (593–94).

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 34 (ch. 44 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

Saint Benedict sees the whole world in one ray

Saint Benedict rose before the night prayers and prayed at the window of his room in a tower at Monte Cassino. He later told how a light drove the darkness away and the whole world was gathered before his eyes, as if under one ray of the sun. In the light he saw angels carry the soul of Saint Germanus, bishop of Capua, to heaven in a globe of fire. His guest Servandus was a deacon and the abbot of a monastery in Campania. He came when Saint Benedict called and saw only a small remnant of the light. A messenger sent to Capua that night found that Saint Germanus had died at that very hour.

Status: told by Saint Gregory the Great in his Dialogues (593–94), from Saint Benedict’s own account. Later theologians asked whether in that light he saw God himself.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 35 (ch. 45 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911; “St. Benedict of Nursia,” Catholic Encyclopedia, 1907, New Advent.

Saint Benedict foretells his death and dies in prayer

In the year of his death Saint Benedict told his monks the day he would die. He told those near him to keep it secret and gave those far away a sign. He had his grave opened six days before he died, and then a fever took him. On the sixth day the monks carried him into the chapel, and he received Holy Communion, the Body and Blood of Christ. His disciples held him up, and he died standing with his hands raised to heaven in prayer.

Status: told by Saint Gregory the Great in his Dialogues (593–94). The tradition of the Church gives 21 March as the day. Sources give the year as 543, about 544, or 547.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 37 (ch. 47 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

Two monks see a lit road to heaven

That same day two monks saw the same vision. One was in his own cell and the other was far away. A road ran from Saint Benedict’s cell to the east and up to heaven, hung with rich cloth and lit by lamps. At the top stood a man in solemn dress, and he asked them if they knew who went that way. They said no. He told them: “This is the way by which Benedict, the beloved servant of God, has gone up to heaven.”

Status: a vision told by Saint Gregory the Great in his Dialogues (593–94).

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 37 (ch. 47 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

After Saint Benedict’s death

A woman is healed in Saint Benedict’s cave

Saint Gregory writes that in his own day Saint Benedict still worked miracles in the cave where he first lived. They happened when the faith of those who prayed asked for them. He tells of a woman who had lost her mind. Day and night she had wandered through the hills and woods without aim, yet she did not miss the right way. She came to the cave, slept there one night, and left in her right mind. Saint Gregory says that this happened not long before he wrote, and that she stayed well to the end of her life.

Status: told by Saint Gregory the Great in his Dialogues (593–94), who says it happened shortly before he wrote.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, chs. 37–38 (chs. 47–48 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

Miracles recorded at Fleury abbey in France

In the 800s the Fleury chronicler Adrevald first told how an abbot named Mommolus carried Saint Benedict’s holy relics from Monte Cassino to the French abbey of Fleury. He said this happened after the Lombards had ruined Monte Cassino. The monks of Monte Cassino hold that the relics never left. Over three centuries, five monks of Fleury wrote a book of miracles called Miracula S. Benedicti. Andrew of Fleury wrote his part about 1043. We do not retell their accounts here.

Status: told by the monks of Fleury from the 800s to the 1000s. The monks of Monte Cassino hold that the relics never left.

Source: “Fleury Abbey,” Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fleury_Abbey; “St. Benedict of Nursia,” Catholic Encyclopedia, 1907, New Advent.

The cross protects Metten abbey in Bavaria, 1647

In 1647 women on trial for witchcraft at Natternberg in Bavaria said they had no power over the nearby abbey of Metten because of the cross. The monks searched and found crosses painted on their walls, with letters around them. They had forgotten what the letters meant. A manuscript of 1415 showed Saint Benedict holding a scroll and a staff topped by a cross. The words were written out in full on the staff and the scroll. The Saint Benedict Medal carries these letters, and Pope Benedict XIV approved it in 1741 and 1742.

Status: told in the Catholic Encyclopedia (1912) and by Dom Prosper Guéranger (1880) from the records of Metten. The prayer on the medal comes from the Middle Ages.

Source: Michael Ott, “Medal of Saint Benedict,” Catholic Encyclopedia, 1912, New Advent; Prosper Guéranger, The Medal or Cross of St. Benedict, 1880, Internet Archive.
