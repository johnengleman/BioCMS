One Easter Sunday a priest who lived a good way from Subiaco had prepared a fine meal for the feast. Then the Lord appeared to him and spoke. “You have prepared a good meal for yourself, while my servant in such a place is hungry.”1

The priest got up and took his food with him. On that same Easter day he went searching through the steep hills, the low valleys, and the hollow pits, until at last he found a cave. A young man had lived in it alone for three years. The two of them prayed together. Then they sat down, gave thanks to God, and talked for a long time about the life of heaven.2

At last the priest said, “Get up, brother, and let us eat, because today is the feast of Easter.”3

The young man had lived apart from people for so long that he had lost count of the days. He did not know it was Easter. But he answered as if the visit itself were the feast. “It is Easter for me, and a great feast, because God has shown me this much kindness, to enjoy your company today.”4

The priest told him again that this was the day of the Lord’s Resurrection, and that he must not fast on such a day. He said that he himself had been sent so that the two of them could eat together. So they said grace and ate. When they had talked a while longer, the priest went home to his church.5

The young man in the cave was Saint Benedict. He had gone into that cave because he wanted to please God alone, and for three years only one friend had known where he was. Yet when a stranger found him there with an Easter dinner, he called the visit itself his Easter. The whole of his life was like that day. People kept finding him, and each time his heart had to open a little wider to take them in. In the end he made a home where others could seek God beside him. For that home he wrote a short rule for the monks’ daily life. He called it “this little rule for beginners”.6 Pope Saint Gregory the Great praised it for its good judgment. By the 800s it was the rule of nearly every monastery in western Europe. In 1964 Pope Saint Paul VI named Saint Benedict the patron of all Europe.7

But first he had to leave Rome.

A Student Leaves Rome to Seek God

His name, Benedict, means “blessed”. Saint Gregory begins his account of Saint Benedict’s life with that name: “There was a man of venerable life, blessed by grace as well as by name.”8

Saint Benedict was born about the year 480 in the province of Nursia, in Italy. His family was of good standing, and they sent him to Rome to study. Saint Gregory says that even as a boy he had an old man’s heart. He did not give himself to any pleasure. The world and all its flowers were his to enjoy, yet he already thought of them as dried up. He left Rome about the year 500.9

In Rome he stood at the doorway of that world, with one foot already set on the threshold. Then he made the choice that shaped the rest of his life.10

“He saw that many people who studied in Rome ended up in loose and bad lives. So he stepped back before he went too deep into that world, in case he fell into a terrible pit. He gave up his books, his father’s house and his money. He wanted only to please God, so he sought out a holy way of life. He left with a learned ignorance and an unlearned wisdom.”

— Saint Gregory the Great, Dialogues, Book II

That wish to please God alone was the heart of Saint Benedict’s whole life. He later put the same longing into the rule he wrote for his monks, in the voice of a father who calls his son home. “Listen, my son, to the master’s teaching, and turn the ear of your heart,” the Rule begins. “Gladly welcome the advice of a loving father and carry it out, so that you return by the work of obedience to the One you left through the laziness of disobedience.” For Saint Benedict, leaving Rome was the first step on that road home. Further on, one short line of the Rule says what the road asks of anyone who takes it: “Put nothing before the love of Christ.”11

He did not leave Rome alone. His nurse loved him dearly, and she would not leave him. She was the only one who followed him. The two of them stopped at Enfide, a mountain village that is now called Affile. Some good men lived there at the church of Saint Peter out of charity, and Saint Benedict and his nurse stayed with them.12

One day the nurse borrowed a sieve from some neighbor women to clean wheat. It was left carelessly on the table, and by accident it broke in two. When she came back and found it broken, she began to weep bitterly, because the sieve was not hers.13

Saint Benedict saw her crying, and her sorrow became his own. He took the two pieces away with him and gave himself to prayer, with tears. When he rose from prayer, the sieve lay beside him whole. God had mended it so well that no one could see where it had broken. He comforted his nurse gently and gave it back to her.14

Soon the whole village knew. The people hung the sieve at the door of the church. They wanted people in their own day and in years to come to know how fully God’s grace had worked in him from the start. It hung there for many years, until the Lombards invaded Italy in Saint Gregory’s own time.15

But Saint Benedict had not left Rome to be admired in a village. He would rather suffer the hardships of the world than be praised. He would rather be worn out with work for God than be lifted up by the passing compliments of this life. So he slipped away from his nurse in secret. She had followed him when no one else did, and now she was left behind.16

Alone now, he went on toward a wild place called Subiaco.

Saint Benedict Lives Alone in a Cave

Subiaco lies almost forty miles from Rome. A spring there pours out cold and clear water. It gathers first in a broad lake and then runs on as a river. On the road Saint Benedict met a monk named Saint Romanus, who asked him where he was going. When Saint Romanus heard what the young man wanted, he kept the secret and helped him. He gave him the robe of a monk, and he served him as well as he could.17

Saint Benedict shut himself away in a very narrow cave under a high rock. Today that cave is a shrine called the Sagro Speco, high on the mountainside above the town. For three years he lived there, and no one knew it except Saint Romanus. His friend lived in a monastery nearby, under the abbot who led it. He stole hours from his duties, and he saved bread from his own meals to carry to the cave. No path led from the place where Saint Romanus lived to the cave, because the rock hung out over it. So he let the loaf down from the top on a long rope, with a little bell tied to the rope. At the sound of the bell Saint Benedict knew that his bread was there, and he came out to take it.18

One day the devil, the old enemy of mankind, saw the loaf coming down. He threw a stone and smashed the bell, because he envied the love of the one man and the meal of the other. Saint Romanus did not give up. He went on serving his friend in every way he could.19

This was the life Saint Benedict had wanted: God, one faithful friend, and a long rope. But God meant to give Saint Romanus rest from his labor. He also meant to show the young man’s life to the world, like a candle set on a candlestick to give light to the Church of God. That was when the priest came with his Easter dinner.20

Shepherds found him next. They saw him through the bushes, dressed in animal skins, and at first they thought he was a wild animal. When they came to know him as a servant of God, many of them turned from a rough life to a life of grace. His name spread through the countryside. People began to come to the cave with food for his body, and they went home with food for their souls.21

The next trial came from inside him. One day when he was alone, a small blackbird began to fly around his face. It came so close that he could have caught it in his hand. He made the sign of the cross, and the bird flew off. At once a temptation of the flesh came over him, harder than any he had ever known. He remembered a woman he had once seen. The memory burned in him so strongly that he almost decided to leave the wilderness.22

Then God’s grace helped him, and he came to himself. Thick briers and nettles grew close by. He threw off his clothes and flung himself into the middle of them, and he rolled there until his whole body was torn. By the wounds of his body he healed the wounds of his soul. He told his disciples later that from that day on, he never felt that temptation again.23

After that, more and more people left the world to put themselves under his teaching. Saint Gregory says that he was like a field cleared of thorns, which now bore a richer harvest. Soon a whole monastery came to ask him for something he did not want to give.24

Monks Try to Poison Their New Abbot

Not far from the cave stood a monastery whose abbot had died. The whole community came to Saint Benedict together and begged him with many prayers to become their father. He put them off for a long time. Their ways and his would never agree, he told them. But they would not stop asking, and at last he gave in and went with them.25

As their abbot, he kept the life of that house in good order. No one was allowed to turn aside from the road of a monk’s life, to the right or to the left. The monks began to rage. They blamed themselves for ever having asked for him, because their crooked ways could not bear his straight ones. They did not want to change how they lived, and it was hard for them to think new thoughts with old minds. Some of them made a plan to get rid of him. They agreed to put poison in his wine.26

In that house it was the custom to offer the abbot his glass at the table so that he could bless it. The monks brought him the poisoned glass. Saint Benedict stretched out his hand and made the sign of the cross. The glass was still held some way off, but it broke in pieces at the sign, as if the cross had been a stone thrown against it.27

He understood at once. The glass had held the drink of death, and it could not bear the sign of life. He rose from his place with a mild face and a quiet mind. He called the monks together and spoke to them.28

“May almighty God have mercy on you and forgive you. Why have you treated me like this? Did I not tell you beforehand that our ways of life could never go together? Go, and find another father who suits your own ways, because I will not stay with you any longer.”

— Saint Benedict to the monks who poisoned his wine, in Saint Gregory’s Dialogues

It was his first try at being a father to monks, and it had ended in a shattered glass. He had found that he could not hold men to a way of life they did not want. Saint Gregory explains why he left. If he had held those men against their will, he might have lost his own devotion and turned the eyes of his soul away from God’s light. Worn out each day with correcting them, he would have cared less for his own soul. He would have lost himself and still not found them. Years later his Rule would say that an abbot who has done all a shepherd can for a restless and disobedient flock is blameless before the Lord.29

So he went back to the wilderness he loved so much. Saint Gregory says that there he “lived with himself”. The son in the Gospel who wasted his father’s money had wandered far from himself, and he had to come back to himself before he could go home. Saint Benedict did not wander. He kept watch over his own soul and held his thoughts in its inner room. He lived alone with himself, in the sight of his Creator.30

Saint Benedict Builds Twelve Monasteries at Subiaco

Saint Benedict was not left alone for long. As he grew in goodness and became known for his miracles, more and more men came to the wilderness to serve almighty God with him. So he built twelve monasteries in the valley. He placed twelve monks in each and set an abbot over them. A few he kept with him, the ones he thought would learn best by living at his side.31

Noble families from Rome began to come too, and they gave him their sons to raise for God. A Roman nobleman gave him his son Saint Maurus, who grew in good ways and became Saint Benedict’s helper. A senator brought his son Saint Placidus, who was still a little boy.32

Life on those rocks was hard. Three of the monasteries stood high on a mountain, and every day the monks had to climb down to the lake for water and carry it back up. The slope was so steep that they were afraid each time they went down. At last the monks of all three houses came to Saint Benedict together. They told him how hard it was, and they asked him to move them somewhere else.33

He would not let them move. He comforted the monks with gentle words and sent them back. That night he climbed the mountain with no one but the boy Saint Placidus, and he stayed on the rock a long time in prayer. When he had finished, he laid three stones on the spot as a mark and went home without a word to anyone.34

The monks came to him again the next day. He said to them, “Go to the rock, and where you find three stones laid one on another, dig a little. Almighty God is able to bring water out of the top of that mountain and spare you this hard work.” They went and found the rock wet, as if it were sweating drops of water. They dug a hollow, and it filled at once. The water flowed so freely that it was still running in Saint Gregory’s day.35

One day young Saint Placidus went down to the lake to draw water. He was the same boy who had gone up the mountain with Saint Benedict to pray. He let his pail down carelessly and fell in after it. The water carried him out from the shore about as far as an arrow flies.36

Saint Benedict was in his cell, his room in the monastery, yet he knew it at once. He called in haste for Saint Maurus. “Brother Maurus, run as fast as you can. Placidus went to the lake for water. He has fallen in, and the water is carrying him far out.”37

Saint Maurus asked for his blessing and ran. He ran down to the lake and out over the water to the boy, thinking all the while that he was running on land. He caught him by the hair and ran back with him to the shore. Only when he stood on land did he look behind him and see what he had done. He had run on the water. He was amazed and afraid. Saint Gregory writes that nothing like it had been heard of since the apostle Saint Peter.38

Back in the cell, master and disciple began a friendly argument. Saint Benedict said that the obedience of Saint Maurus had done it. Saint Maurus said he had only done what he was told, and that he had not even known what he was doing. The boy settled it. He said that as he was pulled out of the water, he had seen the abbot’s own garment over his head.39

None of the three kept the glory for himself. That was the kind of house Saint Benedict was building. But word of the valley spread, and a priest nearby heard the talk and grew jealous.40

A Jealous Priest Drives Saint Benedict Away

By now the whole region was glowing with the love of Christ, and many people were leaving the world. Florentius, the priest of a church nearby, watched with envy. He wanted the praise that Saint Benedict’s life won, without living such a life himself. He spoke against Saint Benedict and tried to keep people from visiting him. When that failed, he poisoned a loaf of bread and sent it to him as a gift of blessing.41

Saint Benedict thanked him and took the loaf. He knew what was hidden inside it. At mealtime a crow used to come out of the nearby wood to take bread from his hand. That day it came as usual. Saint Benedict threw the poisoned loaf down in front of it and gave it an order. “In the name of Jesus Christ our Lord, take this loaf and leave it where no one can find it.”42

The crow opened its beak, spread its wings, and hopped around the loaf, crying out. It seemed to say that it wanted to obey and could not. Saint Benedict told it again and again: “Take it up and do not be afraid. Throw it where no one will find it.” The crow held back for a long time. At last it took the loaf in its beak and flew away. Three hours later it came back and took its usual bread from his hand.43

Saint Benedict could see that the priest’s heart was set against his life. Even so, he was far more sorry for the priest than for himself.44

Florentius could not kill the master’s body, so he set out to kill his disciples’ souls. He sent seven young women with no clothes on into the yard of the monastery, where the monks could see them. There they joined hands and danced for a long time, to stir the young monks to sin.45

Saint Benedict watched from his cell. He was afraid that his younger monks would fall. He could see that the whole attack was aimed at him alone. So he chose to give way to the priest’s envy. He put every chapel and house he had built under leaders, with brothers to help them. Then he took a few monks with him and left the valley. He had built twelve monasteries there, and he left all of them so that his young monks would be safe.46

Florentius was standing in his room when he heard that Saint Benedict had gone, and he was glad. Then that room fell in and killed him, while the rest of the house stood firm.47

Saint Maurus sent word at once to his master, who was barely ten miles away. “Come back,” he said. “The priest who persecuted you is dead.”48

Saint Benedict did not rejoice. He wept and lamented that his enemy had died in such a way. It grieved him too that one of his own disciples was glad of it. So he gave Saint Maurus a penance to make up for daring to rejoice at an enemy’s death. It was a hard lesson, and it was a father’s lesson.49

He did not go back. With his few monks he went on toward a high mountain above the town of Cassino.

Saint Benedict Founds the Abbey of Monte Cassino

The town of Cassino lies on the side of a high mountain. The mountain takes the town into its wide lap and then climbs for three miles, until its top seems to reach the sky. Saint Benedict came there in about 528 or 529. Saint Gregory says that he had changed his place but not his enemy.50

On the summit the country people still worshipped the god Apollo in a very old temple, with groves around it where they offered sacrifice to demons. Saint Benedict broke the idol, overturned the altar, and burned the groves. In the temple he made a chapel of Saint Martin, and where Apollo’s altar had stood he built a chapel of Saint John. Then he preached to the people of the region day after day, until he had brought them to the faith of Christ.51

Now the devil fought him in the open. The monks heard his voice but saw nothing. “Blessed Benedict!” it called. Saint Benedict gave no answer, and the voice changed its cry: “Cursed Benedict, not blessed! What do you want with me? Why do you hunt me down?”52

The real test came while the monks were building. One day they were raising a wall a little higher while Saint Benedict prayed in his cell. The old enemy appeared to him there and mocked him, saying that he was on his way to the monks at their work. Saint Benedict sent word to the brothers at once to be on their guard. The message had barely reached them when the new wall fell. It crushed a little boy who was a monk there, the son of a town councillor. The brothers grieved, and they grieved less for the wall than for their brother.53

Saint Benedict told them to bring the boy to him. The stones had broken his limbs and even his bones, so they had to carry him in a sack. Saint Benedict had him laid in his cell, on the spot where he himself used to pray. Then he sent them all out, shut the door, and prayed harder than he usually did. In that same hour he made the boy sound and as lively as before, and he sent him back to help the monks finish the wall. The hermit who had once shut himself away in a cave now shut his door for the sake of one small monk.54

On that mountain Saint Benedict built the abbey of Monte Cassino, and people climbed to him there as they had climbed to him at Subiaco. One of them was a nobleman named Theoprobus. Saint Benedict’s counsel turned him to God, and Theoprobus became one of his closest friends.55

Once a year Saint Benedict’s sister came to see him. Her name was Saint Scholastica. She had been given to God from her childhood, and she lived in a convent of her own. When she came, he went down with some of his monks to meet her at a house that belonged to the abbey, not far outside the gate.56

Saint Benedict Writes a Rule for His Monks

At some point Saint Benedict wrote down a way of life for his monks. Monks call such a book a rule, and his is short. It divides the monk’s day among prayer, work, and sacred reading, and it warns that “idleness is the enemy of the soul”. Near its end he calls it “this little rule for beginners”. Saint Gregory says that it stands out for its good judgment and is clear in its style. He adds that anyone who wants to know how Saint Benedict lived can find it in the Rule. Saint Benedict could not teach anything he did not live himself.57

The Rule opens with the word “listen”. Saint Benedict had once led monks who would not bear his way, and they had tried to kill him for it. Now he wrote as a father who hoped to be heard. He called the monastery a school, and he made a promise to the beginners who would come to it.58

“We must therefore set up a school of the Lord’s service. In laying this down we hope to order nothing harsh or heavy. If something turns out a little strict, as fairness requires, to correct faults or to protect love, do not run from the way of salvation in fear. It can only start out narrow. As life and faith advance, the heart widens, and one runs the way of God’s commands in the unspeakable sweetness of love.”

— The Rule of St Benedict, Prologue

He knew about narrow starts. His had been in a very narrow cave. The man whose own monks had poisoned his wine now told every abbot to try to be loved more than feared. Guests were to be welcomed as Christ himself, because Christ will say to us: “I was a stranger and you took me in.” The abbot himself was to pour water over their hands, and the whole community was to wash their feet. The sick were to come before everything else, and the brothers were to serve them as they would serve Christ. With dry humor he added that guests “are never lacking in a monastery”. In the Rule he lists many tools for good living, and the last is a word for the discouraged: “And never despair of God’s mercy.”59

Each newcomer was placed in the care of an older monk who was skilled at winning souls. That monk watched him closely to see whether he really sought God. Near the end of the Rule, Saint Benedict came back to the love of Christ. “Let them put absolutely nothing before Christ, who will lead us all together to eternal life.” The young man who had left Rome to please God alone now hoped to reach him with all his brothers.60

His monks saw him live what he wrote. One evening while Saint Benedict ate his supper, a monk stood beside the table holding the candle. He was the son of a town official. As he stood there a proud thought rose in him, and he spoke it silently in his heart. “Who is he, that I wait on him at supper and hold the candle for him? And who am I, that I should do him any such service?”61

Saint Benedict turned to him at once and rebuked him sharply. “Sign your heart, brother. What are you saying? Sign your heart.” He had another monk take the candle from his hands, and he told the first one to stop serving and sit down quietly. Later the brothers asked the monk what had happened. He told them all of it, how pride had swelled in him and what he had said against Saint Benedict in the secret of his heart.62

Then the brothers all saw that nothing could be hidden from Saint Benedict. Even the silent words of a thought reached his ears.63

A Gothic King Comes to Test Saint Benedict

The Goths ruled Italy in Saint Benedict’s lifetime, and in his last years the land was at war. The armies of the emperor Justinian had taken back all of Italy south of the river Po. Then in 541 Totila became king of the Goths, and in the years that followed he won back almost all of it. He heard that the abbot of Monte Cassino had the spirit of prophecy. About the year 542, he set out for the abbey. Some way off he stopped and sent word that he was coming. Saint Benedict sent back word that the king could come when he pleased.64

Totila wanted to test him first. He dressed his guardsman Riggo in the king’s own shoes and royal robes. He gave him three of the men who were always at the king’s side, so that the show would look real. Then he sent him up the mountain in his place.65

Riggo came to the abbey in purple, with a crowd of courtiers around him. Saint Benedict was sitting a little way off. When Riggo came close enough to hear, Saint Benedict called out to him in front of them all. “Take it off, my son, take off those clothes. What you are wearing is not yours.”66

Riggo fell to the ground at once, terrified that he had tried to mock such a man. All his companions fell with him. When they got up, none of them dared to come any nearer. They went back to the king and told him how quickly they had been found out.67

Then Totila came himself. He saw Saint Benedict sitting some way off, and he did not dare to come near. He fell to the ground where he stood. Saint Benedict told him two or three times to get up. Then he went to the king and lifted him from the ground with his own hands. He rebuked him for his deeds, and in a few words he told him what was coming.68

“You do much evil every day, and you have done many great sins. Now at last, stop living in sin. You will enter the city of Rome. You will cross the sea. You will reign nine years, and in the tenth you will leave this mortal life.”

— Saint Benedict to King Totila, in Saint Gregory’s Dialogues

The king was badly frightened. He asked Saint Benedict to pray for him and went away, and from that day he was less cruel than before. In time Totila entered Rome and crossed the sea to Sicily. In the tenth year of his reign he lost his kingdom and his life. Historians place his death in 552.69

Years before, Saint Benedict had run from a village because it praised him. Now a king lay on the ground before him, and he did not run. He lifted the king up, and then he told him the truth.70

Saint Benedict Weeps Over His Abbey’s Future

Theoprobus had come to God through Saint Benedict’s counsel, and the two of them were close friends. One day he came into the cell and found Saint Benedict weeping bitterly. He waited a long time, and the tears did not stop. Saint Benedict often wept in prayer, but this was different. These were tears of grief. At last Theoprobus asked him why he mourned so much, and Saint Benedict answered at once.71

“By the judgment of almighty God, this whole abbey that I built has been handed over to the pagans, to be plundered and torn down. So has everything I prepared for my brothers. I could hardly win from God that the lives of the monks living in it then should be spared.”

— Saint Benedict to Theoprobus, in Saint Gregory’s Dialogues

He had already given up the valley of Subiaco to keep his monks safe. Now God had shown him that this house would be lost too, with everything he had made ready for his brothers. He had pleaded with God for them, and he had barely won their lives.72

Life on the mountain went on. Then his sister came for her yearly visit.

Saint Benedict’s Sister Prays Down a Storm

This is the story of a day when Saint Benedict wanted something and could not have it. Saint Gregory reminds his readers that even Saint Paul asked the Lord three times to take away the thorn in his flesh, and was refused. Saint Scholastica came to the house near the gate, as she did every year. Her brother went down with some of his disciples to meet her, and they spent the whole day praising God and talking of holy things. As darkness fell, they ate supper together. They were still sitting at the table, still talking, and the hour was growing late.73

Then she asked him for one thing. “I beg you, do not leave me tonight,” she said. “Let us talk until morning about the joys of the life of heaven.”74

“What are you saying, sister?” he answered. “I cannot possibly stay outside my cell.” She pressed him, but nothing she said would move him. He would not spend a whole night away from his abbey. Outside, the sky was so clear that not one cloud could be seen.75

Saint Scholastica heard her brother say no. She laced her fingers together and laid her hands on the table. Then she bowed her head onto her hands and prayed to almighty God. As she prayed, she wept, and her tears ran down onto the table. She lifted her head from her hands, and in that very moment lightning flashed and thunder broke. Such a flood of rain poured down that neither Saint Benedict nor the brothers with him could set a foot outside the door.76

In all that lightning, thunder, and pouring rain, Saint Benedict saw that he could not get back to the abbey. Saddened, he began to complain to her. “May God forgive you. What have you done?”77

“I asked you to stay and you would not listen to me. So I asked our good Lord, and he has agreed to my request. Now, if you can leave, go back to your monastery in God’s name, and leave me here alone.”

— Saint Scholastica to Saint Benedict, in Saint Gregory’s Dialogues

He could not leave. So the abbot who would not stay stayed, against his will, and the two of them kept awake the whole night. They took turns telling each other of the life of the spirit until both their hearts were full.78

There is no doubt what Saint Benedict wanted that night, Saint Gregory says. He wanted the sky to stay as clear as it was when he came down. Instead a woman’s prayer worked a miracle against his wish, by the power of almighty God. She had long wanted to see her brother. God is love, and so it was only right that she could do more because she loved more.79

The next day she went back to her convent, and he went back to his abbey. Three days later he was standing in his cell when he lifted his eyes. He saw his sister’s soul leave her body and rise into heaven in the form of a dove. Tradition remembers that day as 10 February.80

He was glad for her great glory, and he thanked almighty God with hymns and songs of praise. Then he told the brothers that she had died, and he sent them to bring her body up to the abbey. They laid her in the grave he had made ready for himself. He had refused her a single night. Now he gave her his own grave.81

Saint Benedict Sees the World in One Ray

Another friend came often to the mountain. Servandus was a deacon and the abbot of a monastery in Campania. Saint Gregory says he was full of heavenly teaching. When he visited, the two men spoke sweet words of life to each other. They could not yet feast on the food of their heavenly homeland, so they tasted it in their longing.82

On one of these visits the two men went to a tower when it was time to rest. A single stair ran from the lower room to the upper one. Saint Benedict slept at the top, and Servandus slept at the foot. In front of the tower stood a larger room, where the disciples of both men lay asleep.83

Saint Benedict was eager to keep watch in prayer by night, and he woke while his brothers were still at rest. Before the hour of the night prayers, he got up and went to the window of his room. There he stood and prayed to almighty God.84

“As he stood there, suddenly, in the dead of night, he looked out and saw a light that drove the darkness away. It shone brighter than daylight. Then something strange and wonderful followed. As he told it himself later, the whole world was gathered together, as if under one ray of the sun, and shown before his eyes.”

— Saint Gregory the Great, Dialogues, Book II

He kept his eyes fixed on that blazing light. In it he saw angels carrying the soul of Saint Germanus, bishop of Capua, up to heaven in a globe of fire.85

He wanted a witness to so great a wonder, so he called Servandus by name, two or three times, in a very loud voice. Servandus was alarmed to hear such a cry from such a man. He hurried up the stair and looked out, and he saw only a small trace of the light. While he stood there amazed, Saint Benedict told him everything in order.86

Then Saint Benedict sent word down to the town of Cassino, to his friend Theoprobus. He asked him to send a man to Capua that same night, to learn how Saint Germanus was. The messenger found the bishop already dead. He asked carefully about the hour. Saint Germanus had died at the very moment that Saint Benedict saw him rise.87

How could one man see the whole world? Saint Gregory answered: “Heaven and earth were not squeezed into a smaller space. The soul of the one who saw them was widened.” Caught up into the light of God, Saint Benedict stood for that moment outside the world, and so he could see it whole. As Saint Gregory put it, “To a soul that sees the Creator, all creation is small.”88

In his Rule he had promised beginners that as life and faith go on, the heart widens. At that window in the dead of night, it happened to him.89

He had begun in a narrow cave, where only one friend knew where he was and a stranger had to search the hills to find him. Now he stood at a high window and called his guest to come up and see.90

The Death of Saint Benedict

Saint Benedict outlived his sister by only a little while. By the Church’s tradition, he died about a month after she did. In the year of his death he made known to his monks the day on which he would die. He told the ones who lived with him to keep it secret. To the ones who lived far away he gave a sign, so that they would know when his soul left his body.91

Six days before he died, he had his grave opened. It was the grave where his sister lay. A fever took him soon after, and its burning heat wore him down. Each day he grew weaker. On the sixth day he had his disciples carry him into the chapel.92

“There he strengthened himself by receiving the body and blood of Christ. His weak body was held up by his disciples’ hands. He stood with his own hands raised to heaven, and while he was praying like this, he died.”

— Saint Gregory the Great, Dialogues, Book II

The man who had once hidden in a cave to be alone with God died standing among his brothers. The tradition of the Church gives 21 March as the day, and the year was about 543 or 547.93

That same day two monks saw the same vision. One was in his own cell, and the other was far away. They saw a road that ran straight from Saint Benedict’s cell toward the east, all the way up to heaven. It was spread with rich cloths and lit by lamps without number. At the top of it stood a man in solemn dress, and he asked them who had passed that way. They said they did not know. “This is the way by which Benedict, the beloved servant of God, has gone up to heaven,” he told them.94

The monks who were with him knew that he had died because they had seen it happen. The monks far away knew it by the sign he had given them. They buried him in the chapel of Saint John the Baptist, which he had built where the altar of Apollo once stood.95

In Saint Gregory’s own day, Saint Benedict still worked miracles in the cave at Subiaco where he first lived, for those who prayed there with faith. Not long before Saint Gregory wrote, a woman who had lost her mind wandered day and night through the hills and woods. Yet one day she did not miss the right way: she came to the cave, slept there that night, and woke in her right mind.96

The abbey on the mountain still stood, and the loss Saint Benedict had wept over had not yet come.

The Abbey Falls and the Monks Reach Rome

Then the Lombards came, a people who had moved into Italy after Saint Benedict’s death. A band of them struck in the 580s, some forty years after he died. They entered the abbey at night while the monks were asleep. They plundered everything, but they could not take a single man. God gave them the house and its goods but spared the monks’ lives, as he had promised his servant. Theoprobus had heard Saint Benedict’s words in the cell, and now Saint Gregory’s own generation saw them proved true. Saint Gregory compared it to the ship of Saint Paul, which lost all its cargo while every soul on board came safe to land.97

The monks went to Rome. Pope Pelagius II gave them a monastery beside the Lateran Basilica, and Saint Benedict’s monks lived there for more than a hundred and forty years. For many years that house was led by Valentinian, one of the disciples who had known him. So some of the men who had lived with Saint Benedict were now living in Rome.98

Saint Gregory the Great had been a monk in Rome before he became pope in 590. In 593 the monks of his household asked him to write about the wonders God had worked through the fathers of Italy. Saint Gregory wrote about this to a bishop in Sicily. “The brothers who live close to me want me to write, briefly, about the miracles of the Fathers that we have heard were done in Italy. For this I need your kind help.” So he wrote the book called the Dialogues, and he gave its whole second book to one man. He gathered the story from four of Saint Benedict’s disciples, among them Valentinian and the abbot of that first monastery at Subiaco.99

Through that book the Church has known Saint Benedict ever since.

How Saint Benedict’s Rule Spread Through the Church

Saint Benedict had written for his own houses. He did not found what we now call an order, a family of monasteries living under one rule. There is no sign that he ever planned for his Rule to go further. For nearly a hundred years after his death, there is no sign that the monasteries of Gaul used it.100

Then monks began to carry it further. Soon after 597, Saint Augustine and his monks founded a monastery at Canterbury in England. By the end of the 700s the monasteries of France had given up their older rules for Saint Benedict’s. In 817 the abbots of the empire met at Aachen and passed laws to make the life of every monastery the same, as additions to Saint Benedict’s Rule. By the 800s it was the only rule for monks in western Europe, except in Scotland, Wales, and Ireland. The little rule for beginners had become the rule of a continent.101

The monks on the mountain had a harder road. A monk named Petronax rebuilt the abbey about 718. In 883 or 884 Saracen raiders sacked it again. Meanwhile the monks of Fleury Abbey in France told a story of their own. From the 800s on they said that one of their abbots had carried home the remains of Saint Benedict, his holy relics, from the ruins after the Lombard attack. The monks of Monte Cassino hold that the relics never left the mountain. In the French wars of religion, Protestant soldiers melted down the case that held the relics at Fleury.102

People remembered the poisoned glass and the poisoned loaf, and they began to ask Saint Benedict’s help against poison. The Saint Benedict Medal shows a cup and a raven beside him. Its letters stand for an old prayer against the devil. In 1647 the monks of Metten Abbey in Bavaria found the words of that prayer written out in full in a manuscript of 1415. Pope Benedict XIV approved the medal in 1741 and 1742. In 1880 the jubilee design that most people know was first struck. Monte Cassino alone had the right to strike it.103

The Church has honored Saint Benedict since very early times. The early lists of saints kept his death day on 21 March, a date that always fell in Lent. Since 1969 the Catholic Church has kept his feast on 11 July, and the Orthodox Church honors him on 14 March.104

Through all of this the monks kept rebuilding the abbey that Saint Benedict had wept over. In 1944 it fell once more.

Monte Cassino Is Destroyed and Rebuilt

In the winter of 1944 the war in Italy reached Monte Cassino. The abbey stood on the German line of defense, and the Allied armies fought for the mountain from January to May. On 15 February 1944 American planes led heavy air raids that left the abbey in ruins. About 230 civilians were killed. There were no German soldiers in the abbey when the bombs fell.105

Three years later Pope Pius XII wrote a letter to the whole Church about Saint Benedict. In it he said that almost nothing had survived except the sacred crypt, the chapel below the church where the holy relics of Saint Benedict were kept. After the bombing, archaeologists found traces of the two chapels Saint Benedict had built on the summit, the chapel of Saint Martin and the chapel of Saint John. They matched Saint Gregory’s account.106

President Einaudi of Italy supported the rebuilding in the early 1950s. On 24 October 1964 Pope Saint Paul VI came up the mountain to consecrate the new church, which means to set it apart for God in a solemn service. He thanked the abbot, the workers, and the Italian authorities. On the same day he named Saint Benedict the principal patron of all Europe. Cardinals, bishops, heads of religious orders, and well-known laymen from many parts of Europe had asked for it. Pope Pius XII had hoped that God would bless the work of uniting Europe through Saint Benedict’s prayers, and Pope Saint John XXIII had wished the same. In his letter naming Saint Benedict patron, Pope Saint Paul VI called him a messenger of peace, a builder of unity, and a teacher of civilization. He wrote that Saint Benedict and his monks had brought Christian civilization to Europe by “the cross, the book, and the plow”. He also recalled that Pope Pius XII had called Saint Benedict the father of Europe. In 1980 Pope Saint John Paul II named Saint Cyril and Saint Methodius patrons of Europe beside him.107

On the mountain where Saint Benedict had once wept for his abbey, Pope Saint Paul VI spoke of the war. He had served Pope Pius XII in those years. He had seen the Holy See, the Pope’s own government in Rome, plead for the abbey to be spared. The plea was not heard. Now he spoke of the walls. “These walls speak,” he said. “It is peace that has made them rise again.” Then he said, “Brothers, let us weep with emotion and gratitude.”108

The monks of Monte Cassino say that Saint Benedict and Saint Scholastica rest together under the high altar. Saint Gregory wrote that “their souls were always one in God, and their bodies were not parted in death.”109

A Note on the Sources

Nearly everything in this life comes from Book II of the Dialogues of Pope Saint Gregory the Great, written in 593 from the memories of four of Saint Benedict’s disciples. Constantine followed Saint Benedict as abbot of Monte Cassino, and Simplicius was the third abbot. Valentinian led the monastery by the Lateran for many years. Honoratus was abbot of Subiaco when Saint Gregory wrote. Saint Gregory says plainly that he could not learn everything about Saint Benedict, only the few things he tells. He wrote the book as a conversation with his deacon Peter, and he often stops the story to explain what a deed meant. Those explanations are his own, written some fifty years after Saint Benedict’s death. Gardner, who edited the English text in 1911, called Book II “the earliest and most authoritative account of St. Benedict that we possess”. Dudden, writing in 1905, thought that Saint Gregory wrote to build up and comfort his readers in the hard years of the Lombard wars. Saint Gregory gives no dates at all, so every year given for Saint Benedict’s own life comes from later scholars. Gardner, for example, says that Saint Benedict left Rome probably a few years before 500, and that King Totila’s visit probably took place in 542. Saint Gregory wrote about Totila and about the fall of the abbey after both had happened. Some scholars give the year of Saint Benedict’s death as 543, and others give 547. A few scholars have doubted whether Saint Gregory wrote the Dialogues, but most accept that he did. Saint Gregory does not name the monastery whose monks tried to poison Saint Benedict, and later tradition calls it Vicovaro. Some later additions are left out: the names of Saint Benedict’s parents, the idea that they were a noble Roman family, and the idea that Saint Scholastica was his twin. So is the later story that Saint Maurus carried the Rule to France in Saint Benedict’s lifetime, which the Catholic Encyclopedia does not accept. Saint Gregory calls the bird that carried off the loaf a crow; the medal shows a raven. He gives no date for Saint Scholastica’s death either, and 10 February is the Church’s tradition. Quotations from Saint Gregory follow the English translation of 1608, put into modern words. A few lines are our own translation of Saint Gregory’s Latin, because the 1608 English gives them only in summary or less closely. They are Saint Scholastica’s request and her brother’s answer, Saint Maurus’s message, and Saint Gregory’s words about the widened soul. In one place we follow the Latin where the 1608 English reads it the other way: Saint Gregory says that Saint Benedict’s tears before Theoprobus were tears of grief, not the tears of prayer he was used to. The quotations from the Rule are our own translation of the Latin. The notes give Saint Gregory’s chapters in the usual numbering of thirty-eight chapters, with the pages of Gardner’s 1911 edition.

How This Life Was Researched

This life draws on twenty-nine sources in four languages: English, Latin, Italian, and French. Among them are three editions of Saint Gregory’s Dialogues in the English of 1608, his Latin text, and the Latin text of the Rule with an English translation of 1906. Other sources include the Catholic Encyclopedia and Dudden’s two-volume study of Saint Gregory from 1905. There are also five papal documents from 1947 to 2008, read in Latin, Italian, and English. For this version the writer went back to the saved texts of Saint Gregory’s Latin and the 1608 English and checked the turning points of the story against both. The research marked the nurse who followed him out of Rome as a detail that many English accounts leave out. Saint Scholastica’s own words at the table were found in the Latin for this version. So was Saint Gregory’s use of the Rule’s own word for the widened heart.
