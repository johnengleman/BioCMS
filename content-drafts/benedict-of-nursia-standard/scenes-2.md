# Benedict of Nursia: scene cards, group 2 (SC-06 to SC-10)

Source note for all cards. Text read this session: Gregory the Great, *Dialogues* II, 1608 English (P.W.), ed. Edmund G. Gardner (London 1911), archive.org id cu31924029222076, OCR. Rights: public-domain. Called W-S1 in the outline. Page numbers are the 1911 book's. The Latin original was not reached (see outline notes); every quotation below is the 1608 English, copied exactly (only OCR damage such as "niy" for "my" is repaired), each with a `plain:` line. Gardner's Latin glosses are given where he quotes them. "Bennet" = Benedict.

## SC-06 — Maurus runs on the lake
- Status: full
- When and where: Subiaco, during the years of the twelve monasteries. No date in Gregory. The lake is the one by which the monks fetched water (ch.5 shows the mountain monasteries going "down unto the lake for water"). Benedict is "in his cell".
- Who was there: Benedict; Maurus, his disciple, son of Evitius; Placidus, "a boy of tender years", son of Tertullus the senator [W-S1 p.60-61]; Placidus is "the holy man's monk".
- What happened, in order:
  1. Benedict had built twelve abbeys, twelve monks in each, and kept a few with himself. Roman nobles gave him their sons to raise: Evitius gave Maurus; Tertullus the senator brought Placidus [W-S1 p.60-61]. Maurus grew in virtue and "began to be his master's co-adjutor"; Placidus was still a small boy [W-S1 p.61].
  2. Placidus went to take water at the lake, put his pail down carelessly, and fell in after it. The water carried him from the shore "so far as one may shoot an arrow" [W-S1 p.64].
  3. Benedict, in his cell, "by and by knew this" and called for Maurus at once [W-S1 p.64].
  4. Maurus asked his father's blessing, ran out at his command, ran "upon the water" to the boy, took him by the hair, and ran back [W-S1 p.64].
  5. On land, Maurus looked behind him and realised he had run on water. He was amazed and afraid at what he had done [W-S1 p.64].
  6. Back with Benedict, Benedict gave the credit to Maurus's obedience. Maurus said it was done only on Benedict's command, and that he did nothing, not knowing what he did [W-S1 p.64].
  7. Placidus settled the "friendly contention": when drawn from the water he saw the abbot's garment on his head, and said the abbot had rescued him [W-S1 p.64-65].
- Details: A pail. A lake. The distance is "an arrow's shot". Gregory himself calls it "A strange thing, and since the time of Peter the Apostle never heard of!" [W-S1 p.64]. Gardner's note: tradition says Maurus became the Benedictine apostle of France and died in 584, and Placidus died a martyr in Sicily in 541 (tradition; Gardner's note, W-S1n p.263-264). Outline says Maurus-in-Gaul is unreliable, so leave it out.
- Words spoken:
  - Benedict to Maurus. "Brother Maurus, run as fast as you can, for Placidus, that went to the lake to fetch water, is fallen in, and is carried a good way off." plain: "Brother Maurus, run as fast as you can. Placidus went to the lake for water. He has fallen in, and the water is carrying him far out." [W-S1 p.64]
- Inner life: none given for Maurus or Benedict beyond the humility each shows in giving the credit away.
- Testimony: Gregory, *Dialogues* II ch.7, W-S1 p.64, public-domain.
  > "On a certain day, as venerable Bennet was in his cell, the foresaid young Placidus, the holy man's monk, went out to take up water at the lake, and putting down his pail carelessly, fell in himself after it, whom the water forthwith carried away from the land so far as one may shoot an arrow. The man of God, being in his cell, by and by knew this, and called in haste for Maurus ... Maurus, craving his father's blessing, and departing in all haste at his commandment, ran to that place upon the water ... and taking fast hold of him by the hair of his head, in all haste he returned back again ... the venerable man did not attribute this to his own merits, but to the obedience of Maurus: but Maurus on the contrary, said that it was done only upon his commandment"

  plain: One day Placidus, the young monk, went to the lake to draw water. He set his pail down carelessly and fell in after it. The water carried him from the shore about as far as an arrow flies. Benedict, in his cell, knew it at once and called for Maurus. Maurus asked his father's blessing, hurried off on Benedict's order, and ran out over the water. He took the boy by the hair and ran back. Benedict did not credit his own merit. He credited Maurus's obedience. Maurus said it was done only on Benedict's command.
- What changed: Benedict is now father of a school of noble boys; the scene shows obedience and humility, each man giving the credit to the other.
- Not in the sources: the weather, the time of day, Placidus's age in years, what Placidus felt or said besides the garment, any words of Maurus (only reported), Benedict walking to the shore. Gregory does not say the lake's name (Gardner's notes locate it at Subiaco). The "a miracle of faith" framing is Gregory's, not ours.
- Sources: W-S1 p.60-61, 64-65; W-S1n p.263 (Maurus and Placidus traditions).

## SC-07 — The crow and the poisoned loaf
- Status: full
- When and where: Subiaco and the abbey nearby, after the twelve monasteries were famous. No date. Benedict's meal, "dinner time"; the abbey yard; later a place "scarce ten miles off".
- Who was there: Benedict; Florentius, "Priest of a church hard by", grandfather of Gregory's own subdeacon Florentius; a crow from the next wood; "seven naked young women"; Benedict's monks; Maurus; a few monks who left with Benedict.
- What happened, in order:
  1. Florentius, burning with envy at Benedict's fame, backbit him and tried to keep people from visiting him. When that failed, he poisoned a loaf and sent it "as it were for an holy present" [W-S1 p.65].
  2. Benedict thanked him and took the loaf, "not ignorant of that which was hidden within" [W-S1 p.65].
  3. At dinner a crow that came daily from the wood to take bread from his hand arrived. Benedict threw it the loaf and gave a charge [W-S1 p.65-66].
  4. The crow hopped, opened its mouth, lifted its wings, and cried, as though willing but unable. Benedict told it again, "Take it up without fear". At last the crow took the loaf and flew off. After three hours it came back for its usual bread [W-S1 p.66].
  5. Benedict was "far more sorry for him than grieved for himself". Florentius, unable to kill the master, sent seven naked young women into the abbey yard before the monks' eyes to dance a long time hand in hand, to arouse the young monks [W-S1 p.66].
  6. Benedict saw it from his cell. Fearing for his younger monks and seeing the attack was aimed at himself, "he gave place to envy": he left, with a few monks, after appointing governors for the abbeys and oratories [W-S1 p.66].
  7. Florentius, in his chamber, heard of the departure and was glad. Then that chamber alone fell, and killed him; the rest of the house stood [W-S1 p.66-67].
  8. Maurus sent word to Benedict, who was "scarce ten miles off", asking him to return because the persecuting priest was dead [W-S1 p.67].
  9. Benedict was "passing sorrowful", grieving that his enemy died so and that one of his own monks rejoiced. He gave that monk a penance [W-S1 p.67].
- Details: A tame crow that came daily; the poisoned loaf sent as a holy present; the dance in the abbey yard "before their eyes"; the chamber alone falling "the whole house besides continuing safe and sound"; three hours; ten miles. Pick plain wording for the women: Gregory says seven young women, naked, who danced hand in hand in the yard. Tell it briefly. The Gregory-and-Peter exchange lists Moses, Elisha, Peter (on the water), Elijah (crow), and David (lament for an enemy) as parallels [W-S1 p.67]; this is Gregory's commentary, not part of the scene.
- Words spoken:
  - Benedict to the crow. "In the name of Jesus Christ our Lord, take up that loaf, and leave it in some such place where no man may find it." plain: "In the name of Jesus Christ our Lord, take this loaf and leave it where no one can find it." [W-S1 p.65-66]
  - Benedict again. "Take it up without fear, and throw it where no man may find it." plain: "Take it up and do not be afraid. Throw it where no one will find it." [W-S1 p.66]
- Inner life: Gregory's report that Benedict was "far more sorry for him than grieved for himself" and "passing sorrowful, and lamented much" at Florentius's death [W-S1 p.66-67]. Gregory's gloss: "upon humility, gave place to the other's malice" [W-S1 p.66].
- Testimony: Gregory, *Dialogues* II ch.8, W-S1 p.66-67, public-domain.
  > "But the venerable father, perceiving the Priest so wickedly bent against his life, was far more sorry for him than grieved for himself ... the holy man beholding out of his cell, and fearing the danger which thereby might ensue to his younger monks, and considering that all this was done only for the persecuting of himself, he gave place to envy ... which thing when Bennet heard, he was passing sorrowful, and lamented much: both because his enemy died in such sort, and also for that one of his monks rejoiced thereat; and therefore he gave him penance, for that, sending such news, he presumed to rejoice at his enemy's death."

  plain: The old father saw how wickedly the priest wanted him dead. He felt more sorry for the priest than angry for himself. He watched from his cell, feared for his younger monks, and saw that all this was aimed at him alone. So he gave way to the envy. When Benedict heard the priest was dead, he was deeply sad and wept. He grieved that his enemy had died that way. He grieved too that one of his own monks was glad. He gave that monk a penance for daring to be glad at an enemy's death.
- What changed: Benedict gives up his own work at Subiaco rather than put his young monks in danger. He leaves for another place (Cassino, SC-08).
- Not in the sources: the crow's name (none given); the priest's name for the loaf's poison; what the dancing women looked like beyond "naked young women"; any ruse behind the women (Gregory says only that Florentius sent them); the name of Maurus's penance; which monks left; the day of Florentius's death. The place Benedict went is not named in this chapter. Gregory does not say the crow is a "raven" (the outline notes a crow). Gregory gives the ten miles as Benedict's distance when Maurus sent word; he does not say where he was going.
- Sources: W-S1 p.65-67.

## SC-08 — The shrine of Apollo
- Status: full (date inferred, see below)
- When and where: Cassino, on the side of "an high mountain", whose top "seemeth to touch the very heavens", above the town of Cassino. Founding date: Gardner says "in 528 or 529"; Gregory gives no date [W-S1n p.263].
- Who was there: Benedict; his monks (those who left Subiaco with him, "a few monks"); the "foolish and simple country people" who worshipped Apollo; the devil, whom the monks heard but did not see.
- What happened, in order:
  1. Benedict changed his place "but did not for all that change his enemy". The devil now fought him openly [W-S1 p.68].
  2. On the mountain stood an "ancient chapel" where country people "according to the custom of the old gentiles, worshipped the god Apollo". Woods stood around it, where "the mad multitude of infidels" still offered sacrifice [W-S1 p.68].
  3. Benedict "beat in pieces the idol, overthrew the altar, set fire on the woods". In the temple of Apollo he built the oratory of St Martin. Where Apollo's altar stood he made an oratory of St John [W-S1 p.68].
  4. By "continual preaching" he brought the people of the region to the Christian faith [W-S1 p.68].
  5. The devil appeared to him in open sight, complaining of violence. The monks heard the noise but could not see him. Benedict later told them how he looked: "most fell and cruel", with "fiery mouth and flaming eyes", as if to tear him in pieces [W-S1 p.68-69].
  6. The devil called him by name, "Blessed Bennet". Benedict gave no answer. Then the devil turned: "Cursed Bennet, and not blessed". All the monks heard it [W-S1 p.69].
- Details: A mountain slope rising three miles; woods round the shrine; an idol, an altar; two new oratories. Archaeology: the sources in the notes say two chapels were found on the site (C-S6, Wikipedia, Monte Cassino; not re-read here). The monks hear the voice only. The description of the devil's face comes from Benedict's own telling to them ("as the venerable father told them") [W-S1 p.68].
- Words spoken:
  - The devil, heard by all the monks. "Cursed Bennet, and not blessed: what hast thou to do with me? and why dost thou thus persecute me?" plain: "Cursed Benedict, not blessed! What do you want with me? Why do you hunt me down?" [W-S1 p.69]
- Inner life: Gregory's gloss: new battles are to be expected, "against whom willingly did he make war, but, against his will, did he give him occasion of many notable victories" [W-S1 p.69]. Benedict gave the devil no answer [W-S1 p.69].
- Testimony: Gregory, *Dialogues* II ch.8, W-S1 p.68, public-domain.
  > "in this place there was an ancient chapel in which the foolish and simple country people, according to the custom of the old gentiles, worshipped the god Apollo. Round about it likewise upon all sides, there were woods for the service of the devils, in which, even to that very time, the mad multitude of infidels did offer most wicked sacrifice. The man of God coming thither, beat in pieces the idol, overthrew the altar, set fire on the woods, and in the temple of Apollo, he built the oratory of St. Martin, and where the altar of the same Apollo was, he made an oratory of St. John: and by his continual preaching, he brought the people dwelling in those parts to embrace the faith of Christ."

  plain: On this mountain stood an ancient chapel. The simple country people still worshipped the god Apollo there, as the old pagans did. Woods all around it were kept for the devils, and a crowd of unbelievers still made wicked sacrifices in them. Benedict came, smashed the idol, knocked down the altar, and burned the woods. In Apollo's temple he built a chapel of St Martin. Where Apollo's altar had stood he made a chapel of St John. By his steady preaching he brought the people of the region to the Christian faith.
- What changed: Benedict has a home site for his monks; he has also made a new enemy, and the next chapters (stone, idol, wall) begin the building of the abbey.
- Not in the sources: the date; how many monks; what the idol looked like; whether the country people resisted or what they said; the exact day of the burning; crowds or tools. Gregory's word "chapel" and "temple" both appear for the same place. Gregory does not name the mountain "Monte Cassino" as an abbey in this chapter; the abbey is named later. The devil's "voice" is heard by monks; no one else sees him. The "528/529" is Gardner's inference only.
- Sources: W-S1 p.67-69; W-S1n p.263 (Gardner's 528 or 529); C-S6 (chapels found, not re-read).

## SC-09 — The king in borrowed shoes
- Status: full
- When and where: At or near Benedict's abbey of Monte Cassino, "in the time of the Goths". Gardner: Totila became king in 541 and the meeting "probably" took place in 542 [W-S1n p.264]. The outline's "543" comes from W-S2/W-S3 (not re-read here). Benedict "sat a little way off".
- Who was there: Totila (Baduila), king of the Goths; Riggo, "a certain man of his guard"; the attendants Vultericus, Rudericus, and Blindinus; Benedict; courtiers. Gardner notes Rudericus and Blindinus ("Vult, Ruderic, and Bleda") are known from history, sent by Totila against Florence in the same year [W-S1n p.264].
- What happened, in order:
  1. Totila heard that Benedict had the spirit of prophecy. On his way to the monastery he stopped some way off and sent word ahead. Benedict answered he might come when he pleased [W-S1 p.73].
  2. Totila, "a man wickedly disposed", decided to test him. He had Riggo wear the king's shoes and royal robes and sent him in his place. He sent three attendants "who especially were always about the king" to act as if Riggo were the king [W-S1 p.73-74].
  3. Riggo arrived at the abbey in purple robes with many courtiers. Benedict sat a little way off [W-S1 p.74].
  4. When Riggo came near enough to hear, Benedict spoke in front of them all. Riggo fell to the ground, afraid. His attendants fell down too. When they rose, they did not dare to come closer. They went back and told the king how quickly they were found out [W-S1 p.74].
  5. Totila then came himself. Seeing Benedict sitting far off, he did not dare come near and fell to the ground. Benedict said twice or thrice "rise up", then came to him and lifted him up with his own hands [W-S1 p.74].
  6. Benedict rebuked him for his wicked deeds and in few words told him what would happen to him [W-S1 p.74-75].
  7. The king was greatly afraid. He asked Benedict to commend him to God in prayer, and left. From then on he was "nothing so cruel as before" [W-S1 p.75].
  8. Gregory adds, writing long after: Totila went to Rome, sailed to Sicily, and "in the tenth year of his reign" lost kingdom and life [W-S1 p.75]. Gardner: Totila took Rome in 546 and 549 and was killed in 552 [W-S1n p.263-264].
- Details: The king's shoes and purple robes; Benedict seated at a distance; the three named attendants. Gardner adds that modern historians draw Totila more favourably than Gregory does; Hodgkin calls him "upon the whole one of the best types of the still future age of chivalry" [W-S1n p.264]. The Rome prophecy, told to the Bishop of Camisina, is a separate exchange (ch.15, p.75), and Gregory says Honoratus heard it from other monks, not from Benedict [W-S1 p.75].
- Words spoken:
  - Benedict to Riggo. "Put off, my good son, put off that apparel, for that which thou hast on, is none of thine." plain: "Take it off, my son, take off those clothes. What you are wearing is not yours." [W-S1 p.74]
  - Benedict to Totila. "Much wickedness do you daily commit, and many great sins have you done: now at length give over your sinful life. Into the city of Rome shall you enter, and over the sea shall you pass: nine years shall you reign, and in the tenth shall you leave this mortal life." plain: "You do much evil every day, and you have done many great sins. Now at last, stop living in sin. You will enter the city of Rome. You will cross the sea. You will reign nine years, and in the tenth you will leave this mortal life." [W-S1 p.74-75]
  - Benedict to the Bishop of Camisina, on Rome. "Rome shall not be utterly destroyed by strangers: but shall be so shaken with tempests, lightnings, whirlwinds, and earthquakes, that it will fall to decay of itself." plain: "Rome will not be wiped out by foreigners. It will be so shaken by storms, lightning, whirlwinds and earthquakes that it will fall to ruin by itself." [W-S1 p.75]
- Inner life: none from Benedict. Gregory's frame: "the man of God had the spirit of prophecy" [W-S1 p.73].
- Testimony: Gregory, *Dialogues* II ch.14, W-S1 p.74, public-domain.
  > "Riggo, furnished with that brave apparel, and accompanied with many courtiers, came unto the Abbey: at which time the man of God sat a little way off, and when Riggo was come so near that he might well understand what the man of God said, then, in the hearing of them all, he spake thus: 'Put off, my good son, put off that apparel, for that which thou hast on, is none of thine.' Riggo, hearing this, fell straightways down to the ground, and was very much afraid, for presuming to go about to mock so worthy a man"

  plain: Riggo, in his fine borrowed clothes and with many courtiers, came to the abbey. Benedict sat a little way off. When Riggo came near enough to hear, Benedict spoke in front of them all: "Take it off, my son, take off those clothes. What you are wearing is not yours." Riggo at once fell to the ground, very afraid that he had tried to mock such a man.
- What changed: The man who ran from the world now speaks to a king as a father. Totila becomes "nothing so cruel as before", in Gregory's words.
- Not in the sources: Totila's face or words beyond asking for prayer; the size of his army; the day or season; Benedict's clothes; any further talk. The text says Benedict spoke to the prostrate king "twice or thrice" before he rose. The prophecies of Totila and Rome were reported fifty years later; Honoratus did not hear the Rome one from Benedict. The date 543 is not in Gregory; Gardner says "probably" 542.
- Sources: W-S1 p.73-75; W-S1n p.263-264.

## SC-10 — Weeping over the abbey
- Status: full
- When and where: Benedict's cell at Monte Cassino. No date in Gregory.
- Who was there: Benedict; Theoprobus, "a certain noble man ... by the good counsel of holy Bennet converted", who was "very intrinsical and familiar with him" [W-S1 p.78]. Gregory also names Theoprobus in ch.35 (outline, SC-12).
- What happened, in order:
  1. Theoprobus came into the cell one day and found Benedict "weeping very bitterly" [W-S1 p.78].
  2. He waited a good while and Benedict did not stop. Gregory adds that Benedict "used not in his prayers to weep, but rather to be sad" [W-S1 p.78].
  3. Theoprobus asked the cause of this great heaviness. Benedict answered at once [W-S1 p.78].
  4. Gregory, years later: the Lombards did attack the abbey. They entered "in the night time, when the monks were asleep" and took everything, but "not one man could they retain there". Gardner dates the destruction 589 [W-S1 p.79; W-S1n p.264].
  5. Gregory compares Benedict to St Paul, whose ship lost all its goods but whose companions all lived [W-S1 p.79].
- Details: Benedict wept for a long time, unusual in him. Gregory gives the reason it was noticed: he was not a man who wept in prayer. The Lombards came at night while the monks slept [W-S1 p.78-79].
- Words spoken:
  - Benedict to Theoprobus. "All this Abbey which I have built, and all such things as I have made ready for my brethren, are by the judgment of almighty God delivered to the gentiles, to be spoiled and overthrown: and scarce could I obtain of God to have their lives spared, that should then live in it." plain: "All this abbey that I built, and everything I prepared for my brothers, God has judged will be handed over to the pagans, to be plundered and torn down. I could hardly win from God that the lives of the monks living in it then should be spared." [W-S1 p.78]
- Inner life: his grief itself, and the plea to God that saved the monks' lives (as Benedict told it). Gregory's remark on his prayer habit: sad, not weeping [W-S1 p.78].
- Testimony: Gregory, *Dialogues* II ch.17, W-S1 p.78-79, public-domain.
  > "This man upon a day, coming into his cell, found him weeping very bitterly. And having expected a good while, and yet not seeing him to make an end (for the man of God used not in his prayers to weep, but rather to be sad), he demanded the cause of that his so great heaviness ... For not long since, in the night time, when the monks were asleep, they entered in, and spoiled all things, but yet not one man could they retain there, and so almighty God fulfilled what he promised to his faithful servant: for though he gave them the house and all the goods, yet did he preserve their lives."

  plain: One day Theoprobus came into his cell and found him weeping bitterly. He waited a good while, and Benedict did not stop. (Benedict did not usually weep in prayer. He was more often sad.) Theoprobus asked why he was so heavy with grief. Gregory adds: Not long ago, at night, while the monks slept, the Lombards came in and plundered everything. But they could not hold one man. So God kept his promise to his faithful servant. He gave them the house and all the goods, but he saved the lives.
- What changed: Benedict accepts that his life's work will fall, and he has already won his monks' lives. Gregory sees the prophecy fulfilled by the Lombards.
- Not in the sources: the date or season of the weeping; the room's details; Theoprobus's reply; whether Benedict told the monks; when "not long since" was (Gardner's 589 is a note, not Gregory). Gregory does not say Benedict prayed on his knees. The word "Lombard" comes from Gregory's later comment, not from Benedict's words. Benedict says only "gentiles".
- Sources: W-S1 p.78-79; W-S1n p.264 (Chapter XVII note: destroyed by the Lombards of Beneventum in 589).
