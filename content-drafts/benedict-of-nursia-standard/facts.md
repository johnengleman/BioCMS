# Facts: Saint Benedict of Nursia (approved by the checker, 2026-10-06)

Use these exactly in `miracles.html`, the teachings essay, and any other text. Notes refer to the numbered notes of `biography.html`. Gregory's chapters use the numbering of thirty-eight chapters; page numbers are Gardner 1911 (the 1608 English).

## Names and spellings
- Saint Benedict of Nursia ("Benedict" means "blessed"). Saint Scholastica (sister). Saint Maurus, Saint Placidus (disciples). Saint Romanus (monk who helped him at Subiaco; his abbot is not named in the text). Saint Gregory the Great (Pope Saint Gregory the Great); his partner in the Dialogues is Peter the deacon (no title).
- Plain, no title (no source calls them saints): Constantine (next abbot of Monte Cassino), Simplicius (third abbot), Valentinian (abbot of the Lateran monastery), Honoratus (abbot of Subiaco when Gregory wrote), Servandus (deacon and abbot in Campania), Theoprobus (nobleman, friend), Florentius (priest), Totila (king of the Goths), Riggo (his guardsman), Pope Pelagius II, Petronax, Mommolus, Exhilaratus.
- Saint Germanus, bishop of Capua (titled). Saint Augustine of Canterbury, Saint Cyril and Saint Methodius, Pope Saint Paul VI, Pope Saint John XXIII, Pope Saint John Paul II (titled). Pope Pius XII and Pope Benedict XIV (no title).
- Places: Nursia; Enfide (now Affile); Subiaco (Sublacum); the Sagro Speco; Cassino and Monte Cassino; Capua; Metten Abbey (Bavaria); Fleury (Saint-Benoît-sur-Loire).
- The bird that took the loaf is a crow in Gregory; the Saint Benedict Medal shows a raven.

## Dates and numbers
- Born about 480 (Gardner p. 51 note). Left Rome "about the year 500" (Gardner: disputed, probably a few years before 500; notes 9).
- Three years in the cave (II.1). Subiaco almost forty miles from Rome (II.1). Twelve monasteries, twelve monks each (II.3, p. 60). Maurus's message sent when Saint Benedict was "barely ten miles" away (II.8). Seven women (II.8). Crow came back after three hours (II.8).
- Monte Cassino founded in about 528 or 529 (Gardner p. 68 note). Mountain rises three miles (II.8).
- Totila became king in 541 (Gardner); visit "about the year 542"; Saint Benedict foretold nine years of reign, death in the tenth; Totila died in 552 (Gardner). Saint Benedict told him: enter Rome, cross the sea, nine years, die in the tenth.
- Saint Scholastica died three days after the storm; tradition 10 February (543 per Gardner p. 94 note). Saint Benedict died about a month later; tradition 21 March; year about 543 or 547. Grave opened six days before his death (II.37).
- Lombard attack on Monte Cassino: 589 (Gardner p. 78 note); about 580 (Alston); "in the 580s" in the text. Monks lived beside the Lateran Basilica more than 140 years (Alston). Dialogues written 593; Gregory pope from 590.
- Petronax rebuilt the abbey about 718; Saracens sacked it in 883 or 884.
- Medal: Metten 1647 (manuscript of 1415); approved by Pope Benedict XIV in 1741 and 1742; jubilee design first struck 1880; Monte Cassino "alone had the right" to strike it (Ott, 1912).
- Feasts: 21 March (early lists); Catholic Church 11 July since 1969; Orthodox 14 March.
- 15 February 1944 bombing of Monte Cassino (American-led raids); about 230 civilians killed; no German soldiers in the abbey (Wikipedia). Pius XII's encyclical Fulgens radiatur: 21 March 1947. Paul VI consecrated the church and named Saint Benedict principal patron of Europe on 24 October 1964 (Pacis nuntius). Saint Cyril and Saint Methodius named patrons beside him on 31 December 1980.
- Counts: 29 sources; 109 notes; three 1608 editions (Gardner 1911, Luck 1880, Little Flowers 1901).

## Approved wording of quotations that other texts may use
- Gregory, opening (note 8, our translation): "There was a man of venerable life, blessed by grace as well as by name."
- Rule: "this little rule for beginners" (73.8; note 6). "Put nothing before the love of Christ." (4.21). "idleness is the enemy of the soul" (48.1). "And never despair of God's mercy." (4.74). "Let them put absolutely nothing before Christ, who will lead us all together to eternal life." (72.11). Guests "are never lacking in a monastery" (53.16).
- The Easter dinner (II.1, note 4): "It is Easter for me, and a great feast, because God has shown me this much kindness, to enjoy your company today." (1608 wording; the Latin is "Scio, quia Pascha est: quia hodie videre te merui.")
- The poison speech (note 28): "May almighty God have mercy on you and forgive you. Why have you treated me like this? Did I not tell you beforehand that our ways of life could never go together? Go, and find another father who suits your own ways, because I will not stay with you any longer."
- The water (note 35): "Go to the rock, and where you find three stones laid one on another, dig a little. Almighty God is able to bring water out of the top of that mountain and spare you this hard work."
- To the crow (notes 42–43): "In the name of Jesus Christ our Lord, take this loaf and leave it where no one can find it." / "Take it up and do not be afraid. Throw it where no one will find it."
- Maurus's message (note 48, our translation): "Come back. The priest who persecuted you is dead."
- The devil: "Blessed Benedict!" / "Cursed Benedict, not blessed! What do you want with me? Why do you hunt me down?"
- Saint Maurus on the water: "Brother Maurus, run as fast as you can. Placidus went to the lake for water. He has fallen in, and the water is carrying him far out." Saint Gregory writes that nothing like it had been heard of since the apostle Saint Peter (a retelling, not a quotation).
- Candle monk (notes 61–62): "Sign your heart, brother. What are you saying? Sign your heart."
- To Riggo: "Take it off, my son, take off those clothes. What you are wearing is not yours." To Totila: "You do much evil every day, and you have done many great sins. Now at last, stop living in sin. You will enter the city of Rome. You will cross the sea. You will reign nine years, and in the tenth you will leave this mortal life."
- To Theoprobus (note 72): "By the judgment of almighty God, this whole abbey that I built has been handed over to the pagans, to be plundered and torn down. So has everything I prepared for my brothers. I could hardly win from God that the lives of the monks living in it then should be spared." The tears: "Saint Benedict often wept in prayer, but this was different. These were tears of grief." (Latin II.17; the 1608 English reads it the other way.)
- Saint Scholastica (notes 74–75, our translation): "I beg you, do not leave me tonight. Let us talk until morning about the joys of the life of heaven." Saint Benedict: "What are you saying, sister? I cannot possibly stay outside my cell." Saint Benedict: "May God forgive you. What have you done?" Saint Scholastica: "I asked you to stay and you would not listen to me. So I asked our good Lord, and he has agreed to my request. Now, if you can leave, go back to your monastery in God's name, and leave me here alone."
- The two souls (II.34, note 109): "their souls were always one in God, and their bodies were not parted in death."
- The world in one ray (II.35, notes 85, 88): "the whole world was gathered together, as if under one ray of the sun, and shown before his eyes." "Heaven and earth were not squeezed into a smaller space. The soul of the one who saw them was widened." "To a soul that sees the Creator, all creation is small." (our translation; Latin "dilatatus", the Rule's "dilatato corde".)
- The death (II.37, note 92): "There he strengthened himself by receiving the body and blood of Christ. His weak body was held up by his disciples' hands. He stood with his own hands raised to heaven, and while he was praying like this, he died." The vision: "This is the way by which Benedict, the beloved servant of God, has gone up to heaven."
- Gregory's letter (note 99): "The brothers who live close to me want me to write, briefly, about the miracles of the Fathers that we have heard were done in Italy. For this I need your kind help."
- Paul VI: Saint Benedict a "messenger of peace, a builder of unity, and a teacher of civilization"; brought Christian civilization by "the cross, the book, and the plow" (Pacis nuntius, our translation); Pius XII called him the father of Europe. Homily, 24 October 1964 (our translation): "These walls speak. It is peace that has made them rise again." "Brothers, let us weep with emotion and gratitude."

## Things the text does not say (do not add)
- The names of Saint Benedict's parents; that the family was noble Roman; that Saint Scholastica was his twin; that Saint Maurus carried the Rule to France in his lifetime; the name of the poisoning monastery (later tradition: Vicovaro); the year of Saint Benedict's canonization.
- That the nurse came from his father's house (the sources give no more than "nutrix").
- That Pope Saint John XXIII or Pope Pius XII named a patron of Europe (only Paul VI did).
