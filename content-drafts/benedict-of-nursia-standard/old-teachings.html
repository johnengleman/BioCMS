Benedict left one writing: his Rule for monks. Gregory the Great, the only early witness to his life, says he “wrote a rule for his monks, both excellent for discretion and also eloquent for the style,” and that anyone who wants to know how Benedict lived can find it there, because he could not teach otherwise than he lived. No source gives the date or place of writing. The themes below are a doorway into it. The English is our own translation from the Latin, checked against Oswald Hunter Blair’s 1907 translation; passages are cited by chapter, because most verse numbers could not be confirmed. The motto Ora et labora (pray and work) is not in the Rule.

Listen with the ear of the heart

The Rule begins with one word, Obsculta: Listen. Benedict speaks as a father to a son, and he describes the monk’s life as a way home: obedience, freely given, undoes the old disobedience that led away from God.

“Listen, my son, to the master’s teaching, and turn the ear of your heart. Gladly welcome the advice of a loving father and carry it out, so that you return by the work of obedience to the One you left through the laziness of disobedience.”

— Benedict, Rule, Prologue (our translation)

These are the opening lines of the Rule, written for men entering his monasteries. Benedict had listened himself when he was young: he left his studies, his father’s house, and its wealth in Rome to seek God at Subiaco.

Nothing before the love of Christ

Everything in the Rule serves one center: Christ, loved above all. Benedict says it twice, once among the “tools of good works” in chapter 4 and again in the last line of chapter 72, where he adds that Christ leads the whole community together to eternal life.

“Let them put absolutely nothing before Christ, who will lead us all together to eternal life.”

— Benedict, Rule, 72:11 (our translation)

The shorter form in chapter 4 is four Latin words, Nihil amori Christi præponere, “Put nothing before the love of Christ” (4:21). Benedict lived it from his youth: Gregory says he left his books and his inheritance, and at the end he died standing in the oratory, having received the Body and Blood of Christ, with his hands raised in prayer.

A school with nothing harsh or heavy

Benedict calls the monastery a school, where people learn to serve God. He hopes to lay down nothing harsh, and he promises that the narrow beginning widens into joy.

“As life and faith advance, the heart widens, and one runs the way of God’s commands in the unspeakable sweetness of love.”

— Benedict, Rule, Prologue (our translation)

Just before, he writes: “We must therefore set up a school of the Lord’s service” (Prologue 45), and “In laying this down we hope to order nothing harsh or heavy.” The man who wrote this had been an abbot whose first monks tried to poison him because they could not bear his discipline and who then founded new communities at Subiaco and at Cassino.

The tools of good works

Chapter 4 lists more than seventy short commands that Benedict calls tools. They begin with love of God and neighbor and run through the works of mercy.

“Refresh the poor. Clothe the naked. Visit the sick. Bury the dead. Help in trouble. Comfort the grieving.”

— Benedict, Rule, chapter 4 (our translation)

The list includes a warning against vanity: “Do not wish to be called holy before you are. First be what can truly be said.” It ends with the tool for the worst days: “And never despair of God’s mercy.” The workshop for all of them is “the monastery enclosure and stability in the community.” Gregory shows Benedict praying with tears over his nurse’s broken sieve and in sorrow over a countryman’s dead son, and refusing credit when Maurus walked on the water.

The ladder of humility

In chapter 7 Benedict takes Jacob’s ladder as a picture of the monk’s life: body and soul are its two sides, and there are twelve steps of humility. The first is to keep the fear of God always in view. The last is love.

“When all the steps are climbed, the monk will come to that love of God which, being perfect, casts out fear. He will keep all things no longer from dread of hell, but from love of Christ, from good habit, and from delight in virtue.”

— Benedict, Rule, chapter 7 (our translation)

On the fourth step, the monk in hard and unjust treatment “quietly embraces patience in his heart, endures, and does not grow weary or leave.” Benedict answered the monks who poisoned him “with a mild countenance and quiet mind,” and mourned the priest who had tried to poison him when he died.

Every guest received as Christ

Hospitality is one of the Rule’s great themes. Guests are received as Christ himself, the poor and pilgrims most of all, the abbot pours water over their hands, and the whole community washes their feet.

“Let all guests who arrive be received as Christ, because he will say: I was a stranger and you took me in.”

— Benedict, Rule, chapter 53 (our translation)

The same chapter gives the poor a special place, “because in them Christ is received more; for the rich, fear itself wins them honour,” and gives the guests a separate kitchen, since guests “are never lacking in a monastery.” Benedict called a priest’s visit to his cave on Easter Day his feast, and he raised a Gothic king who lay on the ground before him.

Tools as altar vessels, and a kind word

In chapter 31 the cellarer, who keeps the monastery’s goods, must be “a father to the whole community.” Ordinary things are to be treated with reverence, and people with gentleness.

“Let him treat all the tools and goods of the monastery as if they were the sacred vessels of the altar.”

— Benedict, Rule, chapter 31 (our translation)

If he has nothing to give, “let him give a kind word,” so that “no one be troubled or saddened in the house of God.” Gregory tells how Benedict brought a Goth’s lost billhook up from the bottom of a lake and handed it back: “work on, and be sad no more.”

Measure in all things

Benedict asks for measure, omnia mensurate, and allows for human weakness, often with dry humor.

“We read that wine is not for monks at all, but since monks of our times cannot be persuaded of that, let us agree at least not to drink to fullness.”

— Benedict, Rule, chapter 40 (our translation)

He sets a hemina of wine a day, thinking of the weakness of the sick, and puts the care of the sick “before all and above all.” Gregory, who called the Rule “excellent for discretion,” shows the same balance in the man: strict about his own rule, yet overruled by his sister’s prayer.

Work, prayer, and sacred reading

The Rule divides the monk’s day among prayer, work, and sacred reading. Work has its own place beside prayer.

“Idleness is the enemy of the soul.”

— Benedict, Rule, chapter 48 (our translation of Otiositas inimica est animæ)

The same chapter says monks are such when they live by the work of their hands, as the fathers and apostles did. The motto Ora et labora is not in the Rule; Terence Kardong, quoted by Subiaco Abbey, traces it to a nineteenth-century book by Abbot Maurus Wolter. In Gregory’s stories Benedict comes in from work in the fields, and sends a monk healed of his injuries straight back to work on the wall.

An abbot loved more than feared

Benedict asks the abbot to lead by serving.

“Let him try to be loved more than feared.”

— Benedict, Rule, chapter 64 (our translation of et studeat plus amari quam timeri)

In the same chapter he tells the abbot that he must serve more than rule, prodesse magis quam præesse. Gregory shows him correcting monks with a question (“Sign your heart, brother”) and giving away the monastery’s last oil in a famine.

The good zeal

Near the end of the Rule, Benedict sets a bitter zeal that leads away from God against a good zeal that leads to God, and describes the good zeal as life together.

“Let them try to outdo each other in honour. Let them bear each other’s weaknesses of body and of character with great patience. Let them race one another in obedience. Let no one follow what he thinks useful for himself, but what is useful for the other.”

— Benedict, Rule, chapter 72 (our translation)

Benedict calls the whole Rule, in its last chapter, “this least little rule for beginners,” hanc minimam inchoationis regulam. He died held up by the hands of his monks.
