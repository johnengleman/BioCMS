This list gathers the wonders told of St. Benedict of Nursia, with their sources and their status. Nearly all of them come from one book: the second book of the Dialogues of Pope Gregory the Great, written in 593 or 594, about fifty years after Benedict’s death, from the accounts of four named disciples (Constantine, Valentinian, Simplicius, and Honoratus) and a few other witnesses whom Gregory names story by story. Benedict was honored as a saint long before the Church had formal procedures for canonization, and no source we read shows any of these accounts being examined or approved in a canonical process. The list makes no claim of scientific proof, and it does not dismiss any account. Chapter numbers follow the Latin division of Gregory’s book into 38 chapters; where it is known, the number in Luck’s 1880 printing of the 1608 English, which divides the book into 48, is given too. The old English calls Benedict “Bennet.”

During Benedict’s life

The broken sieve at Effide

When the young Benedict left Rome, his nurse went with him to Effide (the old English “Enside,” modern Affile). She borrowed a sieve from the neighbors, and it fell and broke in two. Benedict gathered the pieces and “with tears fell to his prayers,” and found the sieve “so whole that the place could not be seen where before it was broken.” The townspeople hung it over the door of their church, where Gregory says it still hung “even to these very troubles of the Lombards.”

Status: recorded by Gregory the Great from Benedict’s disciples. No formal Church approval.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 1 (ch. 3 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

A priest sent to the cave on Easter Day

While Benedict lived hidden in a cave at Subiaco, a priest far away who had prepared a good dinner heard the Lord say to him in a vision: “Thou hast provided good cheer for thyself, and my servant in such a place is afflicted with hunger.” The priest went out and found Benedict in his cave on Easter Day. Benedict had not known it was Easter.

Status: recorded by Gregory. No formal Church approval.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 1 (ch. 6 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

The poisoned glass shatters

The monks of a neighboring monastery, whose abbot had died, persuaded Benedict to rule them, then resented his discipline and mixed poison into his wine. When he made the sign of the cross over the glass, it broke in pieces “as though the sign of the cross had been a stone thrown against it.” Benedict asked God to forgive them, told them to seek another father, and returned to the wilderness. Gregory does not name the monastery; later tradition identifies it with Vicovaro. This is a different attack from the poisoned loaf below.

Status: recorded by Gregory. No formal Church approval. The cup appears on the St. Benedict Medal.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 3 (ch. 9 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

The monk who could not stay at prayer

In one of the Subiaco monasteries, whose father was Pompeianus, a monk could never stay at prayer and kept wandering out. Benedict saw what Gregory describes as a little black boy, a demon, drawing the monk out by the skirt of his habit. No one else could see it until Maurus did, after two days. Benedict struck the monk with a wand, and from then on he stayed at prayer.

Status: recorded by Gregory. No formal Church approval.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 4, Gardner ed., 1911.

Water from the mountain rock

Three of Benedict’s monasteries stood on a mountain without water, and the monks were worn out carrying it. One night Benedict climbed the mountain with the boy Placidus, prayed, and laid three stones on the spot. The next day the monks dug where the stones lay; the rock began “sweating drops of water,” and a spring flowed that Gregory says ran “even to this day.” Peter, in the dialogue, compares it to Moses striking the rock.

Status: recorded by Gregory. No formal Church approval.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 5 (ch. 12 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

The Goth’s billhook rises from the lake

A Goth, poor in spirit, who had joined the community was clearing briers by the lake at Subiaco when the iron head of his billhook slipped off into water “so deep, that there was no hope.” Benedict put the empty handle into the lake, and the iron rose from the bottom. “Behold here is thy bill again,” he said, “work on, and be sad no more.” Peter, in the dialogue, compares it to Elisha’s axe-head. This Goth is not the debtor of the twelve shillings below.

Status: recorded by Gregory. No formal Church approval.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 6 (ch. 13 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

Maurus runs on the water to save Placidus

The boy Placidus fell into the lake, and the water carried him out “so far as one may shoot an arrow.” Benedict, in his cell, knew it at once and sent Maurus, who ran across the surface of the water and pulled Placidus out by the hair. Benedict credited Maurus’s obedience; Maurus credited the abbot’s command. Placidus said he had seen “the Abbot’s garment upon his head” as he was drawn out.

Status: recorded by Gregory. No formal Church approval.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 7 (ch. 14 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

The crow and the poisoned loaf

Florentius, the priest of a nearby church and grandfather of a subdeacon Gregory knew, envied Benedict and sent him a poisoned loaf. Benedict told the crow that came every day from the nearby wood to take bread from his hand: “In the name of Jesus Christ our Lord, take up that loaf, and leave it in some such place where no man may find it.” The crow hopped about and cried, and at last “with much ado” carried the loaf away, returning three hours later for its usual bread. The 1608 English says crow; the St. Benedict Medal shows a raven. Florentius later died when the room where he was collapsed, and Benedict mourned him and gave Maurus a penance for rejoicing at the news.

Status: recorded by Gregory. No formal Church approval. The bird and loaf appear on the St. Benedict Medal.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 8 (chs. 15–17 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

“Blessed Bennet” and “Cursed Bennet”

After Benedict destroyed the shrine of Apollo on the mountain at Cassino and built oratories of St. Martin and St. John the Baptist in its place, Gregory says the devil appeared to him visibly. The monks saw nothing but heard the voice call “Blessed Bennet,” and then, “Cursed Bennet, and not blessed: what hast thou to do with me?”

Status: recorded by Gregory. No formal Church approval.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 8, Gardner ed., 1911.

The stone that would not move

While Monte Cassino was being built, a stone the monks needed could not be moved, because the monks decided the devil was sitting on it. When Benedict blessed it, it could be moved.

Status: recorded by Gregory. No formal Church approval.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 9 (ch. 19 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

The phantom fire in the kitchen

When the monks dug up an idol of brass, the kitchen seemed to burst into flames. The fire was only a phantom.

Status: recorded by Gregory. No formal Church approval.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 10 (ch. 20 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

A young monk crushed by a falling wall

The devil, Gregory says, threw down a new wall at Monte Cassino, and it fell on a young monk, the son of a courtier. His bones were so broken that he was carried to Benedict in a sack. Benedict prayed alone in his cell, and in the same hour the young monk was sound and went back to work on the wall. This is a wall, not a tower; the tower in Gregory’s book is where Benedict later saw the vision of the whole world.

Status: recorded by Gregory. No formal Church approval.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 11 (ch. 21 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

Monks who ate outside the monastery

Some monks ate at the house of a devout woman, against the rule of the house, and denied it on their return. Benedict named the house, the food, and the number of cups: “Why do you tell an untruth?”

Status: recorded by Gregory. No formal Church approval.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 12 (ch. 22 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

Valentinian’s brother eats on the road

The brother of Valentinian, one of Gregory’s four witnesses, was a layman who would not eat on his way to the abbey until he arrived. Once he ate on the road, and Benedict told him so when he came.

Status: recorded by Gregory. No formal Church approval.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 13 (ch. 23 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

Riggo in the king’s clothes

Totila, king of the Goths, tested Benedict’s gift of prophecy by sending his guardsman Riggo in the royal shoes and robes, with three attendants, Vultericus, Rudericus, and Blindinus. From a little way off Benedict called: “Put off, my good son, put off that apparel, for that which thou hast on, is none of thine.” Riggo and his escort fell to the ground and went back to the king. The 1907 Catholic Encyclopedia dates the visit to 543; Gardner (1911) says probably 542.

Status: recorded by Gregory. No formal Church approval.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 14 (ch. 24 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

The prophecy to Totila

When Totila came in person and lay on the ground before him, Benedict raised him and told him: “Into the city of Rome shall you enter, and over the sea shall you pass: nine years shall you reign, and in the tenth shall you leave this mortal life.” Gregory, writing about fifty years later, says Totila entered Rome, crossed to Sicily, and lost kingdom and life in his tenth year, and that he was “nothing so cruel as before.”

Status: a prophecy recorded by Gregory, who tells its fulfilment. No formal Church approval.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 15 (ch. 25 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

Rome will decay of itself

The bishop of Canosa told Benedict that Totila would leave Rome uninhabited. Benedict answered: “Rome shall not be utterly destroyed by strangers: but shall be so shaken with tempests, lightnings, whirlwinds, and earthquakes, that it will fall to decay of itself.” Gregory says he saw this “before our eyes in this very city.” Honoratus, who reported the saying, had it from monks who heard it, not from Benedict himself.

Status: a prophecy recorded by Gregory at second hand. No formal Church approval.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 15, Gardner ed., 1911.

The possessed clergyman of Aquinum

A clergyman of Aquinum, possessed by an evil spirit, had been taken to martyrs’ shrines without being healed, “to the end that the world might know what great grace was in the servant of God, Bennet.” Benedict freed him and forbade him to eat meat or to take higher orders. After many years he took orders, and he died possessed.

Status: recorded by Gregory. No formal Church approval.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 16 (ch. 26 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

The sack of Monte Cassino foretold

Theoprobus, a convert who knew Benedict well, found Benedict weeping bitterly in his cell. Benedict told him that the whole abbey would be “delivered to the gentiles, to be spoiled and overthrown,” and that “scarce could I obtain of God to have their lives spared.” Gregory reports that the Lombards later sacked the abbey by night while the monks slept, and not one monk was taken. Gardner dates the sack to 589; other sources give earlier dates.

Status: a prophecy recorded by Gregory, who tells its fulfilment. No formal Church approval.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 17 (ch. 27 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

The snake in the hidden flagon

Exhilaratus, a lay brother whom Gregory’s listener knew, hid a flagon of wine in a bush. Benedict warned him: “Take heed, my son, that thou drinkest not of that flagon which thou hast hidden in the bush: but first be careful to bow it down, and thou shalt find what is within it.” When he tipped it over, a snake leaped out.

Status: recorded by Gregory. No formal Church approval.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 18 (ch. 28 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

The handkerchiefs

A monk who preached to some nuns accepted handkerchiefs from them and hid them in his habit. Benedict asked him on his return: “How cometh it to pass, brother, that sin is entered into your bosom?”

Status: recorded by Gregory. No formal Church approval.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 19 (ch. 29 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

The proud thought of the candle-bearer

A young monk, the son of a great man, was holding a candle for Benedict when a proud thought rose in him. He said nothing, but Benedict told him: “Sign your heart, brother, for what is it that you say? Sign your heart.”

Status: recorded by Gregory. No formal Church approval.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 20 (ch. 30 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

Two hundred bushels of meal in a famine

During a famine in Campania, the monastery was down to five loaves. Benedict said, “Indeed, to-day some want there is, but to-morrow you shall have plenty.” The next day two hundred bushels of meal were found at the monastery door; who brought them, Gregory says, “is unknown to this very day.”

Status: recorded by Gregory. No formal Church approval.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 21 (ch. 31 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

The dream plan for Terracina

When Benedict sent monks to found a monastery at Terracina, the abbot and the prior both saw him in their sleep, showing them where the buildings should go. Later he asked them: “Did not I appear to either of you in your sleep, and appointed how and where every place was to be builded?”

Status: recorded by Gregory. No formal Church approval.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 22 (ch. 32 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

Nuns absolved after their death

Two nuns of noble family lived near the abbey and could not curb their tongues, and they often provoked the good man who served them. Benedict sent word that he would excommunicate them unless they amended, and they did not. After they died and were buried in the church, the nurse who made an offering for them saw them rise from their graves and leave whenever the deacon told those who did not communicate to depart. When Benedict was told, he gave an offering with his own hands: “Go your ways, and cause this to be offered unto our Lord for them, and they shall not remain any longer excommunicate.” After it was offered, they were not seen to leave again.

Status: recorded by Gregory. No formal Church approval.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 23 (ch. 33 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

The boy cast out of his grave

A young monk who loved his parents more than reason went to their house without asking Benedict’s blessing, and died the same day. When he was buried, his body was found cast out of the grave the next day, and again the day after. His family went to Benedict in tears. He gave them the Communion of the Lord’s body: “Go, and lay with great reverence this our Lord’s body upon his breast, and so bury him.” After that the body lay quietly in the grave.

Status: recorded by Gregory. No formal Church approval.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 24 (ch. 34 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

The dragon

A monk so restless that he kept asking to leave was at last told by Benedict, wearied by his pleading, to go. He was no sooner out of the abbey gate than he met a dragon in the way with its mouth open, and he cried out, “Help, help! for this dragon will eat me up.” The monks who ran out saw no dragon, but found him shaking and brought him back, and he promised never to leave again.

Status: recorded by Gregory. No formal Church approval.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 25 (ch. 35 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

A leper boy healed

Gregory heard from Anthony, “honourable man,” that a boy of his father’s household was cured of leprosy by Benedict.

Status: recorded by Gregory from a named informant. No formal Church approval.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 26 (ch. 36 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

Twelve shillings owed, thirteen found

An honest man who owed twelve shillings (Latin solidi) came to Benedict for help. Benedict did not have so much money and prayed for two days. On the third day thirteen shillings were found on the chest where the monastery kept its corn. Gregory had the story from Peregrinus, one of Benedict’s disciples. The debtor is not a Goth; the Goth of the lost billhook is a different man.

Status: recorded by Gregory from a named informant. No formal Church approval.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 27 (ch. 37 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

A poisoned man healed by a touch

A man who had been poisoned was healed when Benedict touched him.

Status: recorded by Gregory. No formal Church approval.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 27 (ch. 38 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

The glass of oil and the overflowing barrel

In a time of famine Benedict ordered that the little oil the monastery had left be given to the subdeacon Agapitus. A glass of oil thrown from a window did not break, and afterward a barrel in the abbey filled until the oil overflowed.

Status: recorded by Gregory. No formal Church approval.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, chs. 28–29 (ch. 39 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

The devil dressed as a doctor

Benedict saw the devil disguised as a physician, riding a mule with a horn and a mortar, going to the monks “to give them a drench.” An old monk was afflicted, and Benedict freed him. Here Gregory notes that Benedict did some wonders by power and some by prayer.

Status: recorded by Gregory. No formal Church approval.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 30 (ch. 40 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

Zalla’s prisoner set free by a look

A cruel Goth named Zalla (Galla in the 1608 English) had a prisoner bound with cords. When Benedict looked at the bound man, the cords fell away. Gregory admires a man who, “sitting still, did appease the fury of that cruel Goth.”

Status: recorded by Gregory. No formal Church approval.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 31 (ch. 41 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

A dead child restored to his father

A countryman laid his dead son at the monastery gate and cried to Benedict, returning from the fields, “Give me my son, give me my son!” Benedict first refused: “such miracles are not for us to work, but for the blessed Apostles.” Then he stretched himself over the child and prayed, “Behold not, O Lord, my sins, but the faith of this man.” The child trembled, revived, and was given back to his father alive and well. Gregory says this came by prayer.

Status: recorded by Gregory. No formal Church approval.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 32 (ch. 42 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

Scholastica’s storm

On her yearly visit, Benedict’s sister Scholastica begged him to stay the night, and he refused to spend a night outside his abbey. Under a clear sky she laid her head on her folded hands and prayed in tears; as she lifted her head, thunder and rain broke, and he could not leave. “I desired you to stay, and you would not hear me,” she told him, “I have desired our good Lord, and he hath vouchsafed to grant my petition.” They talked all night. Gregory: “she did more which loved more.”

Status: recorded by Gregory. No formal Church approval. Gregory gives no year; tradition gives 10 February 543 for her death.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 33 (ch. 43 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

Scholastica’s soul seen as a dove

Three days later, standing in his cell, Benedict saw his sister’s soul rise to heaven “in the likeness of a dove.” He had her body laid in the grave he had prepared for himself.

Status: a vision recorded by Gregory. No formal Church approval.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 34 (ch. 44 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

The whole world in one ray of light

While the abbot Servandus was visiting, Benedict rose before the night office and prayed at a window of the tower where they slept. A light brighter than day came in the night, and, as Benedict later told it, the whole world seemed gathered together under a single ray of the sun before his eyes. He saw the soul of Germanus, bishop of Capua, carried to heaven by angels in a globe of fire. Servandus, called, saw only “a little remnant of the light,” and a messenger sent to Capua found that Germanus had died at that hour.

Status: a vision recorded by Gregory, from Benedict’s own account. No formal Church approval. Whether Benedict saw God himself in it was later debated by theologians.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 35 (ch. 45 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

His death foretold, and the road of lamps

In the year of his death Benedict told his monks the day; six days before, he had his grave opened. On the sixth day of a fever he received Communion in the oratory and died standing, held up by his disciples, with his hands raised in prayer. Two monks, one in his cell and one far away, saw the same vision: a road hung with tapestry and lit with lamps, running east from his cell to heaven, and a man who said, “This is the way by which the beloved servant of God, Bennet, is ascended up to heaven.”

Status: recorded by Gregory. No formal Church approval. Gregory gives no year, month, or day; the death year is given as 543, about 544, or 547 in different sources.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, ch. 37 (ch. 47 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

After Benedict’s death

A woman healed in his cave at Subiaco

Gregory writes that the cave at Subiaco where Benedict first lived “even to this very time, worketh miracles, if the faith of them that pray requireth.” He tells of a woman out of her mind who had lately slept in the cave and was healed.

Status: recorded by Gregory, writing about fifty years after Benedict’s death. No formal Church approval.

Source: Gregory the Great, Dialogues II, chs. 37–38 (chs. 47–48 in Luck), Gardner ed., 1911.

The miracles recorded at Fleury

The abbey of Fleury on the Loire in France claimed from the ninth century that it held Benedict’s relics, carried from the ruined Monte Cassino; Monte Cassino disputes the claim. Monks of Fleury, among them Andrew of Fleury around 1043, compiled a collection called the Miracula S. Benedicti. Its accounts were not read for this entry.

Status: a medieval collection of a contested shrine. No formal Church approval.

Source: “Fleury Abbey,” Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fleury_Abbey; “St. Benedict of Nursia,” Catholic Encyclopedia, 1907, New Advent.

The cross at Metten, 1647

At a witchcraft trial at Natternberg in Bavaria in 1647, the accused said they had no power over the nearby abbey of Metten because it was protected by the cross. The monks found crosses with mysterious letters painted on their walls; a manuscript of 1415 at Metten showed Benedict with a cross-staff and a scroll, with the words written out in full. This led to the St. Benedict Medal, approved by Pope Benedict XIV in 1741 and 1742. The verses on the medal are medieval, not Benedict’s own.

Status: popular devotion and a reported protection, not a miracle examined by the Church. The medal itself is an approved sacramental.

Source: Michael Ott, “Medal of Saint Benedict,” Catholic Encyclopedia, 1912, New Advent; Prosper Guéranger, The Medal or Cross of St. Benedict, 1880, Internet Archive.
