## Listed for the fact checker (not changed)

1. **Theoprobus finds Saint Benedict weeping (ch. "Saint Benedict Weeps Over His Abbey's Future").** The text follows the 1608 English: "the man of God used not in his prayers to weep, but rather to be sad". I only made the sentence read clearly: "Saint Benedict was usually sad when he prayed, but he did not weep." The Latin (II.17) seems to say the reverse: "nec tamen, ut vir Dei consueverat, orando plangeret sed moerendo" (he was not weeping in prayer, as he usually did, but in grief). Please check which reading to follow.
2. **"the son of a man of the court"** (the falling wall, II.11). Gardner's own endnote (Chapter XI, p. 70) says the 1608 "courtier" is wrong for "cuiusdam curialis filius": a curialis was a member of a town council. A possible fix: "the son of a town councillor". Not changed, because it is a fact.
3. **"probably" in the story.** "He left Rome probably a few years before the year 500." and "Probably in 542, he set out for the abbey." `write-slim.md` keeps "probably" out of the narrative, but taking it out would make the date surer than the source. Please decide (for example, give the date without "probably" in the story and keep the doubt in the note).
4. **Translated words without quotation marks.** These follow a source closely but are not marked: "To a soul that sees the Creator, all of creation is small." (note 88 gives the Latin and "our translation"); "Their souls were always one in God, and their bodies were not parted in death." (close to Gregory II.34); "by the cross, the book, and the plow" (Paul VI, "cruce, libro et aratro"); "there he lived with himself" (II.3, "habitavit secum"). Please decide whether each needs quotation marks.
5. **"in the sight of his Creator, who sees into every heart."** I did not find "who sees into every heart" in the Latin of II.3 at a quick look. Please check whether it comes from the source.
6. **Names without "Saint" in "A Note on the Sources" and the story:** Constantine, Simplicius, Valentinian, Honoratus, and Servandus. Some of them may be venerated as saints. I was not sure, so I left them plain (`titles-pass.md`).
7. **Church words still without a meaning:** "an order in the later sense of the word" (the Rule spreads), "consecrate the new church", "the sacred crypt", "the Holy See". An explanation would need words the checker should approve. "Rule" is first used in the opening (in its plain sense) and explained only in the chapter "Saint Benedict Writes a Rule for His Monks"; I left it, because the plain sense is clear.
8. **"until the Lombard troubles of Saint Gregory's own time"** (the sieve). This is the 1608 wording. The Lombards are explained only near the end. Left as it is.
9. **Enfide.** "Some good men lived there at the church of Saint Peter out of charity" follows the 1608 English ("other virtuous men, which for charity lived in that place"). The Latin can also be read as the good men's charity keeping Saint Benedict and his nurse there. Left as it is.

## Resolved by the checker (2026-10-06)

2. "the son of a man of the court": now "the son of a town councillor" (Latin "curialis"; Gardner p. 70 note).

7. Church words: rule, order, consecrate, crypt, Holy See, relics now carry their meaning.
