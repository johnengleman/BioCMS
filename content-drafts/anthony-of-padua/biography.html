In 1263 the friars of Padua opened a tomb. They had laid a friar in it thirty-two years before, in the summer heat, after four days in which the people of Padua fought one another for his body. He had been thirty-six when he died. Now a new church stood ready to receive him, and the Minister General of the Franciscans, Brother Bonaventure, had come to move him into it.

What they found was what three decades leave behind. The flesh had fallen to dust, and the bones had come apart from one another. Yet the hair, the teeth and the skin of the head were whole, and the dead man’s tongue was incorrupt, fresh and red. Bonaventure lifted it out, kissed it, and spoke to it.

“O blessed tongue, which always didst bless the Lord, and cause others to bless Him, now does it appear plainly how highly thou wert esteemed by God!”

— St. Bonaventure at the opening of the grave, 1263, as given in The Chronicle of St. Antony of Padua (1883)

That tongue had once held a whole city still. In the last Lent of its owner’s life, crowds of thirty thousand and more had stood in the fields outside Padua to hear it. While it spoke, not the slightest sound could be heard among them. Pope Gregory IX had called him the Ark of the Covenant. He was declared a saint less than a year after his death, and seven centuries later Pope Pius XII named him a Doctor of the Church for the sermons he left behind. He had hidden a scholar’s learning in a hermitage kitchen until he was ordered to speak. Once he spoke, he could bring an archbishop to tears and draw a sermon out of a spider’s web.

Yet the man who owned that tongue had never asked to be a preacher. What Fernando of Lisbon wanted, from the day he gave up his name and his settled, learned life, was to shed his blood for Christ among the Saracens. He reached Morocco and fell so ill that he could do nothing there at all. Contrary winds drove the ship that was bringing him home onto the coast of Sicily. Then came a great gathering of friars at Assisi in 1221. When the meeting broke up and the brothers were sent off to their provinces, his early Life records one plain fact: of all the friars there, Anthony alone was asked for by no one.

How did the friar nobody wanted become the voice that shut the shops of Padua? And why did a man who begged to die for Christ end by giving Him, instead, his voice? The answer begins beside a cathedral in Lisbon, in a church that held a martyr’s body.

A Boy Beside the Cathedral

He was baptized Fernando, and he was born in Lisbon in a house near the cathedral, the church of the Virgin, where the body of the martyr St. Vincent lay. The sources do not give the day. The Bollandist scholars, counting back from his age at death, put his birth in about 1195. Of his parents the early Lives say little except that they were upright before God, and the names that later writers gave them come from centuries afterward. A seventeenth-century writer recorded that the family house had by then become a church of St. Antony, and that his mother lay buried in St. Vincent’s.

Bartholomew of Trent, who saw and knew him in person and wrote about him around 1240, began with the simplest fact of all: “Anthony, whom I myself saw and knew, was a Spaniard by birth.” Spaniard, for Bartholomew, meant a man of the Iberian peninsula, and the early Latin Life likewise places Lisbon in Spain. The boy was given to the cathedral to be taught, and he learned his letters and his conduct in the shadow of the Virgin’s church.

Jean Rigauld, a Franciscan who wrote Anthony’s Life near the end of that century, had questioned old friars who still remembered him. Rigauld says that whatever pleasures the young men around him followed, Fernando “never gave way to his passions.” In about 1210, as the old reckoning has it, he went out of the city to the house of the Canons Regular of St. Augustine at St. Vincent’s, just outside Lisbon, and took their habit.

Home was too close. Friends and relatives came to see him so often that the peace he had come for was gone. After about two years he asked to be moved to the canons’ house of Holy Cross at Coimbra, the house of St. Theotonius. He wished, Rigauld says, “like another Abraham, to go forth out of his country and from his kindred.” His superior let him go only with difficulty.

At Holy Cross he stayed for about eight years, and he spent them reading. He read the Fathers of the Church, and what he read, he kept. Both early Lives stop to wonder at his memory. Rigauld wrote that “his memory served him in the place of books, and he was presently filled with the Spirit of Wisdom.” A few years on, at Forlì, he would still lean on that memory rather than on books.

It was a life he could have lived until he died: a learned canon in a quiet house, known to his brothers and to almost no one else. Then, in 1220, the bones of five dead men came through Coimbra.

Five Friars Dead in Morocco

The five had been friars of the new order of Francis of Assisi, the Friars Minor. Their names were Berard, Otto, Peter, Adjutus and Accursius, and they had been killed in Morocco. Rigauld calls them “valiant athletes.” The Infante Pedro of Portugal brought their relics home. As he passed through Coimbra he spoke of their miracles, and of the great dangers from which, he said, their merits had saved him.

For Fernando the news worked like a trumpet. The early Latin Life compares him to an elephant roused to battle by the sight of blood. Rigauld adds a war-horse: he was “like a war-horse ready for the day of battle, or an elephant excited by the sight of blood.” Five men had gone to Morocco and been given the crown he wanted. He decided on the spot that he would follow them.

“I will take their habit,” he said, “and so will I, too, go to the fight.”

— Fernando at Coimbra, 1220, in Jean Rigauld’s Life (c. 1293–1300)

A few Franciscans lived in a small hermitage near Coimbra called St. Antony, at a place known as Olivais, and they were poor enough to beg. One day some of them came to Holy Cross asking for alms. Fernando took them aside, told them what he wanted, and asked them for their habit. On the day they had set, they came back and clothed him in it.

Not everyone at Holy Cross was pleased. A seventeenth-century writer even records a papal letter of 1232 ordering the prior of the canons to stop harassing the friars. As Fernando left, one of the canons, who loved him and took his going hard, called after him in words that sound half like a blessing and half like a jeer. Fernando gave him an answer he would not live to see fulfilled.

“Go, I tell you, go. You will perhaps become a saint.” … “When you hear that I am a saint, surely you will give thanks to God.”

— A canon of Holy Cross and Fernando’s reply, Coimbra, 1220, in Rigauld’s Life

At the hermitage he took a new name, Antonius, after the hermitage itself. The early Life gives his reason, and it is a revealing one: he wanted the people who came looking for Fernando not to find him easily. He had already left one house because visitors would not leave him alone. Now he hid behind a friar’s habit and a new name.

He had not joined the friars to hide, though. Very soon he asked his superior for the thing he had come for, and he was given it. Brother Anthony would go to the Saracens.

The Friar Nobody Wanted

Bartholomew of Trent put the purpose of the voyage in one line. Anthony wished, he wrote, to preach to the Saracens and to receive from them the crown of martyrdom. He sailed to Morocco and arrived. Then nothing happened the way he had imagined it. “He arrived, indeed, among the Saracens,” Rigauld wrote, “but his efforts to obtain the much coveted palm of martyrdom were vain.”

He fell gravely ill. For a long time he lay there, too sick to do any work for the faith he had come to die for, and in the end there was nothing to do but go home. So he took ship, but contrary winds drove it off its course and put him ashore in Sicily instead. The Bollandists’ notes guess that he landed at Messina or Taormina.

The friars were about to hold a General Chapter at Assisi at Pentecost of 1221, and weak and sick as he was, Anthony went. Francis, the founder, was still alive, though Brother Elias was governing the Order as his vicar. When the chapter ended, the friars were sent off to their provinces, each minister taking the brothers he wanted. Anthony was unknown to them all, weak and plainly unwell, and he looked useless. The early Life records the result in a sentence that the rest of his story would turn upside down: of all the friars there, Anthony alone was asked for by no one.

So he asked. He went to Brother Gratian, the minister of the province of Romagna, and begged him to take him and teach him the discipline of the Order. Gratian took him and sent him to a hermitage called Monte Paolo, near the town of Forlì. The man who had set out to be a martyr in Africa was going to a cell in the Italian hills, where no one had any idea what he knew.

The Kitchen Friar at Forlì

At Monte Paolo he lived in a cell made in a crypt, on bread and water. For a time he was so weak that he could hardly drag himself to the meal. The Latin Life describes him there as simple among the simple. He kept his gift of wisdom hidden under the look of a man without learning, and his brothers took him at his word. Rigauld adds what he did instead of teaching. He washed the plates and pots, and he washed the friars’ feet and kissed them.

The hiding was so complete that it took an accident to end it. By the Bollandists’ reckoning he was about twenty-six when he was sent with other friars to Forlì to be ordained. Some Dominican friars were there as well, and at the meal the local minister wanted someone to speak a few words to the brothers.

He asked the Dominicans first. Each one excused himself. He asked his own Franciscans, and they excused themselves too. Then he turned to Anthony, whose learning no one suspected, and told him to speak. Anthony protested that he was fitter for washing the kitchen pots than for opening the mysteries of Scripture. The minister would not take no for an answer, and when the request became a command, Anthony obeyed.

Tradition says that the text he chose that day was the line from St. Paul about Christ, who became obedient for us unto death. If so, it was a fitting choice for a man who had sailed to Africa to die and been sent back alive, and who was now speaking only because he had been ordered to. He began simply. Then the words began to come, out of a memory that had been storing them for years, and the room changed. His listeners marvelled at the sudden turn and said that they had hardly ever heard such things. “Never man spoke like this man,” they told one another, in the words the Gospel uses of Christ.

“It was marvellous that an unknown Religious, of whose learning the Friars themselves were unaware, should have been so persistently compelled to preach! But this was not of man, but of God.”

— Jean Rigauld, Life of St. Antony, on the sermon at Forlì

The news travelled up the Order to the Minister General, who gave Anthony a new duty: from now on he was to preach. The friar nobody had asked for was being sent out on the roads, and they would hold him for most of the years he had left.

A Heretic Thirty Years Blind

He preached through Romagna and the towns of northern Italy, and the early Lives try hard to describe what it was like to hear him. The Latin Life calls him gracious and severe at once, so that people felt love and fear at the same time while he spoke. He could be frightening. Even famous preachers, it says, trembled under his words and hid their faces behind a hand or a sleeve.

He also became a teacher. Around 1222 or 1223, as his 1911 English biographer has it, he was made the first lector, or teacher of theology, for the friars at Bologna. A short letter from Francis approving this is quoted by later writers. It first appears in a collection of Anthony’s miracles written down in the fourteenth century, and scholars still disagree about whether it is genuine. One version has Francis greet him as “my bishop.” In the version a 1911 biography gives, Francis consents with a condition. Anthony may teach the brothers theology, so long as their study does not put out the spirit of prayer and devotion that the Rule requires. Whatever its origin, the letter names the tension in Anthony’s own life. He was a learned man in an order of poor men, told to teach without letting learning crowd out prayer.

Much of his preaching was aimed at the heretics of northern Italy. At Rimini, Rigauld says, he brought back to the Church “a noted heresiarch, named Bononillo, who had been for thirty years blinded by the pestilential mists of error.”

Some of his opponents tried another method. Rigauld tells of heretics in Italy who served Anthony a dish of poisoned food. He made the sign of the cross over it and told them, “I do this not to tempt God, but to show my constant and earnest zeal for your salvation, and the triumph of the Faith.” He ate it and took no harm, and the men who had served it to him returned to the Catholic faith. Rigauld wrote that story down some sixty years after Anthony’s death. By then the friars were telling others, stranger ones, that would make Anthony famous in a way he could never have imagined.

A Sermon to the Fishes

The most famous of them is the sermon to the fishes. In Rigauld’s telling, some heretics near Padua openly mocked Anthony’s preaching. He went down to the river nearby and spoke to them.

“Since you show yourselves unworthy to hear the Word of God, behold, I turn to the fishes, that your unbelief may be put to shame.”

— Anthony to the heretics, in Rigauld’s Life (c. 1293–1300)

Then he preached to the fish, and the fish came. They gathered in crowds, lifted their heads out of the water, and even opened their mouths, as if they were listening. Later chronicles gave the story more colour and moved it to Rimini, to the mouth of the river Marecchia. There the fish lined up in order of size, the smallest nearest the shore, until, in one chronicler’s words, “there could be seen no fairer sight.” Anthony began, “My brothers, the fishes,” and reminded them that they alone, of all creatures, had been saved in the great flood. Sabine Baring-Gould also set the story at Rimini. He thought the fish themselves were “no doubt an addition” by people who had missed the biting satire of a preacher turning his back on men too deaf to hear him.

A second story, which Rigauld says he found in a small treatise on Anthony’s miracles, became just as well known. An obstinate heretic refused to believe that Christ was present in the Sacrament. Anthony put a question to him: “If the horse you so often ride should adore the Body of Christ really present under the species of bread, would you believe in the truth of the Sacrament of our Lord as taught by the Church?”

The heretic kept his horse without food, and on the third day he brought it out, with oats on one side and Anthony on the other holding the Host. The hungry horse passed the oats by and knelt. In later tellings the heretic became Bonvillo of Rimini and the horse became a mule, and other writers moved the scene to Toulouse. In the 1883 chronicle’s version Bonvillo says to Anthony, “You get the better of me in words, because you are more learned than I am, but let us come to proofs.” The kneeling mule became one of Anthony’s emblems in art.

Francis at the Door in Arles

In the mid-1220s he went to southern France. At Arles, in Provence, the friars of the province gathered for a chapter, and Anthony preached to them. His subject was the title fixed over the cross of Christ.

Francis was alive at the time, Rigauld notes, but far away in another part of Italy. While Anthony was speaking, a friar named Monaldo saw Francis at the door of the room. He “appeared at the door of the Chapter-room, raised in the air, with his arms stretched out in the form of a cross blessing the Friars.”

The story is old. Thomas of Celano put it in his first Life of Francis, written in Anthony’s lifetime. There he says that the Lord had opened Brother Antony’s mind to understand the Scriptures. Bartholomew of Trent, who had known Anthony, told it about 1240, and the Latin Life of Anthony includes it. The Bollandists guessed that it happened in 1226. It is a picture worth holding: the founder of the Order in the doorway, raised in the air, his arms spread like the cross his friar is preaching on, blessing the brothers who are listening.

From Provence the road led on into the Limousin, around Limoges, where the Order would make him a superior over other friars.

“I Speak to Thee, Mitre-Bearer”

Rigauld’s Life is the main record of Anthony’s years in France. Anthony served as guardian of the friars at Limoges and then as custodian over the friaries of the region. He founded the house of the friars at Brive. The Limousin stories have names and places in them, and several come with Rigauld’s note on who told him.

On the night of Holy Thursday, the story goes, Anthony was preaching at the church of St. Pierre-du-Queyroix in Limoges. At the same hour his friars were singing Matins in their own house, and as guardian he was due to read one of the lessons. When the moment came, he was there in the choir among them, and he read it, while he was also preaching in the town. At St. Junien the platform he was preaching from collapsed, just as he had told the crowd it would, and no one was hurt.

He did not spare the powerful. Rigauld introduces the next story with the words “Certain Friars, worthy of belief, related to me the following.” At a synod of the clergy at Bourges, Anthony turned to the archbishop and said, “I speak to thee, mitre-bearer!” He rebuked him so sharply that the archbishop was moved to tears.

Other stories show the guardian taking care of his own. A young novice named Peter, of Limoges, was so tormented by temptation that he was ready to give up, until Anthony breathed on him and the temptation left him. At Brive, a maid-servant carried cabbages to the friars through pouring rain, and not a drop touched her. Rigauld heard that one from Pierre de Brive, a canon of Noblac, who had it from his mother. And at a poor woman’s house in a village in Provence, where Anthony was a guest, a glass was broken and the wine was spilt from the cask. The glass, Rigauld says, was made whole again, and the wine was restored.

The best-remembered Limousin scene happened in the open. So many people came to hear him that no church could hold them, so they gathered in a great open space at Limoges called the Creux des Arènes. In the middle of the sermon the thunder began and the rain started to fall, and the people began to get up and go.

“Fear not; do not move nor cease listening to the Divine Word, for I trust in Him who never suffers us to trust in vain that the rain will not touch you.”

— Anthony at the Creux des Arènes, Limoges, in Rigauld’s Life

They stayed, and the rain fell all around them and not on them. When Rigauld joined the Order, he wrote, friars who had been in that crowd were still alive to tell him about it.

Somewhere in these French years, at Montpellier, he lectured to the friars on the Psalms, and he kept his notes for the lectures in a book. One day the book disappeared. What happened to it would one day give him the work for which most of the world now knows him, but that story belongs at the end.

Sermons Written in Winter

By 1230 he was serving as a minister provincial. That year the friars gathered at Assisi for the General Chapter at which the relics of Francis were moved. There they released Anthony from all office and gave him freedom to preach wherever he chose. Pope Gregory IX had heard him and called him the Ark of the Covenant. Now the Cardinal-Bishop of Ostia, whom the Bollandists identify with the future Pope Alexander IV, gave him a task. Anthony had already written out a cycle of sermons for the Sundays of the year, and the cardinal asked him to write the sermons for the feasts of the saints as well.

He went to Padua, and through the winter of 1230 he wrote, though he was sick. The written sermons are notes, not finished speeches. They are dense outlines built from long chains of Bible texts. They are full of what his 1911 biographer called arbitrary etymologies: he could make the name Thamar mean bitter, changed and palm, and preach on all three. He read Scripture in its four senses, the literal, the allegorical, the moral and the anagogical. He taught four attitudes of prayer, which he called obsecratio, oratio, postulatio and gratiarum actio. His English biographer warned that the sermons “cannot be judged from their written records.” The real sermons happened out loud, and he probably spoke them from memory. When he died, the work on the saints’ feasts was only half done.

Yet the notes still show the man. He drew his sermons from the living world around him. In a Lenten sermon that survives in its old Latin, he wrote that the vine is a man or a soul, “Moraliter vinea ista est homo vel anima.” A vine left uncared for, he went on, runs wild and is good for nothing but the fire.

“Man’s soul is a vine, … for to bring forth fruit it must be carefully and ceaselessly cultivated. … The vine left to itself becomes the wildest of all growing things. Its wood is useless, except to burn, when it is dry.”

— Anthony, sermon, in C. M. Antony’s English (1911)

In a sermon for Septuagesima a spider became a warning. “The devil spreads his web like a spider!” he wrote. “The spider begins from the outside and gradually works inwards with thousands of threads, sitting to watch in the centre.” What the devil is after is the heart, he went on, “for is not the heart the source of life?” Cranes flew with one bird in front calling to the rest. Be like them, he told his listeners: point out the way to those who do not know it, help on the slow, and “soothe those who are too hasty.” In the lilies of the field he found a place for penitents living out in the world. “Hermits flourish in the desert, … monks stand like flowers in the cloister garth, protected from the great heat of the sun, but the penitent lives and thrives and blossoms in the field of the world.”

And there were the doves. The most tender of all his surviving passages begins with a dove making her nest.

“Be as the dove who makes her nest in the deepest cleft of the rock. This cleft, in which the soul should hide itself, is the wound in the side of Jesus Christ. … The world despises them as useless, but it is with these that we shall build our nest, deep in the Rock, in the Heart of Jesus.”

— Anthony, sermon, in C. M. Antony’s English (1911)

The things the world despises, in that passage, are the scattered bits the dove builds with, and he names them: humility, gentleness, poverty, patience and mortification. A man who had once wanted to pour out his blood for Christ now told his listeners that the place for a soul to hide was inside Christ’s wound. In Padua that winter, the largest crowds of his life were only weeks away.

Cloth Dyed With Sweat and Blood

The same notes that hold the dove hold passages with a much harder edge. Anthony preached against the sins by which men grew rich at others’ expense: theft, usury and dishonest gain. One Lenten sermon, preserved in the Latin of the old printed editions, cries out, “O quot induuntur hodie purpura, panno tincto sudore et sanguine pauperis.” How many are clothed in purple today, in cloth dyed with the sweat and blood of the poor!

“How many rich men of our day are clad in purple — that is in stuffs dyed with the sweat and blood of the poor, because the clothes they wear are woven out of theft, larceny, usury, and illegitimate gain? … But the garment dyed with the blood of the poor shall be the prey of eternal flames.”

— Anthony, Lenten sermon, in C. M. Antony’s English (1911)

The usurer, he said elsewhere, was worse than Judas. Judas at least brought back the thirty pieces of silver, but the usurer guarded and kept his gains. The cure he offered was almsgiving: “when the bucket of almsgiving is let down into the well of riches it makes their possession purer to the conscience and more agreeable to God.” A later legend, which the Basilica of Padua still tells among his miracles, has him preaching at the funeral of a rich miser in Tuscany. “That rich man is dead and buried in hell,” he says in that legend, “but go to his treasures and there you will find his heart.”

He was no easier on his own Church. He compared bishops who stayed away from their flocks to idols with eyes that do not see, and with “hands to gather money, but not to touch the wounds of Christ.” To the priests who went to law, he put a question: where had they found “these disputes, these lawsuits, these intrigues for transitory and perishable things”? Was it, he asked, in the prophets, the Gospel, the letters of St. Paul, or the Rules of St. Benedict and St. Augustine? And he spoke of judgment in words that one modern critic thought might lie behind the hymn Dies Irae: “Whither will the sinner flee; where will he hide? Flight is impossible; he cannot conceal himself! To appear before God is unbearable terror.”

Near the end of the Lent of 1231, on 15 March, the city of Padua passed a law to protect debtors, and a copy of it still survives in a museum there. By then the man preaching against usury had a congregation larger than any church in Padua could hold.

Thirty Thousand and Not a Sound

Lent began on 5 February that year, with Easter on 23 March, and Anthony preached every day of it. At first he preached in the churches. Soon no church could hold the crowds, and he moved out into the fields around the city. The shops of Padua shut their doors while he spoke.

“Amongst thirty thousand men, and even more, not the slightest sound could be heard when the servant of God was speaking.”

— Jean Rigauld, Life of St. Antony, on his preaching at Padua

The preacher those crowds were listening to did not look like the slender friar of popular pictures. According to the Latin Life he was heavy by nature and ill for much of the time, yet he went on preaching and hearing confessions. His portraits disagree about him. A mosaic in the Lateran showed him lean and bearded. The Bollandists objected that friars who were priests did not wear beards in his century, and they preferred a likeness kept at Padua, which showed a grave, young face. He would not ease the Rule for his body, the “little ass,” as Francis had called his own. After the sermons so many people came to confess that the priests of Padua could not hear them all. Anthony was often so busy that he went fasting until evening without noticing.

The early Life lists what that Lent did to the city. Enemies were reconciled. Prisoners were set free. Usurers gave back what they had taken, and women who had lived in public sin turned from it. Rigauld preserved one more voice. Around 1292 an old man told one of the friars how he had lived before he heard Anthony. “I was a robber by profession,” he said, “I belonged to a gang of a dozen brigands; we lived in the forest and plundered all the passers-by.” Rigauld set the old robber’s story among the fruits of Anthony’s preaching.

The confessional produced its own stories. One, told by a later chronicle, concerns a young man who wept so hard that he could not get his sins out, so Anthony had him write them down. As Anthony read the paper, each sin on it was wiped away. Another, which Franciscan writers attribute to him and which belongs to legend, shows how heavily his words could fall. A man of Padua named Leonardo confessed that he had kicked his own mother. Anthony told him that “the foot of him who kicks his mother deserves to be cut off.” Leonardo went home and took the words literally, and cut off his foot with a hatchet. His mother ran to Anthony and upbraided him, and the story ends with Anthony joining the foot back to the leg without wound or scar.

Not all of that Lent was spent among crowds, though. One night early in it, the Latin Life says, the devil seized Anthony by the throat and would have strangled him. He called on the Virgin, the patroness of the church where he had learned his letters as a boy. A light filled the room, and the devil fled. The Life adds that Anthony told this story himself, later, among friends. He was spending everything he had.

A Cell in a Walnut Tree

After Easter, he stood one day on a hill and looked out over Padua and the plain around it. He said that very soon this place would be honoured. Within three months, Padua would be fighting over his body.

When harvest came, he went to Camposampiero, a village north of Padua where a few poor friars lived. The lord of the place, Count Tiso, built him a little cell up in a walnut tree whose branches spread from a single trunk, and there Anthony prayed and rested. Tiso later became his disciple as a member of the Third Order. A later tradition says that Tiso once looked in on him and saw the Child Jesus with the saint. Writers do not agree on the place: Italian tradition says Camposampiero, the 1883 chronicle sets it in Tiso’s own house at Padua, and French writers set it in the Limousin. The old documents do not decide between them.

One day, at the dinner hour, a sudden illness struck him and his strength gave out. The friars at Camposampiero were poor, and he did not want to be a burden on them, so he asked to be taken back to Padua. They put him on an ox cart, and it set out for the city. Near Padua a friar met them on the road and persuaded him not to go to the friary in the middle of town, where the crowds would give him no rest. Instead they took him to Arcella, a small house just outside Padua where the friars who served the Poor Clares lived.

“I See My Lord”

The cart reached Arcella at dusk, and he arrived worn out. A modern Franciscan writer, reading the earliest account of these hours, notes in it “signs of intense anguish.” The friars around him soon saw that he was dying, and he made his confession and received Communion. Then he began to sing a hymn to the Virgin, “O gloriosa Domina,” O glorious Lady, the same Lady in whose church he had been taught as a child.

When the hymn was over, he raised his eyes and looked upward for a long time. The friars asked him what he saw.

He answered: “I see my Lord.”

— Anthony at Arcella, 13 June 1231, in Rigauld’s Life

They brought the oil to anoint him. He told them, in the early Life’s account, that he had the anointing within his soul already. Still, it was good to receive it, he said, and he would gladly do so. He sang the penitential psalms with the friars, and then, Rigauld wrote, “like one quietly falling asleep,” he died. It was the evening of Friday, 13 June 1231, the weekday of the Crucifixion. He was thirty-six. He had gone to Africa to die for Christ and been refused. Now he died in a small house outside an Italian city, worn out by a Lent of preaching, looking at his Lord.

That same day, later writers add, he appeared far away at Vercelli to Abbot Thomas Gallo. In Padua the friars meant to keep the death quiet. They did not manage it. Before anyone had told the city, children began running through the streets of Padua, crying out.

“The holy father is dead; St Antony is dead!”

— The children of Padua, 13 June 1231, in Rigauld’s Life

Four Days of Fighting

Padua heard the children. Arcella lay near the quarter called Capo di Ponte, the Head of the Bridge, and its people armed themselves to keep the body. The friars wanted him buried with his brothers at the church of St. Mary in the city. For four days the two sides faced each other over the body of a man who had spent his last Lent reconciling enemies.

At night, people tried to break into the house to reach the body. Rigauld says they were “stupefied and struck with blindness,” and could not get in. The body lay in a wooden coffin in a shallow grave in the summer heat. When a rumour went round that someone meant to steal it, a mob came with swords and clubs. The minister of the friars arrived at vespers. The Podestà, the city’s chief magistrate, set a fine of a hundred pounds for violence.

On the fourth day the bishop ruled that Anthony should be buried with the friars. The Podestà had a bridge of boats built across the river, and the men of Capo di Ponte smashed it to pieces. Both sides took up arms. The friars and the Poor Clares prayed. In the end the Podestà bound the ringleaders to go home, and the body was carried in procession to St. Mary’s and buried there, on a Tuesday. Rigauld saw in the whole uproar a sign that the people’s veneration was given “not to a coffin, but to a pearl of great price.”

The cures began on the day of the burial. The first people to come barefoot to the tomb were the people of Capo di Ponte, who had fought hardest to keep him. Then came processions of friars and citizens, of the university and of the bishop. They carried candles so large that they had to be cut down before they would fit inside the church; some, a chronicle says, took sixteen men to carry. His body had come into Padua on a Tuesday, and the same chronicle calls it well attested that no sufferer who asked his help on a Tuesday ever failed to be cured. So Tuesday became his day. Within a month of his death, Padua had sent envoys to the pope.

A Saint Within a Year

The envoys spoke for the whole city: the bishop, the clergy, the friars, the students of the university, and the Podestà. The students sent their own letter as eyewitnesses. Pope Gregory IX ordered an inquiry. A commission at Padua, made up of the bishop, the prior of St. Benedict’s and a Dominican prior, Giovanni da Vicenza, examined the miracles, and the Bishop of Sabina was charged with reviewing them. Two cardinal legates who were in that region wrote to the papal court in his favour. A chronicle counts forty-five miracles judicially proved in the months between his death and his canonization.

Some at the court objected that a year had not yet passed since his death. According to the Latin Life, one of those who objected then had a dream. He saw the pope consecrating a church and asking for relics for the altar, and he saw a covered body whose relics were so precious that everyone present strove to take them. When he woke, he found the envoys from Padua at his door, and the objections fell away. A later chronicle tells it of a cardinal who met the envoys and said, “This is our dream and its interpretation.” On Pentecost, 30 May 1232, eleven months and seventeen days after Anthony died, Gregory had the approved miracles read out in the cathedral at Spoleto. He raised his hands and declared Anthony a saint, and he set the feast on 13 June, the day of his death. In the bull of canonization the pope said that he had known Anthony himself.

The miracles read out that day included the healing of nineteen people who had been lame and six who had been blind. Three deaf people had heard, three mute people had spoken, two with epilepsy and two with fevers had been cured, and others had risen from paralysis. Two people had been raised from the dead. A later chronicle says that in Lisbon, at the moment the sentence was pronounced, the church bells began to ring by themselves.

Back at Coimbra, the canon who had told Fernando he might become a saint had his answer. Rigauld heard from the custodian of St. Jacques that the bed Anthony had slept in at Holy Cross was now treated with the greatest veneration. He heard, too, that after the canonization Anthony appeared to a canon of Holy Cross and told him of the death of the Queen of Portugal, almost at the hour she died. An aunt of Anthony’s was a canoness at Coimbra. A chronicle records that it became a common saying “that to obtain a favour of St. Antony it was a sure way to get his aunt to ask for it.”

The first account of his life was written within the year, by a friar of the Padua community, to be read in the liturgy of his feast. The Bishop of Lisbon, Soeiro, then at the papal court, supplied the story of the early years. That first Life, the Assidua, gave about fourteen pages to everything before Padua, twenty-four to Padua and his death, and forty to the miracles of the first year. On 13 June 1232, one year after he died, Padua kept his feast for the first time, and four years later the city decreed that the shops should shut on his day.

The Tongue in the Dust

Padua built a new church for him, and in 1263 the body was moved into it. That was the day the friars found the flesh turned to dust and the tongue fresh and red, and Bonaventure held it up for everyone to see. Rigauld, writing a generation later, drew the lesson the friars took from it: “the tongue so miraculously preserved was a proof of how faithfully the Man of God had preached the immortal truth.”

The relics were moved again in 1310, to a chapel on the left side of the nave, and again in 1350. A cardinal named Guy, who believed that Anthony had preserved his life, gave a reliquary to hold the saint’s jaw. In the seventeenth century a Jesuit, Ignatius Martini, kissed the reliquary of the tongue and was so struck by the vain use he had made of his own tongue that he repented on the spot. In 1745 the future Pope Clement XIII, then Cardinal Rezzonico, moved the smaller relics to the chapel where they still lie. A chronicle tells that when fire broke out in 1749, the people who climbed onto the burning roof were not hurt and the saint’s altar was untouched. In a storm in 1756 the lead roof of the city hall fell in, and again, the chronicle says, no one was injured.

In 1981, for the 750th anniversary of his death, a commission appointed by the Holy See opened the tomb once more. Inside they found the wooden chest from Bonaventure’s time, holding the skeleton, the habit, and the ashes of the body, along with bundles that bore written notes. The relics lay in a crystal urn for about a month, and more than 650,000 people came to see them. The tongue and the vocal organs are kept in the Chapel of Relics at the Basilica in Padua, where they can be seen today.

His words have had their own afterlife. In 1946 Pope Pius XII declared Anthony a Doctor of the Church, with the title Doctor Evangelicus, the Evangelical Doctor, because of the richness of his sermons. In 2010 Pope Benedict XVI gave a whole general audience to him, and he chose lines that show the same man who walked the vineyards. Charity, Anthony taught, is the soul of faith. Christ hangs on the cross so that we may look at it as into a mirror. And a preacher who preaches Jesus will see hard hearts melt.

The Finder of Lost Things

Most people who know Anthony’s name, though, know it for something else. They call on him when they have lost something, and the story behind that begins with the book of notes that disappeared at Montpellier.

It was a book he used in teaching the friars. The collection of his miracles written about 1367 calls it a glossed psalter, and the 1883 chronicle and his 1911 biographer call it his manuscript of notes on the Psalms. A novice took it and went off with it. Anthony missed the book, and he prayed that it would come back.

On a bridge, the story goes, the fleeing novice was stopped by a terrible figure who ordered him to take the book back. In the 1367 collection the figure is a devil with an axe, and in the 1883 chronicle he is a man of savage countenance with a drawn sword. The novice came back to Anthony’s cell trembling, with the stolen book in his hands, and returned it. The story was first written down in a collection of Anthony’s miracles about 1367, more than a century after his death, though the devotion may be older. An English chronicle of 1883 remarks that “it was the only time when he, who was to be the great finder of lost things in after ages, asked a grace of the kind for himself.”

Over the centuries Catholics came to invoke him especially for the recovery of things lost, and travellers call on him as well. By the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, chronicles were recording the things found through his help: the manuscript of the theologian Ambrosius Catharinus, a sacristan’s thurible, even money belonging to King Charles II.

He never got what he set out for. He wanted to die for Christ in Morocco and was sent home sick. Hiding was what he wanted next, and an order to speak at a meal in Forlì ended his hiding for good. What God asked of him instead was slower and, in its way, just as costly. From Forlì to Arcella he gave away his voice, his strength and his health on the roads of Italy and France, until his heavy, sick body gave out at thirty-six. He never shed his blood. But he died on a Friday, looking at the Lord he had wanted to die for, and when his tomb was opened, the tongue he had spent in Christ’s service was found fresh and red.

The friar nobody wanted had found, long before, where a soul should make its home. He said it in his sermon about the dove. The cleft in the rock where the soul should hide is the wound in the side of Jesus Christ. The world despises the bits the dove builds with, “but it is with these that we shall build our nest, deep in the Rock, in the Heart of Jesus.”

A Note on the Sources

Our researchers could not obtain the oldest Life of Anthony, the anonymous Assidua, nor the Benignitas, the Dialogus, John Peckham’s Life or the Fioretti. This account rests chiefly on the Latin Life printed by the Bollandists, which a 1904 editor identified as Julian of Speyer’s, and on Jean Rigauld’s Life of about 1293–1300. No source our researchers read says that Anthony ever met Francis in person, so no meeting is described here. Rigauld sets the fish sermon near Padua, later chronicles and Baring-Gould set it at Rimini, and the Catholic Encyclopedia sets it on the Brenta. Rigauld’s kneeling animal is a horse, while later versions make it a mule at Rimini or at Toulouse. Italian writers set the vision of the Child Jesus at Camposampiero, the 1883 chronicle sets it in Tiso’s house at Padua, and French writers set it in the Limousin. Sources date the finding of the tongue to 8 April or to 18 February 1263, so we give only the year; the early Lives also say almost nothing about the years between Forlì and 1230. We leave out stories that first appear in Surius and Wadding, who wrote in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, such as a quarrel with Brother Elias and a capon turned into fish. We also leave out the meeting with the tyrant Ezzelino da Romano, which the Catholic Encyclopedia calls an apocryphal legend. No source we read traces the popular “Tony, Tony, turn around” rhyme to him. The wording of Francis’s letter comes from later sources, and the letter’s authenticity is disputed.

How This Life Was Researched

Three researchers worked from 23 sources in Latin, English and French, a few of them read only in part. Among them are the Latin Life printed in the Acta Sanctorum, probably written before 1240, and the testimony of Bartholomew of Trent, who wrote about 1240 that he had seen and known Anthony. They also read Jean Rigauld’s Life, written about 1293–1300 by a friar who questioned brothers who still remembered him. Two sixteenth-century printed editions of Anthony’s Latin Lenten sermons were searched, and two English passages were checked against the Latin. The rest are nineteenth- and twentieth-century chronicles, encyclopedias and Church documents. The researchers flagged several details as rarely told on English-language pages. Among them are the devil’s attack in Lent 1231, the Latin Life’s description of Anthony as heavy and often ill, and the candles too large to fit inside the church. Not read were the Latin Assidua, the Benignitas, the Dialogus, Peckham’s Life and the Fioretti.
