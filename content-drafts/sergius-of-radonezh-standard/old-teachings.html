Sergius left no writings. Only spiritual instructions remembered by his disciples are known, so what follows is the example of his life as his biographer Epiphanius recorded it, arranged by theme. It is the example of a life, not a body of his own doctrine. The translations from Russian are our own.

Poverty Chosen Freely

Sergius chose poverty and kept it all his life. He gave his whole inheritance to his younger brother Petr before he went into the forest. As hegumen he wore sermyaga, the coarse undyed cloth of peasants, and once took a piece of cloth that seven monks had refused and wore it until it fell apart. A visiting plowman took him for an orphan. When Metropolitan Alexis, near death, gave him a jeweled gold cross and offered him the metropolitan see, he refused.

“Forgive me, master: from my youth I have not been a gold-bearer.”

— Sergius to Metropolitan Alexis, Life of Sergius, chapter 25 (our translation)

He added that in old age he wished to stay poor. Alexis died in 1378, and Sergius remained hegumen of his forest monastery.

Work With One’s Own Hands

The hegumen of Trinity worked first and hardest. With his brother Stefan he cut logs and carried them on his shoulders to build the first church. As hegumen he was first at work and first in church, and he stood through the services without leaning on the wall. Visitors found him digging the garden. When the monastery was hungry, he took an axe and built a porch for an old monk in exchange for bread. Newcomers wore a black coat and did every kind of labor for a long time before they received the habit.

A Common Life

At first each monk at Trinity lived in his own cell and kept his own things. Patriarch Philotheus of Constantinople sent Sergius a letter saying that one rule was missing: the monastery had no common life. With the agreement of Metropolitan Alexis, Sergius introduced a common rule, with a cellarer, cooks, bakers, and an infirmarian, and no private property for anyone. His disciples carried this life to other monasteries, among them Kirzhach, Simonov, Dubenka, Golutvin, and Vysoky.

Trust in God in Want

Sergius warned every newcomer that the forest offered hunger, thirst, and poverty, and told them to prepare their hearts for patience and many trials, not for food, drink, or rest. When the bread ran out, he forbade begging and waited. On the fourth day he worked for a sieve of moldy bread, and while the brothers grumbled, fresh loaves arrived at the gate from a benefactor whose name the brothers never learned. He told the hungry monks to feed the men who had carried the bread first. The same trust shows in the letter he sent to Grand Prince Dmitri when the army stopped in doubt before the Tatar host:

“Without any doubt, lord, go boldly against their fierceness.”

— Sergius to Grand Prince Dmitri, from the Life as quoted in A. P. Rogovich, 1922 (our translation)

Hiding God’s Gifts

Sergius did everything he could to keep praise away from himself. When people called the spring in the ravine by his name, he was angry: the Lord had given the water, he said, not he. When a dead child lived again after his prayer, he told the father the boy had only been cold, and forbade him to tell. He asked Isaac and Makarii to keep the angel at the altar secret while he lived, and Simon to say nothing of the fire in the chalice until after his death.

Correction Without Anger

At night Sergius walked past the cells. Where he heard prayer, he blessed it; where he heard talk or laughter, he knocked on the door or window and went on. The next morning he spoke to the brothers concerned quietly and gently, “from afar,” as Epiphanius says, as if telling parables, and never in anger. When the monks grumbled against him over hunger and over water, he answered with patience and with prayer.

His Last Charge

When he fell ill in September 1392, Sergius gathered the brothers and told them to keep the Orthodox faith and unity among themselves, to keep themselves pure, to welcome strangers, to avoid quarrels, and not to seek honors. He entrusted them to the Lord Almighty and to His Most Pure Mother, whom he called their refuge and wall against the snares of the enemy. Six months earlier he had already handed the monastery to his disciple Nikon.
