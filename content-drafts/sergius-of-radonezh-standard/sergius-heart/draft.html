For a long time Saint Sergius asked God for one thing: to live without a single person near him. In a forest near Radonezh, God gave it to him. He had a hut and a small wooden church. No road led there, no village stood nearby, and wolves came by night and by day. Today that place is the Trinity Lavra, the great shrine of the Russian Church. The Church calls the man who wanted to be alone there the Hegumen of the Russian land. A hegumen is the abbot of a monastery, so the title makes him the abbot of all Russia.⟦E:5–6|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles of Sergius, ch. 5–6; “Sergii Radonezhskii,” Russian Wikipedia, introduction (the title).⟧

He founded the monastery that grew into the Lavra, and at the Patriarch’s word he gave it the common life, in which no monk owns anything. His disciples carried that life to new monasteries. He turned down the highest seat in the Russian Church, and he blessed the Grand Prince of Moscow before the war with Mamai. He had asked for none of it. The story begins with a boy who could not read.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 16–25; Dimitry of Rostov, Life of Sergius, pp. 533–534, 541–542.⟧

A Boy Who Cannot Read Meets a Stranger

Saint Sergius was born Bartholomew, in the land of Rostov. His father was Saint Kirill and his mother was Saint Maria. They belonged to the boyars, the old nobility of Rostov. Before the boy was born, he cried out three times from his mother’s womb during one Sunday Liturgy. The women around her wondered what this child would be. As a baby he would not take milk on Wednesdays and Fridays, the fast days of the week.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 2, 4.⟧

At seven he was sent to learn his letters. His brothers Stefan and Petr learned quickly, but Bartholomew could not. His teacher punished him, his parents scolded him, and his schoolmates reproached him. So he went off alone and prayed with tears, asking the Lord to teach him and give him understanding.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 2 (end).⟧

One day his father sent him out to look for some foals. In a field he came upon an old monk in a black robe, standing under an oak and praying with tears. The boy bowed, came near, and waited until the prayer was done. Then the old man looked at him and called him over. He blessed the boy and kissed him, and asked what he was looking for. The boy had been sent for foals, but he asked for something else.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 3.⟧

“Holy father, pray to God for me, so that I can learn to read.”⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 3 (our translation).⟧

The old man lifted his hands and prayed, and he said “Amen.” Then he reached into his pocket and took out a small piece of white wheat bread with three fingers. It was holy bread, blessed at the Liturgy, and he held it out like a treasure. “Open your mouth, child. Take this and eat.” The bread tasted as sweet as honey. The boy remembered the psalm that calls God’s words sweeter than honey, and he said it aloud. The old man told him not to grieve over his letters any longer. He said that from that day the Lord would give him better knowledge than his brothers and friends had.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 3 (our translation).⟧

The boy fell at his feet in tears. He begged the stranger to come home with him, because his parents loved such men very much. Saint Kirill and Saint Maria came out and bowed to their guest, and a meal was ready. But the old man went first into the family chapel and took the boy with him. He began the set prayers and told the boy to read a psalm. “I cannot,” the boy said. The old man told him to read without doubting. So Bartholomew read, and he read clearly and well. His parents and brothers stood amazed, and they praised God.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 3.⟧

At the table the parents begged him to stay. They told him about the three cries before the child was born, and they asked him to ease their worry about the boy. He told them not to be afraid. The boy would read well, he said, and he would be great before God and before people. As he was leaving, he spoke the words the family kept.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 3.⟧

“Your son will be a dwelling of the Holy Trinity, and he will lead many after him to understand the commandments of God.”

— The stranger under the oak, in the Life of Sergius

They walked him to the gate, and there he vanished from their sight. Saint Kirill and Saint Maria believed he was an angel, sent to give their son the knowledge of letters.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 3 (our translation).⟧

From then on the boy could read any book. Before he was twelve he lived on bread and water, and on Wednesdays and Fridays he ate nothing at all. His mother begged him to stop. He answered that no one is clean before God, and he quoted Job and the psalm of David’s repentance. “You are not twelve years old,” she said, “and you speak of your sins.”⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 3–4 (our translation).⟧

In those years Saint Kirill was still a rich man.

Two Brothers Build a Church in the Forest

Little by little, Saint Kirill lost his wealth. He went often with his prince to the Tatar Horde, and the Horde took heavy tribute. Tatar raids came, and the harvests failed. Worst of all was a great Tatar raid, and after it Rostov passed under the power of Moscow. Moscow sent its governors, and they beat the people of Rostov and took their goods. They even hanged the eldest boyar of the town head down, and fear spread through the whole country around. So Saint Kirill gathered his household and moved to Radonezh, a village that the Grand Prince had given to his younger son. Many others from Rostov went with him. The family settled near the church of the Nativity of Christ.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 5.⟧

Stefan and Petr married, but Bartholomew would not. He wanted the monk’s life with his whole soul, and he asked his father again and again to let him go. “My lord, let me go now, as you promised, so that I can begin the monk’s life with your blessing.” His parents asked him to wait. “We are old, poor, and sick, and there is no one to care for us,” they said. “When you lay us in the grave and cover us with earth, then you will do what you desire.”⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 5 (our translation).⟧

He promised gladly, and he kept his promise. He cared for them every day until both of them took monastic vows, each in a monastery. A few years later they died, and to their last breath they blessed him. He sang the funeral hymns over them, kissed them, and laid them in the grave with tears. For forty days he kept their memory with prayers and services, and with alms and food for the poor. Then he went home with a glad heart, as if he had found a treasure. Now nothing held him back.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 5.⟧

He looked at the house and everything in it, and he wanted none of it. He remembered the psalm of David: “I would go far away and stay in the wilderness, hoping in God, who saves me.” What he wanted more than anything was the monk’s life, lived “in fasting and silence.” So he called his younger brother Petr and gave him the whole inheritance, and he kept nothing for himself.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 5 (our translation).⟧

Their older brother Stefan was a monk by then. He had lived only a short time with his wife before she died, leaving him two sons, one of them named Ivan. Then he had entered the monastery of the Intercession at Khotkovo. Bartholomew went to him there and asked him to come and look for a lonely place. Stefan agreed and left the monastery. The two brothers walked through many forests until they came to a place deep in the thicket where there was a spring of water. They walked around it and came to love it, and they prayed.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 5.⟧

With their own hands they cut down trees and carried the logs on their shoulders. First they built a hut to sleep in, with a storeroom and a roof. Then they built a cell, and then a small church. When it stood ready, Bartholomew asked his older brother which saint or feast the church should honor. “You know the answer to your question as well as I do,” said Stefan. Their parents had often told Bartholomew, “You are a gift of God and not our son.” Priests had explained the three cries before his birth: the boy would be a disciple of the Holy Trinity. So the church must bear the name of the Holy Trinity.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 5 (our translation).⟧

Bartholomew sighed from the depth of his heart. “You have spoken rightly, my lord.” He had wanted the same thing all along, he said, and he had asked only out of humility. Priests came from Saint Theognostus, the Metropolitan who led the Russian Church. They brought what was needed for the altar, with holy relics of martyrs. They consecrated the church to the Holy Trinity, early in the reign of Grand Prince Simeon of Moscow.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 5 (our translation).⟧

Stefan stayed a while longer. Then he saw how hard the life was. There was no food and nothing to drink, and nothing could be brought in. There was no road and no path, no village and no house, and no one ever passed by. On every side stood the forest. Stefan grew weary of it. He left the wilderness and his own brother and went to Moscow, to the Epiphany monastery. There he lived a strict life and sang in the choir beside a monk named Alexis, who would one day be Metropolitan. Grand Prince Simeon later made Stefan abbot of that monastery and took him as his own spiritual father.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 5.⟧

The two were brothers of one father and one mother, and they had chosen the place together. One of them wanted a monastery in the city. The other “made the wilderness like a city.”⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 5 (our translation).⟧

Bartholomew had his solitude now, and he was not yet even a monk.

Saint Sergius Becomes a Monk and Lives Alone

He would not take the monk’s robe until he had learned the whole rule of monastic life. Then he called an abbot named Mitrofan to his little church. He bowed at Mitrofan’s feet and asked him again and again to make him a monk. He had wanted it since his youth, he said, and only his parents’ will had held him back.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 6.⟧

“Now I am free of everything, and I thirst to be made a monk. As a deer longs for a spring of water, so my soul thirsts for a monk’s life in the wilderness.”

— Bartholomew to the abbot Mitrofan, in the Life of Sergius

Mitrofan made him a monk in that church on 7 October, the feast of the martyrs Saint Sergius and Saint Bacchus. So the new monk took the name Sergius. He was twenty-three years old. When he received Holy Communion, a sweet fragrance filled the church and was felt outside it too. For seven days he stayed in the church and ate nothing but the holy bread from the abbot’s hand. The psalms were always on his lips: “One day in your courts is better than a thousand.”⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 6 (our translation).⟧

When Mitrofan was ready to go, Saint Sergius saw him off. “Today you are going away, father, and leaving me alone, as I wanted,” he said. “For a long time I have longed with all my thoughts and desires to live alone in the wilderness, without a single person.” God had heard him, he said, and he thanked God for it. Then he asked Mitrofan to teach him how to live alone, how to pray, and how to stand against the enemy, because he had no experience. Mitrofan was amazed that he should ask. He answered with the words of Saint Paul: God is faithful, and He will not let you be tempted beyond your strength. Then he gave Saint Sergius into God’s hands and left him.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 6 (our translation).⟧

Wild animals came to him often, by night and by day. Wolves came in packs, howling and roaring, and sometimes bears came. Saint Sergius “feared them a little, like any man,” but he turned to God in prayer, and none of them harmed him.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 6 (our translation).⟧

One night he went into the church to sing the night service. As he began, the wall of the church opened. The devil came in with a crowd of demon warriors, not through the door but like a thief. They wore Lithuanian clothes and tall, pointed Lithuanian caps. They rushed at him as if to tear the church down to the ground, grinding their teeth and threatening to kill him. “Run, go away from here, and do not stay in this place any longer.”⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 7 (our translation).⟧

Saint Sergius armed himself with prayer. He called on the Holy Trinity and put his hope in the Mother of God, whom the Orthodox Church calls the Theotokos, the one who gave birth to God. The Cross was his weapon, and he said the psalm: “Let God rise, and let His enemies vanish.” At once the devil and his army vanished, and Saint Sergius thanked God.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 7 (our translation).⟧

A few days later they came again. He was praying through the night in his hut, awake and not dreaming, when a noise and a crashing began. A crowd of demons shouted at him to leave. The place was far from people, they said, and he would die of hunger, or robbers or wild beasts would kill him. Before long the power of God covered him and scattered them. He thanked the Lord for not leaving him and for hearing him so quickly. The Life says the devil fought so hard because he feared that a monastery would rise in that place.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 7.⟧

Other animals came close, circled him, and sniffed at him. One bear began to come often, and Saint Sergius saw that it came for a little food and meant no harm. So he would bring a small piece of bread out of his hut and lay it on a stump or a log. The bear would take the bread in its mouth and go. When there was no bread, it did not go. It stood there a long time and looked around, “like a hard creditor who wants his debt.”⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 7 (our translation).⟧

Saint Sergius had only bread and water from the spring, and not much of either. Often there was no bread for a whole day, and then both he and the bear went hungry. If he had one piece, he broke it in two and gave half to the bear. Sometimes he gave the bear the whole piece and fasted himself, because he would rather go hungry than cheat the animal. The bear came many times a day, and this went on for more than a year.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 7.⟧

No one knew how he lived; only God saw it. He spent whole nights in prayer, and he spoke with God alone. He had tasted the sweetness of silence, and he did not want to give it up.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 7.⟧

About two years went by in this way. Then God put it into the hearts of some monks to come to him through the forest.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 7.⟧

Monks Come and the Bread Runs Out

They came one at a time, then in twos and sometimes in threes. They fell at his feet. “Father, accept us,” they said. “We want to live with you in this place and save our souls.” He would not accept them. “You cannot live in this place,” he told them, “or bear the hardships of the wilderness: hunger, thirst, sorrow, poverty, and need.” They said they would bear it with God’s help. “Only do not send us away from you and from this place that we love.”⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 7 (our translation).⟧

He saw their faith, and he gave way.

“I wanted to live alone in this wilderness, brothers, and to die here. But if God has willed it, and if it pleases Him that there be a monastery in this place and many brothers gathered here, let the Lord’s will be done! I receive you with joy.”

— Saint Sergius to the first monks, in the Life of Sergius

He told them to prepare their hearts for patience and many troubles, not for food, drink, or rest. Each man built his own cell. Saint Sergius was young and had the strength of two men. He served them all like a servant bought with money. He chopped their wood and carried it to each cell. He ground the grain and baked the bread, and he cut and sewed their shoes and clothes. He carried water from the spring in two buckets on his shoulders, up the hill to each brother’s door.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 7 (our translation).⟧

After a year the abbot Mitrofan, who had made him a monk, fell ill and died. Saint Sergius grieved for him greatly and prayed that God would give the brothers an abbot. The brothers came to him together. “Father, we cannot live without an abbot,” they said, and they asked him to take the place. He sighed from the depth of his soul. “I have never even thought of being abbot. My soul wants one thing: to die a monk in this place.” They told him he must become abbot or find them one, or they would all leave. So in the end he went to the bishop, who made him a priest and the abbot of the little community.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 7 (our translation).⟧

When he came home, the brothers bowed to the ground. He lay face down in the church and prayed with tears before the icon of the Holy Trinity. Then he turned to them. “Pray for me, brothers, for I am a man of little learning and no experience,” he said. He had been given the care of their souls, and he would have to answer for it.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 8 (our translation).⟧

For two or three years there were twelve brothers, and Saint Sergius was the thirteenth. He served the Liturgy every day, and he baked the holy bread for it himself. He would let no one else do it, though many wished to. Then one day Stefan came back from Moscow with his younger son Ivan. Holding the boy by the right hand, he led him into the church and gave him to his brother to be made a monk. Saint Sergius gave the boy the name Theodore. The old monks marveled that Stefan would give God his own child.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 8–9.⟧

The abbot went out to work before anyone else, and he came first to church and stood without leaning on the wall. Late in the evening, especially on the long, dark nights of autumn and winter, he walked past the cells. Where he heard prayer, he thanked God and prayed for the monk inside. Where he heard talk or laughter, he knocked on the door or the window and walked on. In the morning he called the monk to him. He did not scold him in anger. He spoke “indirectly, quietly and gently, as if telling parables,” until the man understood.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 9 (our translation).⟧

In those first years the monastery had no stores of anything, and once the bread ran out, and the salt with it. Saint Sergius forbade the brothers to go out and beg from lay people in the villages. They were to ask God and wait, and he kept the same rule himself. He waited three days.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 10; Dimitry of Rostov, Life of Sergius, p. 525.⟧

At dawn on the fourth day he took an axe and went to an old monk named Daniil. “Old man, I hear you want to add a porch to your cell,” he said. “I do not want my hands to sit idle, so I have come to you. Let me build the porch.” Daniil agreed, but he feared the price. “I only wait for a carpenter from the village. I do not dare hire you, because you will charge me a lot.”⟦x|Dimitry of Rostov, Life of Sergius, p. 525 (our translation).⟧

Saint Sergius asked only for old, rotten bread. “I take no pay before I have done the work,” he said, and he told Daniil to keep the bread until the ninth hour of the day. He worked all day. In the evening Daniil brought out a sieve of old moldy bread and some dry cakes. Saint Sergius set it before him, prayed, and blessed it. He ate it with water, with no soup, no salt, and nothing else. Some of the brothers watched and saw dust come from his mouth as he ate the spoiled bread.⟦x|Dimitry of Rostov, Life of Sergius, pp. 525–526 (our translation); Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 10.⟧

The brothers had eaten nothing for two days, and one of them spoke for the rest. “How long will you forbid us to leave and ask lay people for bread? We will bear one more night. In the morning we will leave and not come back, so that we do not die of hunger.” The others came to the abbot with him. “Our bread has gone moldy!” they cried. They had obeyed him, and now they were starving. Saint Sergius reminded them how Christ spoke of the birds, which do not sow or reap and which the heavenly Father feeds.⟦x|Dimitry of Rostov, Life of Sergius, p. 526 (our translation); Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 10 (our translation).⟧

“If He feeds the birds, can He not give food to us? Now is the time for patience, and we complain. If we bear this short trial with thanks, the trial itself will do us great good. Even gold is not made pure without fire.”

— Saint Sergius to his monks, in the Life of Sergius by Saint Dimitry of Rostov

He was still speaking when someone knocked at the gate. The gatekeeper looked through the little window and saw a cart full of bread. He was very hungry himself, but in his joy he did not stop to open the gate. He ran to the abbot instead. “Father, bless the men who brought the loaves! Through your prayers they have brought us much food, and it is at the gate.” The brothers opened the gate, saw the cart and the baskets, and praised God. The men who had brought it said only that “a rich man who loved Christ” had sent it to Saint Sergius and the brothers who lived with him. Saint Sergius told the brothers to feed the carriers before anyone else ate. But the carriers would not stay, and they hurried away.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 10 (our translation); Dimitry of Rostov, Life of Sergius, p. 526.⟧

Saint Sergius was as hungry as anyone, and still he did not touch the bread. He had the wooden board struck to call the monks to church, and he led them in to give thanks to God. Only then did he sit down with them. He prayed over the loaves, blessed them, broke them, and shared them out. The bread was still warm and soft.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 10.⟧

A Farmer Mistakes Saint Sergius for a Beggar

Saint Sergius wore clothes of rough, undyed peasant cloth. Once the monastery was given a poor piece of cloth, and seven monks in turn refused it. Saint Sergius took it and made himself a robe, and he wore the robe until it fell apart.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 11; Dimitry of Rostov, Life of Sergius, p. 530.⟧

A farmer came from a distant village to see him, and he asked the brothers where the abbot was. Saint Sergius was digging in the vegetable garden, so they sent the man to the fence. Through a gap he saw an old man in a poor robe, torn and patched, digging the ground. The farmer had hoped to see a prophet in great honor and glory, and he thought the monks were mocking him. “I came to see a prophet,” he said, “and you show me an orphan.” “The old man you saw is our abbot,” the brothers told him. When Saint Sergius came out of the garden, the farmer turned his back on him and laughed.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 11 (our translation); Dimitry of Rostov, Life of Sergius, p. 531 (our translation).⟧

The brothers were angry and wanted to throw him out, but Saint Sergius would not allow it. He called the farmer over. He bowed to the ground before him and kissed him. He sat him at his right hand and gave him food. The farmer was still unhappy. “Do not be sad, friend,” Saint Sergius told him. “By God’s mercy, no one leaves here sad, and your wish will be granted.”⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 11; Dimitry of Rostov, Life of Sergius, p. 531 (our translation).⟧

As they talked, word came that a prince had arrived with many servants. Saint Sergius went out to meet him. The prince bowed to the ground from far off and asked for his blessing. Then the prince and the old man sat side by side and talked, and the servants pushed the farmer far back. The farmer asked a man nearby who the old man was. “Are you a newcomer here, that you do not know this old man? This is the venerable Sergius.” The farmer began to tremble and blamed himself for his lack of faith. When the prince had gone, he fell at the abbot’s feet and asked forgiveness. Years later he came back and became a monk himself.⟦x|Dimitry of Rostov, Life of Sergius, pp. 531–532 (our translation); Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 11.⟧

The brothers had their own complaints. As their number grew, water ran short, and they had to carry it from far away. Some of them grumbled at him, not once but many times. “Why did you settle here without thinking, and build a monastery in a place where there is no water?” they asked. When the first monks came years before, he had said that he would accept a monastery “if God has willed it.” Now he spoke as a man who knew.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 7, 12 (our translation).⟧

“I wanted to live here in silence, alone. But it pleased God to raise up such a monastery in this place, to the glory of His most holy name. Be bold in prayer, and do not lose heart.”

— Saint Sergius to his monks, in the Life of Sergius

God had once brought water out of a rock for His people in the desert, he reminded them. Would He leave them, who worked for Him day and night? Then he took one monk with him and went down into a wooded ravine below the monastery. The old people said no spring had ever run there. He found a little rainwater standing in a ditch, knelt beside it, and prayed. He asked God to show His power here, as He had through Moses in the desert, and to give them water. Then he made the sign of the Cross over the rainwater, and a full spring burst up out of the ground.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 12.⟧

The spring kept running, and the monastery drew its water from it. Sick people who came to it with faith were healed, and some carried its water home to the sick. For ten or fifteen years people called it Sergius’s spring, and the name made him angry. “Let me never hear you call this spring by my name,” he said. “I did not give you the water. The Lord gave it to us, unworthy as we are.”⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 12 (our translation).⟧

Far away in Constantinople, the Patriarch had heard of him too.

A Letter From Constantinople Changes the Monastery

One day Greek envoys came to the gate from Patriarch Saint Philotheus of Constantinople. They brought his blessing and his gifts: a cross, a paraman, and a schema. A paraman is a small cloth with a cross on it, worn on a monk’s back. The schema is the robe of the strictest rank of monks. The monastery itself had no gold or silver then, and it lacked even the things it needed. The envoys also brought a letter.⟦x|Dimitry of Rostov, Life of Sergius, p. 533; Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 16 and the closing praise.⟧

“By God’s mercy, the Archbishop of Constantinople and Universal Patriarch, lord Philotheus, to Sergius, son and fellow servant of our humility in the Holy Spirit: grace, peace, and our blessing. We heard of your virtuous life by God’s commandments, and we praised God and glorified His name. But one thing, and the most important, is still missing for you: you have no common life.”

— Patriarch Saint Philotheus to Saint Sergius, in the Life of Sergius by Saint Dimitry of Rostov

Until then each monk had kept his own cell and his own things. Saint Sergius did not decide alone. He took the letter to Metropolitan Saint Alexis in Moscow. This was the monk who had once sung in the choir beside his brother. “Holy master, what do you command?” he asked. Saint Alexis agreed with the Patriarch and advised a strict common life.⟦x|Dimitry of Rostov, Life of Sergius, pp. 533–534 (our translation); Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 5, 16.⟧

So Saint Sergius set up the common life. No monk could own anything or call anything his own, and food, clothing, and housing were shared by all. He named brothers to care for the table, the household, the treasury, the sick, and the church. What was left over went to the poor, to guests, and to the sick. He promised that the monastery would stand as long as the brothers kept the works of mercy. The poverty he had chosen for himself in the forest was now the rule of the whole house.⟦x|Dimitry of Rostov, Life of Sergius, p. 534.⟧

While Theodore still lived at the Trinity, Saint Sergius once served the Liturgy with his brother Stefan and with Theodore. Prince Vladimir of Serpukhov was staying at the monastery then. In the church was Saint Isaac the Silent, a monk who loved stillness. During the service he saw a fourth priest at the altar, shining so brightly that he could not look at his face. A second monk named Makarii saw him too. The prince’s people had brought no priest with them. Afterward the two monks pressed their abbot until he told them the truth. It was an angel of the Lord, he said, and an angel served with him whenever he served the Liturgy. He asked them to tell no one while he lived.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 21 and note 67 (our translation).⟧

Theodore grew up in his uncle’s monastery and became a priest. In time he asked to found a monastery of his own, and after a long while Saint Sergius blessed him. Theodore built the Simonov monastery by the Moscow River and gave it the common life. As Theodore’s fame grew, it sometimes troubled Saint Sergius, and he prayed without ceasing that his nephew would finish his road without stumbling. Saint Theodore later became Archbishop of Rostov.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 20.⟧

Some time later, trouble came again. The Life says the devil put a thought into the minds of some brothers: to reject Saint Sergius as their abbot. One Saturday the evening service was being sung, and Saint Sergius stood at the altar in his priest’s robes. Stefan stood in the choir on the left. Suddenly he asked the monk who led the singing, “Who gave you this book?” “The abbot gave it to me,” the monk said. Stefan cried out in anger. “Who is abbot here? Was it not I who first founded this place?” Other unfitting words were spoken too.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 17 (our translation).⟧

Saint Sergius heard it all at the altar, and he said nothing. He would not quarrel with his brother over the abbot’s place. When the service ended, he came out of the church and did not go to his cell. No one saw him go. He left the monastery alone and took the road toward Kinela, and night fell while he was still walking. He slept in the wilderness, alone again, as he had once asked God to let him live. In the morning he went on to the monastery at Makhrishche and asked its abbot for a monk to show him the lonely places. The two of them walked through many wild thickets. At last they came to a beautiful place by the river Kirzhach.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 17.⟧

Back at the Trinity, the brothers could not find him anywhere. They were frightened, and they searched the lonely places and the towns. At last one of them learned at Makhrishche where he had gone. They began to come to him at Kirzhach by twos and threes, and sometimes in large groups. He built a cell, and with the blessing of Saint Alexis he built a church. Princes and boyars came and gave silver for the building, and soon many brothers lived there.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 17; Dimitry of Rostov, Life of Sergius, p. 535.⟧

The monks who had stayed at the Trinity went to the Metropolitan. “We live like sheep without a shepherd,” they told him, and they begged him to send their abbot home. Saint Alexis sent two senior abbots to him with a letter. It was enough, he wrote, that Saint Sergius had built a church at Kirzhach and gathered brothers there. Let him put a disciple in his place and return to the monastery of the Holy Trinity. “Those who oppose you I will drive out of the monastery, so that not one who wishes you ill is left there,” the Metropolitan promised. “Only do not disobey us.”⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 17 (our translation).⟧

Saint Sergius gave the envoys his answer. “Tell my lord the Metropolitan: everything that comes from your mouth I will receive with joy, as if it came from the mouth of Christ. I will not disobey you in anything.”⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 17 (our translation).⟧

He chose his disciple Saint Roman to lead the new house, sent him to the Metropolitan to be made a priest, and went home. The whole brotherhood came out to meet him. When they saw him, it seemed to them that a second sun had risen. Some kissed his hands, some his feet, and some the hem of his robe, and some ran ahead to look at him. “Glory to you, O God, who cares for all!” they cried. And he rejoiced in spirit too, seeing his children together again.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 17 (our translation); Dimitry of Rostov, Life of Sergius, p. 535.⟧

Then Saint Alexis sent for him once more.

Saint Sergius Refuses to Lead the Russian Church

Saint Alexis felt his strength failing and knew his end was near. He wanted Saint Sergius to follow him as Metropolitan. When Saint Sergius came, the Metropolitan held out his own paraman with a gold cross set with precious stones. Saint Sergius bowed and would not take it.⟦x|Dimitry of Rostov, Life of Sergius, p. 541; Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 25.⟧

“Forgive me, holy master. From my youth I have not worn gold, and in old age I want even more to stay in poverty.”

— Saint Sergius to Metropolitan Saint Alexis, in the Life of Sergius by Saint Dimitry of Rostov

“Beloved, I know you have always lived so,” Saint Alexis answered. “Now show obedience and accept the blessing we give you.” With his own hands he put the paraman on Saint Sergius. Then he told him why he had called him. He had led the Russian Church as long as God willed, he said, and now he wanted to find a man who could shepherd it after him. He found no one but Saint Sergius. “I know well that everyone wants you, from the prince to the last man. So accept the rank of bishop now, and after my death you will take my seat.”⟦x|Dimitry of Rostov, Life of Sergius, p. 541 (our translation).⟧

Saint Sergius was greatly troubled. To him the rank of bishop would be a great misfortune. “Forgive me, master. This is beyond my strength. You will never find in me what you seek. I am a sinner and the last of all people.” Saint Alexis pressed him for a long time and quoted Scripture to him. Saint Sergius would not bend. “Holy master, if you do not want to drive me out of this land, so that you hear nothing more of me, do not speak of this again. Let no one else speak to me so. No one will find me willing.”⟦x|Dimitry of Rostov, Life of Sergius, pp. 541–542 (our translation); Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 25.⟧

Saint Alexis said no more. He was afraid that Saint Sergius would flee to some far wilderness, and that Moscow would lose such a light. He comforted him with words of the spirit and sent him back to his monastery in peace.⟦x|Dimitry of Rostov, Life of Sergius, p. 542; Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 25.⟧

Saint Alexis reposed on 12 February 1378. Saint Alexis had been Stefan’s friend in the choir, Saint Sergius’s counselor about the common life, and the father who called him home from Kirzhach. Now the grand princes begged Saint Sergius again to become Metropolitan. He stood “firm as a diamond.”⟦x|Dimitry of Rostov, Life of Sergius, p. 542; Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 25 (our translation).⟧

Soon a greater fear came over the whole Russian land.

Saint Sergius Blesses the Prince Before Battle

Word came that a prince of the Horde named Mamai had gathered all the Tatar armies and was marching on Russia. The people were seized with fear. Grand Prince Saint Dmitri Ivanovich of Moscow had great faith in Saint Sergius and believed he had the gift of seeing what was to come. He rode to the monastery and asked him one question: did Saint Sergius bid him go out against the godless? Saint Sergius blessed him, armed him with prayer, and answered.⟦x|Life of Sergius, Old Russian text, BLDR vol. 6, chapter “On the Victory over Mamai”; Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 22.⟧

“It is right, my lord, that you care for the Christian flock God has given you. Go against the godless. With God helping you, you will win, and you will come back to your own land safe, with great honor.”

— Saint Sergius to Grand Prince Saint Dmitri, in the Life of Sergius

“If God helps me, Father, I will build a monastery in the name of the Most Pure Mother of God,” the prince vowed. He took the blessing and left in haste.⟦x|Life of Sergius, Old Russian text, BLDR vol. 6, “On the Victory over Mamai” (our translation).⟧

Saint Dmitri gathered all his men and marched. Later accounts say this march led to the Kulikovo field by the river Don in September 1380. The Russians saw the Tatar host and how great it was, and they stopped in doubt. Many were afraid. At that very hour a runner arrived with a letter from Saint Sergius. “Without any doubt, my lord, go boldly against their fierceness. Do not be afraid at all. God will surely help you.”⟦x|Life of Sergius, Old Russian text, BLDR vol. 6, “On the Victory over Mamai” (our translation); Dimitry of Rostov, Life of Sergius, pp. 543–544 (the field and the date).⟧

The prince and his whole army took heart. Saint Dmitri prayed to the great God who made heaven and earth to be his helper against the enemies of His holy name. Then the battle began, and many men fell. The Tatars were beaten and fled.⟦x|Life of Sergius, Old Russian text, BLDR vol. 6, “On the Victory over Mamai”; Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 22.⟧

Many days’ walk away, Saint Sergius stood in prayer with his brothers, and he saw the battle as if it were near. A little later he told the brothers of the victory. Then he named the fallen Russian men one by one and prayed for the repose of their souls.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 22; Life of Sergius, Old Russian text, BLDR vol. 6, “On the Victory over Mamai.”⟧

Saint Dmitri came back to the monastery to thank him and gave it a rich gift. He reminded the abbot of his vow. Saint Sergius went out to find a place, and on the river Dubenka he built a church of the Dormition of the Mother of God. He made his disciple Saint Savva its abbot. Another time the prince asked him to bless a place for a monastery at Golutvin, near Kolomna. Saint Sergius went there on foot, as he always traveled, and built the church. When Prince Vladimir of Serpukhov asked for a monastery too, Saint Sergius gave him his disciple Saint Athanasius to lead it, though he loved Saint Athanasius very much.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 22–24.⟧

The prince came to him often for counsel and for blessing, and he asked Saint Sergius to stand as godfather to his children. The prince of Ryazan would make no peace with Moscow. In 1385 Saint Sergius went to Ryazan as Saint Dmitri’s envoy. He talked with the angry prince for a long time about the soul, about peace, and about love. His quiet and gentle words moved the prince, who agreed to a lasting peace. Saint Dmitri reposed in 1389.⟦x|Dimitry of Rostov, Life of Sergius, p. 546; Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, editors’ notes 80 and 175.⟧

Through all these years, one prayer stayed with Saint Sergius. He prayed for his monastery and for the men in it.

The Mother of God Visits Saint Sergius

Once Saint Sergius stood before an icon of the Mother of God in his cell, saying his usual prayers. He looked at the icon again and again as he prayed. He sang the akathist, the long hymn of praise to her. Then he prayed in his own words.⟦x|Life of Sergius, Old Russian text, BLDR vol. 6, chapter “On the Visit of the Mother of God”; Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 26.⟧

“Most Pure Mother of my Christ, mediator and defender, strong helper of the human race!” He asked her to plead for them, unworthy as they were, and for that place. The man who had once asked God to let him live without a single person near him now prayed for his disciples.⟦x|Life of Sergius, Old Russian text, BLDR vol. 6, “On the Visit of the Mother of God” (our translation).⟧

When he had finished, he sat down to rest a little. His disciple Saint Micah was with him. “Child, be sober and keep watch,” Saint Sergius said, “for a wondrous and fearful visit is about to come to us in this hour.”⟦x|Life of Sergius, Old Russian text, BLDR vol. 6, “On the Visit of the Mother of God” (our translation).⟧

He had hardly spoken when a voice was heard: “Look, the Most Pure One is coming!” Saint Sergius rose and went quickly out of his cell into the porch. A great light fell on him, brighter than the sun. In that light he saw the Mother of God with the apostles Saint Peter and Saint John. They shone in a light that no words can tell. He fell on his face. He could not bear that brightness.⟦x|Life of Sergius, Old Russian text, BLDR vol. 6, “On the Visit of the Mother of God” (our translation).⟧

She touched him with her hands and spoke.

“Do not be afraid, my chosen one. I have come to visit you. Your prayer for your disciples and for your monastery has been heard. Do not grieve any more, because from now on it will have plenty of everything. Not only in your life, but after you go to the Lord, I will not leave your monastery. I will give what it needs without lack, and I will keep it and cover it.”

— The Mother of God to Saint Sergius, in the Life of Sergius

Then she was gone. Saint Sergius was seized with fear and trembling, and for a while he was beside himself. When he came to himself he found Saint Micah lying on the floor as if dead from fear. He lifted him up. Saint Micah threw himself at the abbot’s feet and asked what the vision had been. His spirit had nearly left his body, he said. Saint Sergius’s face glowed with joy, but he could not answer yet. “Wait, child, for my spirit also trembles at the wondrous vision.”⟦x|Life of Sergius, Old Russian text, BLDR vol. 6, “On the Visit of the Mother of God” (our translation); Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 26.⟧

After a while he sent Saint Micah for two more disciples, Isaac and Simon. When they came, he told them everything in order. Together they sang a service of prayer to the Mother of God and gave glory to God. Saint Sergius did not sleep for the rest of that night.⟦x|Life of Sergius, Old Russian text, BLDR vol. 6, “On the Visit of the Mother of God.”⟧

The Repose of Saint Sergius

Six months before his death, Saint Sergius knew that his departure was near. He called the brothers and gave the care of the monastery to his beloved disciple Saint Nikon, “young in body but gray-haired in mind.” He told Saint Nikon to feed the flock with care, because he would answer for many souls. From then on Saint Sergius kept silence.⟦x|Life of Sergius, Old Russian text, BLDR vol. 6, chapter “On the Repose of the Saint” (our translation).⟧

In September he fell ill. He called his brothers once more and taught them for the last time. He told them to hold to the Orthodox faith and to be of one mind. He told them to keep their souls and bodies pure, to love without pretending, and above all to clothe themselves in humility. They were not to forget hospitality, and they were to count the honor and glory of this life as nothing. Then he spoke his last words to them.⟦x|Life of Sergius, Old Russian text, BLDR vol. 6, “On the Repose of the Saint.”⟧

“God is calling me, and I am going away from you. I give you to the Almighty Lord and to his Most Pure Mother. Let her be your refuge and a wall against the snares of the enemy and his barking.”

— Saint Sergius’s last words to his monks, in the Life of Sergius

At the very end he received the Body and Blood of the Lord, while his disciples held up his weak limbs with their hands. Then he raised his hands to heaven and prayed, and with that prayer he gave his soul to the Lord. The Life says he went to “the desired Jesus.” Saint Sergius reposed on 25 September 1392, and the Life counts his years as seventy-eight.⟦x|Life of Sergius, Old Russian text, BLDR vol. 6, “On the Repose of the Saint” (our translation); Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 32.⟧

A great fragrance poured from his body, and the brothers wept. They laid him on a bier and carried him out with psalms and funeral prayers, and they buried him in the monastery he had founded. His face shone like snow. It looked like the face of a living man or of an angel of God, and nothing like the face of the dead.⟦x|Life of Sergius, Old Russian text, BLDR vol. 6, “On the Repose of the Saint” (our translation).⟧

Monks Find Saint Sergius’s Body Whole

Thirty years later, God chose to glorify His servant. A man who lived near the monastery often prayed at Saint Sergius’s grave. One night he fell into a light sleep as he prayed there, and Saint Sergius appeared to him. “Why do you leave me so long in the grave, covered with earth? The water presses on my body!” He told the man to tell the abbot. The man woke afraid and glad at once and went straight to Saint Nikon, and Saint Nikon told the brothers.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 33 (our translation).⟧

Princes gathered, and a council of clergy with them. Among them was Saint Dmitri’s son Prince Yury, whom Saint Sergius himself had baptized. They opened the grave, and the place filled with fragrance. The body was whole and bright, and the robe was whole too. Water stood on both sides of the grave, but it had not touched the body or the robe. They gave glory to God. On 5 July, with psalms and singing, they laid his holy relics in a shrine, where the sick who came with faith received healing. Most accounts give the year as 1422.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 33 and notes 93–94; “Sergii Radonezhskii,” Russian Wikipedia, section on the relics.⟧

Over the grave Saint Nikon built a church of stone, dedicated to the Holy Trinity, and he had it painted by two old masters, Daniil and Andrei. Later sources name this Andrei as Saint Andrei Rublev. The Lavra says that his workshop painted the church between 1425 and 1427. They also painted its chief icon of the Holy Trinity in praise of Saint Sergius.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 33; “Andrei Rublev,” Pravoslavnaya entsiklopediya; “Troitskii sobor Lavry,” Trinity-Sergius Lavra.⟧

By then his Life had been written. Saint Epiphanius the Wise was a monk of the monastery, and he had gathered stories for many years. Some came from Saint Sergius himself. Others came from Stefan, the brother who had helped him carry the first logs. He wrote the Life in 1417 and 1418. In 1449 or 1450 Metropolitan Saint Jona of Moscow named Saint Sergius among the wonderworkers in a letter, and the Russian Church glorified him as a saint around that time. His people had honored him long before.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, preface; “Sergii Radonezhskii,” Russian Wikipedia, sections on the Life and on glorification.⟧

The Monastery Saint Sergius Left Behind

Saint Sergius wrote no books. He left the Church a way of life: the common life, in which no monk owns anything. He also left men who carried it into new monasteries, and their disciples carried it farther still. Russians pray to him as the patron of their land. Students and teachers pray to him too, because of the boy who could not learn his letters until he met the stranger under the oak.⟦x|“Sergii Radonezhskii,” Russian Wikipedia; Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 24; Murzin, “Kak Sergii Radonezhskii stal pokrovitelem uchashchikhsya.”⟧

The Mother of God had promised not to leave his monastery after he was gone. Two hundred years later an army laid siege to it, from 1608 to 1610. One day a chief of the enemy soldiers named Andrei fled with his men to the monastery. They swore an oath and then told what they had seen before one attack. A fast river had flowed between them and the walls, where no river runs. Two gray-haired old men had stood on the walls and shouted at them. The defenders inside knew nothing of this, but the attack failed. The Life tells it as an appearance of Saint Sergius, and says God gave the monastery victory through his prayers.⟦x|Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 63 and notes 134–135.⟧

In April 1919 the Soviet government opened his shrine and made the monastery a museum. In 1941 the shrine was sent far east to Solikamsk, and it came back in 1944.⟦x|“Sergii Radonezhskii,” Russian Wikipedia, section on the relics.⟧

On 20 April 1946 the state gave his holy relics back to the Church. That day was Holy Saturday, the eve of Easter. Today they rest again in the Trinity church that Saint Nikon built over his grave. It stands where he once lived alone, without a single person near him.⟦x|“Sergii Radonezhskii,” Russian Wikipedia, section on the relics; Epiphanius and Pachomius, Life and Miracles, ch. 6, 33.⟧

A Note on the Sources

Saint Epiphanius the Wise wrote the Life of Saint Sergius in 1417 and 1418, and Pachomius the Serb reworked it between 1440 and 1459. Saint Epiphanius’s own text does not survive on its own. This Life follows the old Life in a modern Russian translation and in an Old Russian compilation of the sixteenth century, with Saint Dimitry of Rostov’s retelling of the 1690s. The chapter on the finding of the relics is credited in the edition used here to Simon Azarin in 1646, although its introduction says Pachomius first told that story. The edition’s editors date the finding to 1423; most accounts give 1422. The Life gives no dates except the death. Scholars place the birth in 1314 or in 1322, and the editors doubt the Life’s age of seventy-eight.

The Life names no battle in the war with Mamai and gives no year. Later works link the blessing to the Battle of Kulikovo in 1380. One is the Tale of the Rout of Mamai, which the editors date most likely to the first quarter of the fifteenth century. Another is Saint Dimitry of Rostov’s Life. Only the Tale has the holy water and the two warrior monks, Saint Peresvet and Saint Oslyabya. The historian V. A. Kuchkin has argued that the blessing belongs before the battle on the Vozha River in 1378, and the writer Dmitry Volodikhin defends the traditional view. In the oldest text the blessing says “God helping you”; later texts turn it into a firm promise.

Saint Dimitry of Rostov places Stefan’s outburst after the common life began. He suggests that the brothers resented its strictness and that Stefan wanted power, and those guesses are left out here. The Life does not say how Stefan’s life ended. Saint Dmitri’s death year comes from the editors’ notes, and the peace mission to Ryazan in 1385 from Saint Dimitry of Rostov. Isaac the Silent is counted a saint in the editors’ note; the Isaac who heard of the Mother of God’s visit may be another monk. The Orthodox Encyclopedia identifies the painter Andrei as Saint Andrei Rublev, on the basis of Pachomius’s Life of Saint Nikon. Church tradition holds that the saint’s head was hidden before the 1919 opening and returned in 1946. The Soviet record of that day lists the bones of a whole body. In the sixteenth century Saint Maxim the Greek questioned whether Saint Sergius was a saint, because the monasteries by then held land and wealth.

How This Life Was Researched

This Life rests on eleven sources: two in Old Russian, eight in modern Russian, and one in English. The oldest is the Life by Saint Epiphanius the Wise. It was read in a sixteenth-century Old Russian compilation and in a modern Russian translation of the eighty-eight-chapter version, and more than twenty-five of its chapters were read in full. Beside it stand Saint Dimitry of Rostov’s Life of the 1690s and the Tale of the Rout of Mamai. A study printed in 1922, an encyclopedia, the Lavra’s own pages, and two church magazine articles fill in the rest. The research notes mark nine facts as likely missing from popular English pages, among them the violence at Rostov, the night rounds, the hunger, and the farmer at the fence.
