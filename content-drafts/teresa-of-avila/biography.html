At five o’clock in the afternoon on 3 October 1582, in the convent of the Carmelite nuns at Alba de Tormes, a sick woman of sixty-seven asked for the Sacrament. Four days earlier, on the feast of St. Michael, she had heard Mass and then taken to her bed with a flow of blood, and by now it took two people to turn her over. Her nuns were with her. When the priest came in carrying the Host, she sat up in bed alone and with great ease. Then she began to say the same words over and over: “En fin, Señor, soy hija de la Iglesia.” In the end, Lord, I am a daughter of the Church.

It seems a strange thing for a nun to insist on at the hour of her death. Teresa of Jesus had worn the Carmelite habit for forty-seven years, and she had spent the last twenty of them founding houses for nuns and friars and writing books on prayer. Yet for most of those years churchmen had argued about her. A preacher had compared her to Magdalena de la Cruz, the notorious fraud of Córdoba. The book she wrote about her own soul had been carried to the Inquisition, and it stayed in the Inquisition’s hands for years. The pope’s nuncio in Spain called her a fémina inquieta y andariega, a restless gadabout of a woman.

Three hundred and eighty-eight years later, on 27 September 1970, Pope Paul VI declared Teresa of Ávila a Doctor of the Church. She was the first woman to receive the title, which the Church gives to the few saints whose teaching it holds up to all its members. In his homily that day the pope repeated her words from the deathbed at Alba. Her statue in St. Peter’s Basilica bears the title Mater Spiritualium, mother of those who follow the spiritual way.

How did the restless gadabout become the Church’s teacher of prayer? The answer is the whole of her life, and it begins with a contradiction. At seven, Teresa wanted to see God so much that dying for him seemed a bargain. As a young nun she gave up prayer for more than a year, and for years afterward she struggled to pray at all. Among the saints who had been converted to God she could find none to comfort her, because none of them, once God had called them, had fallen back as she had. What carried her from there to the deathbed at Alba was a discovery she put in the plainest words: prayer, she wrote, is nothing but friendship with the one we know loves us. The story begins in a crowded house in Ávila, with a brother four years older and the lives of the saints.

Two Children on the Adaja Bridge

Teresa de Cepeda y Ahumada was born in Ávila on 28 March 1515. Her father, Alonso Sánchez de Cepeda, had three children by his first wife and nine by his second, Beatriz de Ahumada, who was Teresa’s mother. Out of that crowd of brothers and sisters, her companion in reading was Rodrigo, about four years older than she was. The two of them read the lives of the saints together, and the martyrs caught hold of her. Long afterwards she remembered clearly what she had thought.

“When I read of martyrdom undergone by the Saints for the love of God, it struck me that the vision of God was very cheaply purchased.”

— Teresa, The Life of St. Teresa of Jesus, ch. 1 (tr. David Lewis)

So the two children made a plan. They would go to the land of the Moors, begging their way, and there they would be beheaded for Christ. The only difficulty, as Teresa saw it, was that they had a father and a mother. One day, when she was about seven, she and Rodrigo slipped out of the city by the gate on the Adaja river. They crossed the bridge, and there an uncle met them and took them home. Their mother had already had the house searched, afraid that the children had fallen into the well. Rodrigo, facing the grown-ups, put the blame on his sister.

Martyrdom having failed, they tried being hermits. In the garden of the house they piled up stones to make little hermitages, and the walls kept falling down. What lasted was a word. The thought of eternity held them, and they would say it to each other like a game. “We had a pleasure in repeating frequently, ‘For ever, ever, ever,’” she wrote.

She kept a picture, too, of Christ with the Samaritan woman at the well, and on it were the woman’s words: Domine, da mihi aquam. Lord, give me water. It was a child’s prayer, and years later water would become the image she used for prayer itself.

Her mother, Beatriz, died young, at thirty-three. Teresa was about twelve, by her own reckoning, though her mother’s will is dated November 1528, when she was nearer fourteen. What she did in her grief she told in a few lines.

“When my mother died, I was about twelve years old—a little less. … I went in my affliction to an image of our Lady, and with many tears implored her to be my mother.”

— Teresa, The Life of St. Teresa of Jesus, ch. 1 (tr. David Lewis)

The girl who had set out to die for God was about to discover other books, and other company.

A Girl Who Read Romances

The new books were romances of chivalry, tales of knights and their adventures. A cousin, whom Teresa’s own account calls light, shared her vanities, and there was a friendship that, as she put it, “distressed my father and sister.” Writing about those years long afterwards, she did not soften them. In the story of her life she named her faults one by one: vanity, the romances, and a long habit of trying to keep hold of God and the world at once.

In 1531 her half-sister María de Cepeda married, and Teresa, now sixteen, was sent to board with the Augustinian nuns of Santa María de Gracia. The girl of the romances and the vanities was living among nuns. Some four years later, she chose to become one.

On 2 November 1535, or by some reckonings a year or two earlier, Teresa left her father’s house and took the habit at the Carmelite convent of the Incarnation in Ávila. She was about twenty. She had talked one of her brothers into becoming a friar, and the two of them agreed to set out together very early one morning. Deciding, she found, was the easy part.

“The pain I felt when I left my father’s house was so great, that I do not believe the pain of dying will be greater—for it seemed to me as if every bone in my body were wrenched asunder.”

— Teresa, The Life of St. Teresa of Jesus, ch. 4 (tr. David Lewis)

She had left her father’s house for God. Before two years were out, a grave would be dug for her.

This Daughter Is Not for Burying

After she made her vows, Teresa’s health broke, and a harsh cure only made her worse. Her father brought her to his house in Ávila, and there the doctors gave her up. On the night of 15 August 1537, the feast of the Assumption, she sank into a coma that lasted four days.

Those who watched over her took her for dead more than once. Afterward she found drops of wax on her eyelids. At the Incarnation a grave stood open for a day and a half, waiting for her. One night her brother Lorenzo, keeping watch at her side, fell asleep, and a candle came close to setting the bed on fire. Through all of it her father would not give her up. When they spoke of burying her, he answered, “Esta hija no es para enterrar.” This daughter is not for burying.

She woke. Her body, though, would not obey her. At Easter she was carried back to the Incarnation, and for eight months she lay crippled. One of the nuns, Ana de la Encarnación, repeated under oath what she had heard of those months. Teresa could move nothing but one finger of one hand, and she bore it so cheerfully that those who nursed her took pleasure in her company.

The illness as a whole lasted three years. When at last she could move again, it was on hands and knees, and she wrote about it in one short line: Cuando comencé a andar a gatas, alababa a Dios. When I began to crawl on all fours, I praised God. She credited her cure to St. Joseph. Her first foundation would bear his name.

She was on her feet again, in the convent where her grave had stood open. The years that followed are the ones she judged most harshly.

I Would Not Rise Up

People came to the Incarnation to see Teresa. Báñez, who later became one of her confessors, heard that they came for her buena gracia y donaire, her grace and her wit, and she wept over that all her life. He understood that she had no faults beyond those of other good nuns, and that she was known for her nursing and for more prayer than most. She judged those years differently. She stopped practising mental prayer, the inward prayer in which the soul speaks with God alone, and she stayed away from it for more than a year, thinking it the humbler course. It was the worst temptation of her life, she wrote, and it very nearly ruined her.

The earliest of her letters that survives, probably from January 1546, gives a glimpse of the nun of those years. It is a short note to a man named Alonso Venigrilla, asking him to be kind enough to feed her doves and take good care of them through the cold months. She signed it with her name from the world, Teresa de Ahumada. Nothing in it is wrong. Nothing in it suggests the woman who would one day sign her letters to the King of Spain “Teresa de Jesús, Carmelite.”

Once, while she was with a person whose friendship was doing her no good, she saw Christ standing before her, stern and grave, and understood that he was displeased with her. She wrote that she seemed to see him still. She counted nearly twenty years on that stormy sea, falling and rising. Among the saints who were converted to God, she wrote, she could find none to comfort her, because after God called them they never fell into sin again. She had.

The change came in the oratory, in front of an image of Christ covered with wounds. She described the moment herself.

“It was a representation of Christ most grievously wounded; and so devotional, that the very sight of it, when I saw it, moved me. … I thought my heart was breaking. I threw myself on the ground beside it, my tears flowing plenteously.”

— Teresa, The Life of St. Teresa of Jesus, ch. 9 (tr. David Lewis)

There on the floor, the child who had once thought heaven cheap at the price of her head made a bargain of a different kind. “It seems to me that I said to Him then that I would not rise up till He granted my petition.”

About the same time, the Confessions of St. Augustine came into her hands. “Our Lord seems to have so ordained it,” she wrote, “for I did not seek them myself.” Augustine, too, had put God off for years. When she reached the place where he hears, in a garden, the call that turned his life, she wept.

Later in her Life she put a question to her readers. “Who can look upon our Lord, covered with wounds, and bowed down under persecutions,” she asked, “without accepting, loving, and longing for them?”

Something had opened in her, and it soon showed in ways that frightened the men who heard her confessions.

Lifted Up from the Ground

From about 1556 the visions began. On the feast of St. Peter, 29 June 1557, she felt Christ close beside her. She saw nothing with her eyes, and yet she knew he was there. In 1558 came her first rapture, and in the same year a vision of hell. She tried to say what a rapture was like.

“My soul was carried away, and almost always my head with it,—I had no power over it,—and now and then the whole body as well, so that it was lifted up from the ground.”

— Teresa, The Life of St. Teresa of Jesus, ch. 20 (tr. David Lewis)

It felt, she said, “as if a great force beneath my feet lifted me up,” and she was enough herself to see it happening. The raptures did not always wait until she was alone. Once, in choir, as she was about to receive Communion, she felt herself rising from her knees, and she told the nuns to say nothing about it. Another time, during a sermon on the feast of the house, with great ladies present, she threw herself on the ground; the nuns came and held her down, and it was seen all the same. She begged God to stop giving her favours that others could see. By the time she wrote her Life she thought her prayer had been heard. “I have never been enraptured since,” she wrote, and then added, “It is true that it was not long ago.”

Her nuns had their own stories, and some of them told them under oath many years later. One said that Ana de los Ángeles had seen Teresa lifted from the ground while she prayed, clutching at the floor mats so that no one would notice. Another said that María Bautista had seen her raised from the floor several times, once in the kitchen, rapt, with the pan still in her hand.

Teresa was afraid of being deceived, and she said so. Spiritual men, Báñez remembered her saying, were the easiest to fool, and she preferred to open her soul to learned men who were no great mystics themselves. Báñez could be severe with her. In 1591 he swore that she once pleaded with him: “Por amor de Dios, Padre, que no esté tan sin miedo, que me le hace tomar a mí; de nuevo mire, que no querría engañarle.” For the love of God, Father, do not be so without fear, for you make me afraid. Look again; I would not want to deceive you.

Some confessors went further. They ordered her to make signs of scorn at a vision, and she obeyed. She might be mistaken about a vision, she said, and she could never be mistaken in obeying. Others saw it differently, and in 1557 Francis Borgia approved the spirit in which she prayed. The child who had learned God from books now had another teacher. “His Majesty,” she wrote, “has been to me a veritable Book, in which I saw all truth.”

Then, most probably in 1559, when she was forty-four, came the vision for which she is best remembered.

A Long Spear of Gold

She saw an angel near her, on her left side, in bodily form: Vía un ángel cabe mí hacia el lado izquierdo en forma corporal. She described him with care.

“He was not large, but small of stature, and most beautiful—his face burning, as if he were one of the highest angels. … I saw in his hand a long spear of gold, and at the iron’s point there seemed to be a little fire. He appeared to me to be thrusting it at times into my heart, and to pierce my very entrails; when he drew it out, he seemed to draw them out also, and to leave me all on fire with a great love of God.”

— Teresa, The Life of St. Teresa of Jesus, ch. 29 (tr. David Lewis)

The pain was real, and so was its sweetness: “so surpassing was the sweetness of this excessive pain, that I could not wish to be rid of it.” She knew how it would sound to some readers, and she answered them in advance. “I pray God of His goodness,” she wrote, “to make him experience it who may think that I am lying.”

In her manuscript she named the angel among the cherubim. Báñez, reading it, wrote in the margin that he seemed rather to be a seraph, and the editors who printed the book followed him. She wrote that she saw this algunas veces, more than once, and the nuns of the Incarnation kept a tradition that it happened again years later, when she was their prioress. In Rome, in the church of Santa Maria della Vittoria, Bernini’s marble of the angel and the spear still shows the scene to everyone who walks in.

The next year, in 1560, Teresa made a vow always to do whatever was most perfect. What that meant for a woman who had once given up prayer, she was only beginning to find out, and she was writing it down.

Friendship with One Who Loves

By June 1562 she had finished the first draft of a book about her life and her prayer. It is frank about her failings to the point of discomfort, and it is full of attempts to say what she admitted she could barely say. She could never make herself understood, she complained, except “at the cost of many words.” Writing about the love of God, she confessed, “At one time, I laugh at myself; and at another, I am very much distressed.”

The girl who had kept a picture of the Samaritan woman asking for water now wrote of prayer as the watering of a garden. The work begins, she said, with poor ground: “A beginner must look upon himself as making a garden, wherein our Lord may take His delight, but in a soil unfruitful, and abounding in weeds.”

All her teaching on prayer came back, in the end, to one idea. She put it in a single sentence, in the chapter of her Life where she writes of the prayer she had abandoned, and it is the sentence that answers all those years of struggle.

“Mental prayer, to my mind, is nothing else than dealing as a friend, often alone, with the one who we know loves us.”

— Teresa, Libro de la Vida, ch. 8 (our translation from her Spanish)

In Spanish the heart of it is three words, tratar de amistad: to deal as friends. In the same sentence she added what she had learned about this friend. Nadie le tomó por amigo que no se lo pagase: no one ever took him for a friend without being repaid. Prayer, for Teresa, was time spent with someone who loved her. She had learned that by going without it.

She finished the book in June 1562. Two months later she did something that set the whole city of Ávila against her.

Four Novices and a Hostile City

On 24 August 1562, the feast of St. Bartholomew, a new convent was founded in Ávila under the name of St. Joseph, the saint she believed had given her back the use of her body. Maestro Daza placed the Blessed Sacrament there, and four novices received the habit. A handful of people saw it done: Gonzalo de Aranda, Francisco Salcedo, the priest Julián de Ávila, Juan de Ovalle, and Juana de Ahumada. In this house Teresa and her nuns would live by the primitive Rule of the Carmelites, the Rule of the order in its first and stricter form.

Báñez later swore that the whole city and all its religious orders were against the new house, and that he alone stood up for it before the city council. Teresa herself was ordered back to the Incarnation, and she had to answer for what she had done before the provincial of her order. The house survived the storm. Teresa lived by its Rule for the last twenty years of her life.

For the little community she wrote Constitutions, after consulting the bishop of Ávila, Don Álvaro de Mendoza, and Báñez, Daza, Salcedo and others whose judgment she trusted. They asked a great deal. The nuns were to give two hours a day to mental prayer, eat no meat, and fast from September to Easter. They would own nothing of their own, not a chest or a box or a drawer, “because in small things the devil can loosen the perfection of poverty.” They would sleep on sacks of straw, with no mattress, and she added, as if to head off the objection, that weak and unwell people had tried it and found that it could be done.

Then the same pages turn tender. The sick were to be cared for “con todo amor y regalo y piedad,” with all love, comfort and kindness, as far as their poverty allowed. Sick sisters were to have linen and good beds, “digo colchones,” I mean mattresses. Rather than let the sick go without some kindness, she wrote, let the healthy go without what they need. And at recreation she banned games, for a reason only Teresa would give: el Señor dará gracia a unas para que den recreación a otras. The Lord would give some sisters the grace to entertain the rest, and on that ground, she wrote, all the time was well spent.

Then, in 1567, the general of the whole Carmelite order came to Ávila. She showed him her Constitutions and asked for his approval, and he gave her far more than she asked.

As Many Houses as Hairs

The general was Rubeo. He told Teresa to found tantos monasterios cuantos pelos tenía en la cabeza, as many monasteries as she had hairs on her head. Báñez said he heard it from Rubeo himself. Teresa was fifty-two.

At Medina del Campo the house was close to a ruin. The party reached it in the dead of night, with nothing but a few hangings, and the steward of the lady who owned the house had tapestries to lend. They hung them over the bare, unplastered walls with nails they found by searching along the walls themselves, and by daybreak Mass was said. The nuns watched it through the chinks of a door opposite the altar. By daylight Teresa saw that in places the walls were “level with the ground.” It was like seeing “His Majesty in the street in times so full of peril because of those Lutherans.” Remembering that time, she cried out on the page: “O my God! what a sight is that soul which Thou givest up to suffer!”

Not everyone in Medina wanted her there. At a meeting about the new house, Báñez said, a preacher spoke ill of her and compared her to Magdalena de la Cruz, the notorious fraud of Córdoba. He had it from the Dominican Pedro Fernández, who answered that he thought her a good woman and would leave if that went on.

At Medina, too, in 1567, she met a young friar who was then a student at Salamanca. His name in religion was John of St. Matthias, and he was thinking of becoming a Carthusian. She asked him to wait. She had in mind a house of friars who would live as her nuns lived. For it she had found, in Ribera’s words, dos piedras vivas, two living stones: an older friar, Antonio de Heredia, and the young John. The Church would come to know him as St. John of the Cross.

A house for the friars turned up the next year, when a gentleman, Don Rafael Mejía, offered one at Duruelo. It had, in Ribera’s description, un portal razonable, y una cámara doblada, y una cocinilla: a decent porch, a room with a loft, and a little kitchen. It was enough for two living stones. By then Teresa was living much of her life on the road, and in the spring of 1568 a whole town came out to meet her.

Snow on the Friars’ Habits

On Palm Sunday, 11 April 1568, the people of Malagón came out in procession to meet the new nuns, “in our white mantles, with our veils over our faces.” That spring, on the advice of an inquisitor, she sent the final version of her Life to the priest John of Ávila to be judged.

At Valladolid a nobleman, Don Bernardino de Mendoza, had offered her a house by the river and then died suddenly, without time to confess. In prayer, she wrote, Christ told her that his salvation had been in serious danger, and that his soul would be freed from purgatory when the first Mass was said in that house. She arrived on 10 August, tired, and found the place damp and unhealthy. She had the walls quietly filled up with clay. At the first Mass, on the feast of the Assumption, she saw him beside the priest, “his face was bright and shining, his hands were joined,” and he thanked her.

Meanwhile the friars had begun. John had received the new habit from Teresa. At the end of September 1568 he reached Duruelo, spent the night in prayer, laid the habit on the altar, blessed it, said Mass, and put it on. Antonio de Heredia, who was fifty-eight, came in November. All he had gathered for the house, he told Teresa joyfully, was five hour-glasses to keep the hours, “that amused me much,” she wrote. On the first Sunday of Advent they took new names: Antonio of Jesus, and John of the Cross.

In Lent 1569 Teresa went to see them. She found Fray Antonio sweeping the door of the church, and he looked happy. “What is this, father?” she asked him. “What has become of your dignity?” He answered, “I execrate the time wherein I had any.” The two merchants who had come with her could do nothing but cry, there were so many crosses and so many skulls. The choir was in the garret, so low that the friars stooped to go in. There was a little wooden cross with a paper picture of Christ stuck to it, and Teresa thought it stirred more devotion than the finest carving. The friars prayed in little hermitages made of hay, and she recorded that they would stay there until Prime, “having their habits covered with snow, but they did not know it.”

She asked them not to be so severe with themselves, and they made light of her advice. Still, she wrote that this beginning was “a much greater grace on His part than was that which He gave me in founding the houses of nuns.” Then she went on to Toledo, where she meant to found another house of nuns with three or four ducats in her purse.

God Among the Pots and Pans

She arrived in Toledo on the eve of the Annunciation, 1569, with two nuns. The heirs of a merchant, Martín Ramírez, were pressing conditions on the foundation, and the city council would not give a licence. At last she met the administrator of the diocese in a church near his house and spoke to him, she wrote, “with a certain firmness of purpose,” and he gave way. Now she needed a house. Her helper was a poor student named Andrada, sent by a Franciscan friar, and her companions laughed at him. The next morning he came back with a house, and the keys in his hand.

With her three or four ducats she had bought two pictures on canvas, two straw mattresses and a blanket. They took possession by night, carrying a little bell for the elevation, and cleaned until morning, in great fear. The first Mass was said on 14 May by the Carmelite prior of the city, Juan Gutiérrez de la Magdalena. Eight years later the same man would stand against her.

On that first day, she wrote, “we had not even a withered leaf to fry a sardine with,” until someone left a bundle of firewood in the church. When money began to come in, the sisters came to her. “What is the matter, mother? We do not seem to be poor any longer.” Teresa felt the same. “I was in pain when my poverty was ended,” she wrote.

The following January she wrote to her brother Lorenzo, who had gone to the Indies thirty years before. “I have become such a bargainer and business woman,” she told him, “from having to do with these houses and the Order, that I know how to deal with such matters.” She had not felt so well, she said, for forty years. The houses did so much good that she felt bound to found as many as she could, “although it is extremely tiring work.”

At Salamanca, on the eve of All Souls in 1570, she took possession of a large house with only one companion, Sister Mary of the Blessed Sacrament. The students who had been living there had to be coaxed out, and the house was large and rambling, with many garrets. Sister Mary could not stop thinking that some of the students might still be hiding in them. They shut themselves into a room where straw had been laid for beds, with two blankets lent by the neighbouring nuns, and Mary kept looking about her. The bells were tolling for the dead. “Mother,” she said at last, “I am thinking, if I were to die now, what you would do alone.” Teresa answered, “Sister, when that shall happen I will consider what I shall do; now let me go to sleep.” She wrote afterward that she was still ready to laugh whenever she remembered it.

She could complain to God, too. When the Salamanca nuns moved again in September 1573, it rained so hard that the chapel leaked. Teresa told the Lord that he must either stop sending her to make foundations or help them now. The next day the sun came out. “I found I was very imperfect that day,” she wrote. A nineteenth-century life of Ana de Jesús, one of the nuns there, adds that when the sky cleared she told Teresa, “Your Reverence might very well have asked this favour a little sooner.” Teresa only smiled at her and went to her room.

In between, she was sent back where she began. In 1571 the apostolic visitor, the Dominican Pedro Fernández, made her prioress of the Incarnation, the large convent she had left, against her own wishes and those of many of the nuns. She asked for two friars of her reform as their confessors, and one of them was John of the Cross.

The nuns who travelled with her remembered her good spirits more than the hardships. Ana de San Bartolomé said that Teresa was not fond of sad people, was not sad herself, and did not want her companions to be. “Dios me libre de santos encapotados,” Ana remembered her saying: God deliver me from sullen saints. Another nun, Ana de Jesús, said that what wore Teresa out was personas encapotadas y oraciones estrujadas, gloomy people and prayers squeezed out by force. Her own pen said much the same: “From silly devotions, God deliver us!”

In 1573, at Salamanca, the Jesuit Father Ripalda ordered her to write the story of her foundations. She began on 24 August, eleven years to the day after St. Joseph’s opened in Ávila. In it the woman her sisters had once seen rapt in the kitchen, with a pan in her hand, told her nuns where to look for God.

“Even if you are in the kitchen our Lord moves amidst the pots and pans, helping us both within and without.”

— Teresa, The Book of the Foundations, ch. 5 (tr. David Lewis)

One house in those years brought her no joy at all. It had begun in Toledo, in the spring of 1569, with a message from a princess.

A Book in the Inquisition’s Hands

On the eve of Pentecost, as Teresa sat at table in the new Toledo house, so happy that she could scarcely eat, a servant arrived from Ana de Mendoza, Princess of Eboli. The princess wanted a convent on her estates at Pastrana. Teresa refused to go. Then she prayed, and she heard Christ tell her that she was going for something more than one foundation.

The something more was waiting at Madrid. There she met a hermit from Italy, Mariano Azaro, a doctor of both laws who had been at the Council of Trent, and his companion, a lay brother named Juan de la Miseria. She showed Mariano the primitive Rule of the Carmelites. He said he would think about it that night, and by morning he wanted to live by it. Teresa noted that he was amazed at the change so suddenly worked in him, “especially by a woman.” At Pastrana, that summer, the two hermits became the first friars of a second house of the reform.

Juan de la Miseria could paint, and later, at Seville, he made a portrait of Teresa from life. Gracián, her superior by then, ordered her to sit for it, for her greater mortification. She is said to have laughed at the result: “Fray Juan, God forgive thee! what I have had to suffer at thy hands and after all to paint me blear-eyed and ugly.”

The princess was harder to deal with. She wanted things at Pastrana that, in Teresa’s words, did not become the order, including an Augustinian nun of her choosing who was to be given the Carmelite habit on the spot. Teresa would sooner leave than agree, and the prince, Ruy Gómez, had to calm his wife. The princess had also found out that Teresa had written an account of her soul, and she insisted on reading it. According to the chronicle of the order, she laughed at the book and left it for her servants to read, and they spread what was in it. That, the chronicle says, is what led the Inquisition, the Church’s court for matters of heresy, to demand the book.

In the end it was Báñez, her own confessor, who delivered it. “Yo llevé el libro,” he said later: I took the book, to the Holy Office in Madrid. The story of her soul, with every vision and every fault she had confessed, stayed in the Inquisition’s hands for almost twelve years.

In July 1573 Ruy Gómez died, and the princess, in the first storm of her grief, insisted on becoming a nun at Pastrana at once. The prioress, woken before dawn with the news, said, “The princess a nun? The monastery is lost.” She was right. Before long the princess was receiving her visitors in a nun’s habit and demanding, “What have the friars to do with my monastery?” Teresa’s own account is mild: the devil, or perhaps the Lord, “His Majesty knoweth why,” had sent the princess there as a nun. The following Holy Week the nuns of Pastrana left at midnight, with a receipt from the magistrate for every gift the princess had made them, and travelled to Segovia. “I had the greatest joy in the world,” Teresa wrote, “when I saw them in peace.”

The book was still in Madrid. In February 1575, while founding a house at Veas, she heard from a messenger, as Ana de Jesús remembered it, that officers of the Inquisition were searching for it. And at Veas, that April, a young friar walked into her life.

Carriages Cut with a Saw

His name was Jerome Gracián. He was not yet thirty, a priest and scholar, the son of a royal secretary, and he had taken the habit of the reform at Pastrana. Many people had praised him to her. “He pleased me so much,” she wrote, “that I did not think that they who had spoken so highly of him really knew him at all.” For days afterwards she felt such comfort and happiness that she was astonished at herself. She came to believe that the Lord had given her this young man to stand in his place for her.

Before long he put her obedience to the test. As her superior, he told her to ask God whether her next house should be in Madrid or in Seville. In prayer the answer came: Madrid. Gracián ordered her to Seville. She obeyed, and two or three days later he asked her why she had obeyed him rather than Christ. She told him, as Yepes later recorded it, that she could not be as sure of any revelation as she was of his order. He told her to ask again, and this time the answer was Seville. She had always refused, she admitted, to make any foundation in Andalusia. On the road south she made a vow of obedience to Gracián.

They set out on 18 May 1575, six nuns in covered carriages, with Julián de Ávila, Antonio Gaytán and a barefoot friar. The heat in the carriages was so fierce that getting into them was “like going into Purgatory.” Teresa fell into a violent fever. The nuns threw water on her face, but the water was so warm that it hardly helped. She spent a night in a room like a shed with no window, on a bed that felt “as if made of sharp stones,” and in the morning she got up and went on. Of the nuns who rode with her she wrote that she could have ventured with them into the country of the Turks.

At the Guadalquivir the ferry slipped its rope and drifted off with no oars, while the ferryman’s boy, ten or eleven years old, cried for his father. A nobleman sent help, the boat ran aground on a sandbank, and a man from the castle guided them on in the dark. At Córdoba, on the Monday after Pentecost, they hoped to hear Mass unseen, but the church was crowded for the feast, and the carriages were too wide for the gate of the bridge. Someone had to cut them down with a saw. “It must have taken away my fever altogether,” Teresa wrote. That noon they ate their meal under a bridge.

They reached Seville on 26 May. The friars who had hired them a house had never told the archbishop, and he would not hear of a convent of nuns living without an income. They had one coin left. Teresa was ready, she wrote, to go back to Veas “with very little regret.” Instead she waited, and in time the archbishop came to see her and told her to do what she liked, and as she liked. A year later, when the Blessed Sacrament was carried in procession into the new house, it was he who carried it. At the end, says the chronicle of the order, he knelt and asked her to bless him.

From Seville she wrote to the king. Philip II already knew her letters; two years earlier she had written to promise him the prayers of her nuns. Now she asked him to make the houses of the reform a province of their own, under Gracián. She did not grovel: “since God Himself listens to the poor, and your Majesty stands in His place, I do not think that you will be annoyed with me.”

That August her brother Lorenzo landed at Sanlúcar after thirty-four years in the Indies, where he had been treasurer to the viceroy at Quito. He came home a widower with children. He stood surety for her new house in Seville, and when a notary’s error in the deed got him into trouble, he took refuge in the Carmelite friary. The nuns moved in by night, so afraid of their Franciscan neighbours that, Teresa wrote, they thought every shadow was a friar.

Seville cost her more than any house except St. Joseph’s, and the trials, she said, were mostly inside. “I felt our Lord was in some measure withdrawing His hand,” she wrote, “that I might see that any courage I had before was not mine.” She left in June 1576. On 2 June 1577, Trinity Sunday, in Toledo, she began her greatest book.

A Castle Made of Crystal

Diego de Yepes later told how that book began. On the eve of Trinity Sunday, he said, Teresa had seen in a vision a castle of crystal with seven rooms. When she told him about it, he promised to say nothing until after her death.

She worked on The Interior Castle from Trinity Sunday until 29 November 1577, with a break of five months in the middle; the writing itself took about four weeks. A nun of the Toledo house, Mary of the Nativity, said afterward that she often saw Teresa writing, generally after Communion, her face “resplendent.” Mother Mariana of the Angels reported hearing from her that once, when she brought a message to the cell, Teresa was just beginning a new sheet. A trance seized her and lasted for hours. The nun did not stir. When it ended, the paper, which had been blank, was covered with writing, and Teresa quietly put it away in her box.

The book imagines the soul as a castle with many dwelling places, moradas, and the way to God as a way inward, from room to room. In one of her poems, God says the same thing to the soul: si hallarme quisieres, a Mí buscarme has en ti. Porque tú eres mi aposento, eres mi casa y morada. If you want to find me, you will look for me in yourself, for you are my room, my house and my dwelling. The God she had once set out to find in the land of the Moors had been living inside her all along. The way in, she insisted, was love more than cleverness.

“To make rapid progress and to reach the mansions we wish to enter, it is not so essential to think much as to love much.”

— Teresa, The Interior Castle, Fourth Mansions (tr. Benedictines of Stanbrook)

She was careful to say what she meant by love. “Love does not consist in great sweetness of devotion,” she wrote, “but in a fervent determination to strive to please God in all things.” And in the last and highest dwelling place, she would not let her readers float away from the kitchen. “Both Martha and Mary must entertain our Lord and keep Him as their Guest, nor must they be so inhospitable as to offer Him no food.”

The calm of the book came from no calm in her life. The friars of the older observance, the Calced, had turned against the reform, and the papal nuncio, Felipe Sega, was hostile to her and to her friars. He called her, Yepes recorded, fémina inquieta y andariega, a restless gadabout of a woman. Leaders of the Discalced friars, as the friars of her reform were called, were put in prison or sent away. In Seville a novice gave false evidence against the prioress and against Gracián. Teresa told the king that she was astounded at the tricks of the devil and of the Calced fathers. She would laugh at their inventions, she said, if she did not fear the harm they could do.

In October 1577 the nuns of the Incarnation elected her their prioress again. The provincial was Juan Gutiérrez de la Magdalena, the prior who had said the first Mass in her poor house at Toledo, and he declared the fifty-five nuns who had voted for her excommunicated. Then, on the night of 3 December, he had John of the Cross and his companion, Fray Germán, seized and carried off. The next day Teresa wrote to Philip II. All Ávila, she told him, thought John a saint, “as in my opinion he is,” and he was so weak from all he had suffered that she feared for his life.

“I would rather have seen our fathers in the power of the Moors who might be more merciful.”

— Teresa to King Philip II, Ávila, 4 December 1577 (tr. Benedictines of Stanbrook)

The child who had set out for the land of the Moors, eager to die there, was sixty-two. Of all of this there is no trace in The Interior Castle.

The storm passed slowly. In March 1580, at Toledo, Cardinal Quiroga told her, in Gracián’s hearing, that he had read her Life, the book Báñez had carried to the Holy Office. “No hay en él cosa que no sea muy buena,” the cardinal said: there is nothing in it that is not very good. Teresa had two and a half years left to live, and in June of that year her brother Lorenzo told her that he knew he would die soon.

Figs on the Road to Alba

“I cannot tell how you know that you will die soon,” Teresa wrote to Lorenzo from Segovia on 19 June 1580, “or why you imagine such foolish things.” In the same letter she told him to trust in God, “Who is a faithful Friend and will never fail either your children or yourself.” Two days after the feast of St. John, Lorenzo died of a haemorrhage in less than six hours. He had received Communion two days before.

His daughter Teresita was living among her nuns, and her aunt’s letters to her are some of the gentlest she wrote. When the girl wrote of dryness in her prayer, Teresa told her that such dryness showed the Lord thought her a strong soul. “Do not be unhappy about it: perfection does not consist in feelings but in virtue. Your devotion will return when you least expect it.” She ended, “You know that your Charity must always be the one I love best.”

In November 1581 a letter reached her from Pedro de Castro, a canon who had once said that he had small faith in her revelations. He had read her Life, and it had moved him. Teresa answered that she had said a Te Deum before she even thanked him. Her bad deeds had done him good, she wrote, “since you see that I am not in hell as I have so often deserved to be. That is why I named the book ‘Of the mercies of God.’” And then, at sixty-six: “I have never been so happy as I am to-night.”

Late in 1581 John of the Cross came to Ávila with mounts to take her to Granada, where a new house was planned. She would not go. A foundation at Burgos had been settled, and she sent nuns to Granada in her place. In April 1582 she founded the house at Burgos, the last of her foundations. After St. Joseph’s there had been sixteen more.

By September she was at Valladolid. On the first of the month she wrote two letters to Gracián, who was far away in Seville. Teresita had been bitterly disappointed, she told him, that he had not come back. She scolded him over a muddle about the Salamanca house: “Do not trust to what nuns say; if they want to do a thing they will find a thousand reasons for it.” She would not drag the child about from place to place any longer. “Give kind greetings from me to Fray John of the Cross,” she added, and she ended, “To-day is September 1.”

On 15 September she left Valladolid. To the nuns there she left a last word of advice: “Desire great things; we gain much by this even if we cannot carry out our wishes.” Ana de San Bartolomé, who travelled with her, remembered the prioress, María Bautista, seeing them off with the words, “Go away, both of you, and never come back.” Her last dated letter was begun as they were about to start, “with so much to do that I feel beside myself,” and it carries a postscript: “We have reached Medina.” She was still planning to go on to Ávila, and after that to Salamanca and Madrid.

At Medina the vicar provincial, Fray Antonio de Jesús, was waiting with the coach of the Duchess of Alba, who wanted her at Alba de Tormes. He ordered her to go. Ana de San Bartolomé testified years later that nothing in all her life had been ordered of her that weighed on her more, ill as she was, and with her road leading home to Ávila. But she would obey, she said, as she always had.

She left Medina the next morning with nothing to take for the road. Near Peñaranda she fainted. There was nothing to eat but a few dried figs, and Ana de San Bartolomé was in distress about it. Teresa comforted her: “No tengas pena por mí, hija, que muy buenos son estos higos; muchos pobres no tendrán tanto regalo.” Do not worry for me, daughter. These figs are very good, and many poor people will not have such a treat.

She reached Alba on 20 September, the eve of St. Matthew. “Válame Dios, qué cansada me siento,” she said, “más ha de veinte años que nunca me acosté temprano, sino ahora.” God help me, how tired I am. It is more than twenty years since I went to bed early, until now. Twenty years earlier she had founded St. Joseph’s.

Nine days later, on the feast of St. Michael, she heard Mass and went to bed with a flow of blood. The Duchess of Alba, María Enríquez, fed her with her own hand. Two days before the end Teresa told Ana that she would die of this illness. She had not said so sooner, she explained, to spare her the pain. On 3 October, at five in the afternoon, she asked for the Sacrament.

A Daughter of the Church

Before the priest came with the Host, she spoke to her nuns. Her early biographer Francisco de Ribera, who had known her, set down her words.

“My daughters and my ladies, for the love of God I ask you to keep the Rule and constitutions with great care, and do not look at the bad example this bad nun has given you, and forgive me.”

— Teresa at Alba de Tormes, 3 October 1582, in Francisco de Ribera’s Life (our translation)

Mariana de Jesús, a girl then living in the convent, the daughter of Antonio Gaytán, saw the Host carried in. She swore years later that Teresa, who until then had needed two people to turn her in bed, sat up alone and with great ease. Her face, the girl thought, looked more like the face of a very venerable man than of a woman. “Oh, my Lord and my Spouse,” Teresa said, “the hour I have so longed for has come. It is time now, my God, that we should meet.” She thanked God again and again that he had made her a daughter of the Church: “En fin, Señor, soy hija de la Iglesia.” She had been compared to a fraud, her book had sat in the Inquisition’s hands, and the nuncio had called her a gadabout. At the end she is not recorded as defending her visions or her houses. She said whose daughter she was.

That night she said verses from the psalms over and over. Cor contritum et humiliatum: a contrite and humbled heart. Ne projicias me a facie tua: cast me not away from your face. Cor mundum crea in me, Deus: create a clean heart in me, O God.

Fray Antonio asked her whether she wanted her body taken home to Ávila or buried at Alba. “¿Tengo yo de tener cosa propia?” she answered. “¿Aquí no me darán un poco de tierra?” Must I have anything of my own? Will they not give me a little earth here? “Ande, Madre, vámonos a Avila,” said Ana de San Bartolomé: come, Mother, let us go to Ávila, you are prioress of that house. Teresa lifted her arm with something like annoyance. “Déjense de eso,” she said. Enough of that. It was Sister Juana del Espíritu Santo who found the right words. “You do well, Mother,” she said, “for this house and all of them are yours, and Christ in this life had no house of his own.” Teresa answered, “¡Oh, qué bien me dice, hija!” How well you put it, daughter! Forty-five years before, her father had refused a grave for her. Now she asked for one, and nothing more.

From seven the next morning, 4 October, she lay on her side with a crucifix in her hand, and she stayed so through the day. María de San Francisco, who was in the room, later swore that her face was turned toward the nuns. It was muy bello y encendido, very beautiful and glowing, with such loveliness that she had never seen greater in her life. It looked, she said, like nothing so much as a full moon. The same nun testified that Isabel de la Cruz had a headache and pain in her eyes. She took Teresa’s hands, laid them on her own head, and was freed from it at once.

Between nine and ten that night, Teresa died in the arms of Ana de San Bartolomé. She was sixty-seven years, six months and seven days old. She had been a nun for forty-seven years: twenty-seven at the Incarnation, and twenty under the primitive Rule. A fragrance filled the cell. Even the little dishes in which they had given her some broth, Ana said, gave off a scent. The nuns who sat with the body that night could not say what it smelled of, because it smelled of many things.

Stories gathered around that night. Ana testified that at the moment of death she saw the Lord at the foot of the bed, with saints and angels waiting for Teresa’s soul. After that, she said, she never again felt grief at her loss. Other nuns spoke of a white dove rising from Teresa’s mouth, a star over the church, and visitors in white robes in her cell. At Veas, far to the south, Catalina de Jesús said it had been revealed to her that Teresa had died of an ímpetu de amor, a rush of love. Such accounts belong more to the legend of her death than to its history.

She died on Thursday, 4 October, the feast of St. Francis. That same year, in his reform of the calendar, Pope Gregory XIII had cut ten days out of October 1582. The day after the feast of St. Francis was counted as 15 October. A nun who had known her at Seville dated the death simply when she testified in 1596: it was “the year they gained the ten days.” When they buried her, the town council came, and the Franciscans, and many people, who kissed her feet and her habit as one kisses a saint’s. They laid her between the two grilles of the lower choir. Then, at the request of the foundress of the house, a mason and a carpenter built a wall of stones and lime over the coffin. It was the little earth she had asked for. It did not keep her for long.

A Little Earth at Alba

The nuns of Alba noticed a fragrance coming from the tomb, and they told Gracián, now their provincial. On 4 July 1583, nine months after the burial, he and his companions dug down through the stones and lime. Even the earth smelled sweet. The coffin was broken, rotten and mouldy, and her clothes had rotted. But the body, Mariana de Jesús swore, was tan fresco y entero, que parecía estaba corriendo la sangre, so fresh and whole that the blood seemed to be flowing. Only the tip of the nose was damaged, where a board had given way under the weight of the stones. Before they closed the grave again Gracián cut off her left hand, which went in time to the Carmelites of Lisbon. The next day a painter working on the grille asked the nuns what the smell was.

Ávila wanted her back. Bishop Álvaro de Mendoza had built the main chapel of St. Joseph’s on the condition that his body and hers would lie there side by side. The first chapter of her friars after her death agreed. In November 1585 the provincial came to Alba with Gracián, and the nuns were sworn to silence and sent to Matins. While they were in choir the friars took the body out. Juana del Espíritu Santo held it in her arms a long while, she said, and it was as light and easy to handle as if it were not flesh and bone. An arm was cut off to stay at Alba, as easily, she said, as if he were cutting the breast of a bird. In the choir the nuns smelled the fragrance, left Matins, and found her gone.

Alba fought back. The Duke of Alba and his uncle, the prior of the Order of St. John, went to law and won letters from Pope Sixtus V. On 23 August 1586 the body came back to Alba. However much Ávila pleaded, it never left again. In March 1592 the bishop of Salamanca moved it into a new chest, given by the Duchess of Alba, and found it as before. Pieces of her travelled further than she ever did, to Lisbon, to Rome, and as far as Puebla in Mexico, while her heart is kept in crystal at Alba. When her tomb was opened for study in our own century, two radiographers who examined the body described it as mummified rather than incorrupt in the strict sense. They found a severely curved spine in a woman who had once lain crippled and who spent her last fifteen years on the road.

Gracián’s own road was hard. In February 1592, on falsified evidence, he was expelled from the reform she had trusted him to lead. Pirates captured him and took him to Tunis, where for eighteen months he worked among the Christian slaves. Pope Clement VIII is reported to have said of him, “This man is a Saint.” Through all of it he worked for her cause. He wrote a defence of her teaching against those who wanted to make her out a heretic. Without the two hundred reales he sent to Rome, he wrote, the first summaries of evidence would never have reached the Rota. In 1610, at Antwerp, he wrote out his own long testimony. He died at Brussels on 21 September 1614, five months after she was beatified.

Her books had gone out into the world in 1588, in an edition by Fray Luis de León. More than four hundred witnesses gave sworn statements about her, many of whom had known her. The articles drawn up for the hearings at Salamanca in 1609 and 1610 put forward healings in Lisbon, Seville, Zaragoza, Salamanca, Medina and Granada. One of them told of a judge’s only son at Alba, two years old, whose life had been despaired of. The chaplain laid on his head a cloth stained with Teresa’s blood, and, the article says, the child revived, clutched the cloth, and would let no one take it from him. His father, waiting in the church, heard the boy’s voice and did not dare to look. On 24 April 1614 Pope Paul V beatified her. Paul V died before he could finish the work, and on 12 March 1622, in St. Peter’s, Pope Gregory XV canonized her together with Ignatius of Loyola, Francis Xavier, Isidore the Farmer and Philip Neri. The Catholic Church keeps her feast on 15 October, the day after her death in the new calendar.

On her feast day in 1967 Pope Paul VI said publicly that he meant to make her a Doctor of the Church. No woman had ever held the title, and the question had to be asked whether one could. In March 1968 the pope confirmed that a woman could. On 27 September 1970, during Mass in St. Peter’s, he declared Teresa of Jesus, virgin of Ávila, “Doctor of the Universal Church.” His letter called her a woman of simple customs and without literary learning, who had excelled in words and writings all the same. “We have conferred, or rather We have recognized,” he said in his homily, “the title of Doctor of the Church on Saint Teresa of Jesus.” It gave her no office of teaching in the hierarchy. It recognized what she had already done. And he quoted the words she had repeated at Alba: “Finalmente, sono figlia della Chiesa!” At last, I am a daughter of the Church.

When she died, her breviary was among her things at Alba. In it she had kept nine short lines of verse, and Father Gracián later wrote in the book that it had been hers and signed his name. The lines begin Nada te turbe.

“Let nothing trouble you, let nothing frighten you. All things pass. God does not change. Patience gains everything. Whoever has God lacks nothing. God alone is enough.”

— Teresa, Nada te turbe, kept in her breviary (our translation)

A Note on the Sources

The year Teresa entered the Incarnation is disputed: early documents give 2 November 1535, other biographers 1533 or 1534. Her mother’s will shows she was nearly fourteen when her mother died, not twelve. The scene before the wounded Christ is often dated 1554; our sources do not confirm that year, so it is left out. Diego de Yepes’s Life was not available to us. The deathbed rests on Ribera, writing in 1590, and on sworn statements made from 1591. Her exchange with Ana de San Bartolomé and Juana del Espíritu Santo is in one deposition only. The exhumation dates follow the articles of 1609–10. Gracián recorded that Juan de la Miseria painted her from life; her remark about the portrait comes from the nineteenth-century editor Fuente, as quoted by Zimmerman. The words of Ana de Jesús about the rain come from a nineteenth-century life of her. A letter describing an audience with Philip II at the Escorial in December 1577 was first published in 1919, and since we could not confirm it, the meeting is left out. The Nada te turbe is hers on the word of its modern editor; the longer poem beginning “Eleva el pensamiento” is a later gloss. “God deliver me from sullen saints” comes from Ana de San Bartolomé’s own account of the journeys, and the version with “sour-faced saints” from an English translator’s footnote. The line about more tears shed over answered prayers is credited to her in a 1967 interview with Truman Capote, the earliest source we found, and we did not find it in her writings. “Christ has no body now but yours” is popularly credited to her, but we did not find it in any of her writings.

How This Life Was Researched

This life draws on twenty-four sources in Spanish, English, Italian and Latin. Teresa’s own words come from her Life, Foundations, Interior Castle, Constitutions, poems and about fifteen of her letters. Ribera, her confessor, wrote an early Life of her in 1590. Witnesses from the sworn processes for her beatification speak here, among them Báñez and the nuns who were with her at Alba. Other sources are the eighteen articles on wonders after her death drawn up at Salamanca in 1609–10 and the Rota’s decree of 1614. Gregory XIII’s bull on the calendar, Paul VI’s homily and letter of 1970, and a 2024 news report on her body complete the list. Translations are our own unless a translator is named.
