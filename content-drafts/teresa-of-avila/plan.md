# Plan: St. Teresa of Ávila

## The driving question

At seven Teresa wanted to see God so much that martyrdom seemed to her a cheap price, and she set off with her brother to be beheaded by the Moors. What stood in the way was Teresa herself: charm and vanity, a long illness, and years in which, by her own account, she gave up prayer and could find no saint who had fallen back as she had. When God did take hold of her, in front of an image of the wounded Christ and then in raptures and the angel with the golden spear, a new obstacle rose: the fear of churchmen that she was deceived, the Inquisition's hold on her book, a nuncio's insult. She resolved it in two ways that are one. She learned that prayer is friendship with "the one who we know loves us", found among the pots and pans and in the rooms of an inner castle; and she tested every grace by obedience, ready to be wrong about a vision but never about obeying.

The hook raises the question with her last Communion: a dying nun who sits up and says again and again, "In the end, Lord, I am a daughter of the Church." Why would she need to say it? The death chapter answers the first half, and the last chapter answers both: Paul VI quoted those words in 1970 when he made her the first woman Doctor of the Church, and in her breviary at Alba she had kept nine lines that end "God alone is enough."

## Why famous, and what set her apart

Famous: the Carmelite reformer and mystic of sixteenth-century Spain whose books on prayer made her the first woman Doctor of the Church.

Set apart: she founded houses on the road, laughing, in a century that suspected women who had visions and wrote books. She wrote about the highest states of prayer in the plain voice of a woman who knew kitchens, mules, fevers and lawsuits.

## The hook

Scene at the edge: Alba de Tormes, 3 October 1582, five in the afternoon. The Host enters; the dying woman sits up and says she is a daughter of the Church. Then the contrast: a preacher had compared her to the fraud of Córdoba, her Life had sat in the Inquisition's hands, the nuncio had called her restless and a gadabout. Answered in "A Daughter of the Church" and "A Little Earth at Alba".

## The driving question (unchanged, sharpened)

She wanted God so much that martyrdom looked cheap; she found herself charming, distracted and unable to pray; she learned that prayer is friendship with the one who loves us, and she tested every grace by obedience. The new material lets the middle of the life show this in scenes: the foundress who buys pictures before food, laughs at herself in a haunted house, obeys Gracián against a locution, and at the end obeys "the heaviest order of her life" and goes to Alba instead of home.
