Saint Nicholas: Teachings and Influence

On 6 December the Orthodox Church sings a short hymn to Saint Nicholas of Myra. The hymn is called a troparion, and it says what the Church believes he was.

“The truth of things revealed you to your flock as a rule of faith, a model of meekness, and a teacher of temperance.”

— Troparion for 6 December, tone 4, Orthodox Church in America text

Temperance means self-control. The hymn calls Saint Nicholas a rule of faith and a teacher, and the Church sings those words to him every year. In the fourth century he was bishop of Myra, a city on the south coast of what is now Turkey. He wrote no books. He taught with his life, and the Church learned the lesson from the stories it kept of him.

So what did he teach? The Church answers with his deeds. We read the hymn’s “truth of things” as the truth of his life, and the Church read that life as a lesson. He gave gold in secret. At Myra he took a sword out of an executioner’s hand. When strangers asked his name, he called himself a sinner. From those deeds the Church drew its teaching and put it into hymns, feasts, and customs.

His influence took the same road. It reached the whole Church, East and West, by example, prayer, and popular custom. A Russian family and an Italian port both call on him by name, and so does a child who waits for a present in December.

What Saint Nicholas Left Us

Saint Nicholas left the Church no books. He left it the memory of a bishop who gave in secret, stood between the innocent and the sword, and called himself a sinner. The Church has kept that memory for seventeen centuries, and it kept it first in stories. The oldest is a short Greek story about three army officers called the Praxis de stratelatis. In it three strangers in a prison call on Saint Nicholas by name, and he comes to them.

Sermons in his honor followed. One carries the name of Saint Proclus. Another carries the name of Saint Andrew of Crete and comes from the early eighth century. Then the monk Michael the Archimandrite wrote his first full Life, between 814 and 842. Saint Symeon Metaphrastes retold it in the tenth century, and Nicephorus of Bari wrote the story of his relics in 1087. Beside the Lives stand the hymns of the Eastern services. The Church sings them to him every 6 December and on almost every Thursday of the year.

The Lives also remember him as a defender of the faith. Michael tells how Saint Nicholas stood against the errors of Arius and Sabellius, two teachers the Church had condemned. Later tradition also gives him a speech on the Trinity, the Church’s name for the one God in three Persons. The troparion gathers all this into a few words and calls him “a rule of faith.” The icons of the East show him as a bishop wearing the omophorion, the bishop’s stole, and holding the Gospel book.

A reader who wants the oldest Saint Nicholas should start with the Praxis in Charles W. Jones’s translation. Michael’s Life is translated by John Quinn and the Roger Pearse team. The troparion and kontakion are on the website of the Orthodox Church in America.

What Saint Nicholas Taught About Everyday Life

The Church read lessons from his deeds. In a homily for Thursday, the Greek bishop Hierotheos of Nafpaktos names “faith, meekness, temperance, humility, material poverty” as the “characteristic features of the apostolic life.” The oldest stories show four lessons more plainly.

Give in a Way No One Sees

The first lesson is the three bags of gold. A neighbor was about to put his three daughters in a brothel, and Saint Nicholas threw gold through the man’s window at night. Michael says he did not offer help openly, because that would have shamed the man. When the father caught him on the third night, Saint Nicholas made him swear to tell no one as long as he lived. Saint Symeon Metaphrastes later wrote that of all the deeds of Saint Nicholas, this one was “the most charitable and the best known.”

The lesson is a gift that shames no one, because the giver stays hidden. A reader can do the same for a tired coworker or a neighbor behind on rent. Michael gave the story its first full telling in the ninth century, and one Orthodox history lists it among the chief miracles that tradition has always told of him.

Stand Between the Innocent and the Sword

A second lesson comes from the oldest story. The governor of the province had condemned three innocent men, and he stood to be paid for it. Saint Nicholas ran to the place of execution at Byrra, took the sword out of the executioner’s hand, and threw it down. In the governor’s hall the governor blamed two officials, and in the Praxis Saint Nicholas answers him: “Eudoxius and Simonides did not do this. Silver and gold did.”

A bishop’s place is between the condemned and the blade, even when the condemned are strangers. He also pardoned the governor once the men were cleared, so mercy for the guilty followed justice for the innocent. A second short hymn for 6 December is called the kontakion, and it sings of a bishop who saved the innocent from death:

“…for you fulfilled the Gospel of Christ … by laying down your life for your people, and saving the innocent from death.”

— Kontakion for 6 December, tone 3, Orthodox Church in America text

Call Yourself a Sinner

Three times in the early stories a stranger asks his name, and three times he gives the same answer. Michael tells that he gave it at his election as bishop: “Nicholas, a sinner, a servant of your Holiness, master.” In the Praxis he says the same in dreams to the emperor Constantine and to the prefect Ablabius. The man who saved three lives names himself by his faults.

In all three he gives his name with no word about his deeds. The troparion sings, “Through humility, you attained the heights; and through poverty, riches.” A reader can practice the same thing when praised: say thank you, and pass the credit on.

Feed People, Then Tell Them the Truth

The fourth lesson joins two of Michael’s stories. Grain ships from Alexandria put in at Myra’s harbor during a famine, and the captains said the grain belonged to the state. Saint Nicholas told them to take a hundred measures from each ship, and he would answer for the loss himself in Constantinople. He took the risk so that the city could eat.

The other story is the sailors. Men caught in a storm called his name, and he came to them. Afterward they found him in his church and fell at his feet. Michael writes that he told them how their greed and impure lives called down God’s correction the way sickness calls for a doctor. He quoted Scripture to them too: “Do not do evil things, and evil will not overcome you.” Mercy and correction came from the same bishop. A parent knows this order: feed the child first, then talk about what went wrong. Michael tells the story in his Life of the bishop of Myra, and sailors have called on Saint Nicholas ever since.

How Saint Nicholas Changed the Church in His Lifetime

The oldest story shows how Saint Nicholas changed the people around him. The Praxis tells of a governor rebuked and then pardoned, three men freed, and three officers who gave alms to the poor for many years afterward. Michael adds a tradition that came to him from a monk he knew. The monk said that those who were with Saint Nicholas became better just by seeing him. Michael writes that a heretic who met him as he passed by gave up his false belief at once and accepted the faith.

The Church also remembers him at the Council of Nicaea. In 325 the emperor Constantine gathered the bishops to settle what the Church taught about Christ. A list of those bishops made about the year 515 by Theodore the Lector carries the name of Saint Nicholas. Saint Symeon Metaphrastes wrote of the council in the tenth century: “The admirable Nicholas helped to bring this about as a member of the sacred synod.” The best-known story of the council came last. In it Saint Nicholas loses his temper and strikes the heretic Arius. The St. Nicholas Center says the story is more than five hundred years old. Gerardo Cioffari is a Dominican historian of the saint, and he writes that the slap “was well in tone with Nicholas’ character.”

How Saint Nicholas’s Influence Grew After Death

The Church calls the honor paid to a saint his cult. Saint Nicholas’s cult kept the shape of the oldest story: strangers who called on him by name from far away.

His tomb came first. The Life of Saint Nicholas of Sion was written about 564, and it tells of a visit to the tomb of Saint Nicholas at Myra. Pilgrims on their way to the Holy Land stopped there. After 583 Eustratios of Constantinople cited the story of the three officers. In the sixth century the emperor Justinian built him a church at Blachernae in Constantinople. Before 800 his image was painted on an icon at Mount Sinai and on a church wall in Rome. The sermon credited to Saint Andrew of Crete calls him a “pillar and foundation of the Church,” and one Orthodox history says it was preached at the grave.

In the East he then entered the week itself. Thursday is kept for the apostles and for Saint Nicholas. A speaker in the Russian Church Abroad said in 1995 that his place in the weekly cycle “indicates that great veneration that the Church accords him.” The Octoechos is the book of weekly hymns, and it has hymns to him on almost every Thursday. The Orthodox Church in America says the Russian veneration of him came from Greece “simultaneously with the acceptance of Christianity,” and it describes the result:

“There was no house or temple in the Russian land in which there was not an icon of Saint Nicholas the Wonderworker.”

— Orthodox Church in America, life of the saint for 9 May

Two of the famous wonderworking icons are those of Mozhaisk and Zaraisk.

Then the West took his body. In 1087 sailors from Bari broke open the tomb at Myra and carried the bones to Italy. Two years later Abbot Elias of Bari went to the Synod of Melfi and invited Blessed Pope Urban II to the city. In October 1089 Blessed Urban II laid the relics under the altar of the new crypt, and some men setting out on the First Crusade visited them there. The historian Dawn Marie Hayes writes that “economic developments, church politics and the demands of the First Crusade merged to render Nicholas an ideal patron for the city.” The East split over the event. Within a few years the Church of Kiev took up a feast of the translation on 9 May. The Greek Church did not, because it “regarded the loss of the relics of Saint Nicholas as a sad event.” Centuries later Saint Nikodemos of Mount Athos still wrote a Greek service for the feast.

Churches followed. The Catholic Encyclopedia says his cult in Germany “began already under Otto II, probably because his wife Theophano was a Grecian.” Bishop Reginald of Eichstätt wrote a Life of him in verse before his death in 991. The reviewer Howard Hageman sums up the work of Charles W. Jones this way: from Bari, legends, plays, and church dedications reached nearly every country of western Europe. Greece, Russia, Lorraine, and the city of Moscow took him as patron. So did sailors, merchants, travelers, and children. The storm story lies behind the sailors, and the pilgrims who passed through Myra lie behind the travelers.

Last came the gift-giver. By 1163 the Janskerk in Utrecht kept his day as a high feast. A Swiss writer named Hospinian described the eve of the feast, and Jeremy Seal quotes him. Parents “convey secretly presents … to their little sons and daughters who were taught to believe that they owed them to the kindness of St Nicholas and his train.” A German verse of the sixteenth century by Naogeorgus describes the same hidden gifts. The Reformation ended his public cult in many lands, and the folk gift-giving went on in parts of England, Germany, and the Low Countries. By 1911 the Catholic Encyclopedia said that in the United States and some other countries he “has become identified with Santa Claus.” Roger Pearse puts the link carefully: Santa Claus derives “at some remove” from the medieval legends. Jones held that the New York cult of Saint Nicholas began around 1800 and was “fostered largely by Washington Irving.” Hageman disputed Jones’s claim that the early Dutch settlers brought no such customs.

Saint Nicholas in the Church Today

The Catholic Church keeps his feast on 6 December as an optional memorial, a day that may be kept or left out. Its Roman Martyrology entry for that day names only one of his stories, the three officers saved through Constantine’s dream. The Orthodox Church keeps 6 December and 9 May, sings to him on almost every Thursday, and in Russia keeps 29 July as well. The July feast marks his birth and was brought back in 2004. One Orthodox history ranks him after only the Mother of God and Saint John the Baptist.

Bari has become a meeting place. Dominican friars have kept the basilica since 1951, and an Orthodox chapel has stood in the crypt since 1966. On 1 March 2009 the Russian church of Saint Nicholas in Bari was handed to the Moscow Patriarchate, and Pope Benedict XVI sent a message for the day. He wrote that “the Russian people have never faltered in their love for this great Saint,” and he hoped Bari would remain “a natural bridge to the East.” In May 2017 a fragment of the relics came to Russia. It was to stay in Moscow until 12 July and in St Petersburg until 28 July. Patriarch Kirill spoke as it arrived:

“We’re divided by the calendar reform but still are not divided by the date on which we venerate St Nicholas and the delivery of the relics from Myra in Lycia to Bari.”

— Patriarch Kirill of Moscow, reported 22 May 2017

In 2018 Pope Francis invited the church leaders of the Middle East to Bari to pray for peace. The St. Nicholas Center reports that Bari was chosen because it is a “window into the East.” The bishop who called himself a sinner is the saint both Churches still pray to together.

A Note on the Sources

No text from Saint Nicholas’s own lifetime survives. The Catholic Encyclopedia of 1911 says that almost nothing about him is historically certain except that he was bishop of Myra in the fourth century. Scholars place the Praxis de stratelatis anywhere between about 400 and the sixth century. The German scholar Gustav Anrich questioned whether Saint Proclus or Saint Andrew of Crete wrote the sermons that carry their names. The Dominican historian Gerardo Cioffari allows that the sermon under Saint Proclus’s name may be genuine. Cioffari finds three very old and very different forms of the story of the gold, and he holds its core to be historical. He thinks the story of the sailors fits Saint Nicholas of Sion better. That monk died about two hundred years after the bishop, and by the tenth century the Lives of the two men had been mixed together.

On Nicaea, the oldest and shorter lists of the council’s bishops leave his name out. Anrich took Theodore the Lector’s entry for a later insertion, and Cioffari holds it genuine. No known writing by anyone who sat at the council names him. Michael’s Life does not mention the council, and the first Life to place him there was written before 890. Cioffari counts the Trinity speech and the slap among the “popular developments” that grew up around the council. The speeches in the Lives express the faith of the biographers who told them, and no record of his own words survives. No source names the authors of the hymns or the date of the Thursday arrangement. The essay’s pairing of the kontakion with the Praxis, and of the troparion with the three “sinner” scenes, is our own reading. Jeremy Seal counts the tomb at Myra as almost the only firm evidence that the bishop lived. Several facts about his influence come from modern Orthodox websites and popular sites, above all the Orthodox Church in America and the St. Nicholas Center. The essay names them where it uses them.
